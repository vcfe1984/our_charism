[Exordium] Dear brothers and sisters: As last Sunday, the Church continues to present to us—as she does every year—in this season of Advent the figure of St. John the Baptist, the precursor. Last Sunday we read the Gospel of John’s preaching in Judea, a preaching of conversion in view of the coming of Christ; a conversion that we must know how to listen to in a particular way, we who look forward with longing to the feast of Christmas, so that the coming of the Lord may find us with a well-disposed heart. That is why Advent is a time of conversion, a strong season in the life of the Church, for drawing closer to God, for deepening and examining how our spiritual life is going, how our sacramental life is going.

Last Sunday John bore witness to Christ: repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven has come. This is the one of whom the prophet Isaiah speaks … the one who comes after me is greater than I1. In this Gospel, the one we have just read, it is Christ who bears witness, first to Himself and then to John, saying: truly I say to you, among those born of woman there has not arisen one greater than John the Baptist… 2 He is Elijah who was to come3. Very beautiful is that description by Christ: Whom did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet4. I believe that throughout these 12 verses we have just read—full of content, to be sure—there is a theme we can bring to light which I consider very profitable to consider in the heart of Advent. And it is the demand of the Gospel: what it implies, the work it entails. We are in Advent; it is good to reflect on this.

This Gospel has three parts.

1. Christ’s testimony about Himself

John, the Gospel says, was imprisoned in jail; from that gloom John would go forth to the glory of martyrdom at the episode of Herod and the dance of Salome, the daughter of Herodias. It was from that cellar of Herod’s palace that John closed his mission by handing over the rest of his disciples—he had already sent others—to that Jesus who from Capernaum was going about the lake, the villages and the hills working miracles. The disciples came to Jesus and asked Him: Are you the one who is to come, or must we wait for another?5 And Jesus answered in an apologetic way—the works bear witness to me—and answered with scripture: Go and tell John what you have seen and heard: the blind see and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, the dead are raised and the Good News is proclaimed to the poor6. A forceful answer: those who heard Him could not doubt the truthfulness of His testimony. For Jesus summed up in

that brief answer the wonder-working prophecies of Isaiah—from the songs 29, 35, 61, 13, 26 and above all from song 5, of which two literal phrases are here, and which were applied to the Messiah—as if to say: “this is what was announced; well, this is what you are seeing.”

But we want to speak about the demand of the Gospel. And there is a phrase in Jesus’ answer on which I believe we must reflect—an enigmatic phrase, as one of the holy fathers says—and it is when Jesus, after telling John’s envoys that the miracles testify that I am He, says the following: “go also and tell John not to be scandalized by me,” literally blessed is he who finds no scandal in me7. There is a mystery in the whole Gospel. John was the greatest of those born of woman; John is a lamp that burns and gives light8, says the fourth Gospel; John had faithfully fulfilled his mission and with great humility had announced the precursor; yet John was in prison in a cellar of Herod’s palace, suffering chains and very close to death. Tell John not to be scandalized by me: from this phrase we must learn that whoever wants to follow Jesus in this life must carry his cross, fulfilling his duty. Such a one will be great in the eyes of God. Blessed is he who is not scandalized by me, that is, “blessed are those who do not stumble over me, because on encountering Christ, one either believes, or stumbles and is scandalized.”

2. Christ’s testimony about John the Baptist

Then in the Gospel we see that Christ bears witness to John the Baptist. Those disciples of John had come to the place where Our Lord was working miracles; they had asked Him the question whether He was the one or whether they should wait for another. Jesus had answered and many had heard Him; among them were many who had previously heard John. Christ now bore witness to him, showing the worth of John’s life, and said: What did you go out into the desert to see? A reed shaken by the wind? What did you go out to see, if not a man elegantly dressed? No! Those who dress elegantly are in the palaces of kings. Then what did you go out for? To see a prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet—the precursor. This is the one of whom it is written: behold, I send my messenger before me … Truly I say to you, among those born of woman there has not arisen one greater than John the Baptist9.

These words are also very interesting because in a certain way they mark the spirit of Christianity. The vigor, the firmness and the manliness they contain paint a full-length portrait of the greatness of that man who was John the Baptist. An upright man, who practiced what he preached; who asked for conversion and penance and himself dressed in camel’s hide and ate locusts and wild honey. Christ’s phrase is challenging. Christianity was not made for pampering oneself in this life but for those who demand much of themselves. John was one of them. It also shows the manliness contained in the Christian spirit.

Certainly our religion was not made for the weak (in spirit) but for those who want to demand much of themselves wholeheartedly, for those who want to be rigorous with themselves, who know how to master their passions and practice virtue—which will always be difficult—and who know how to be faithful to God even to martyrdom, of which we have had so many examples in the History of the Church. Whom did you go out to see?10 Not just anyone, but a grown man in every sense, faithful to himself and faithful to God. The greatest among the sons of woman11.

3. The verse that culminates the Gospel

The third and last part I would like to point out are the verses with which the Gospel culminates. Also enigmatic is the phrase when Jesus says: the Kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and only the violent take it by force12. Jesus had just canonized John, that man who had fulfilled with such rigor the mission entrusted to him; He had also said that, nevertheless, even the least in His kingdom is greater than he. Now He also declares that to reach His kingdom it will be necessary to work to attain it. Only those who do violence to themselves attain it13; even more, the text, if we have been attentive, says take it by force, as if giving strength to the expression.

Therefore, the proposition is clear: to reach heaven it is necessary to make an effort, it is necessary to work. This is perceived throughout the whole of Sacred Scripture when man’s life on earth is presented as a warfare, as a combat that must be won—against what enemies? Our struggle is not against flesh and blood but against the spirits of the air14, St. Paul will say. A struggle against the devil, against the world and against the flesh; for to overcome them we will have to do violence to ourselves. Violence to reject temptations, to fulfill the commandments perfectly, to be faithful to what God asks of us each day and at every moment. The kingdom of heaven suffers violence and only the violent take it by force15. We must arrive in heaven weary. The path of this life will not always be one of roses; there will also be thorns, and before them it is necessary to be faithful, it is necessary to do violence to oneself. And it is necessary to work, then, to win that great heaven that Jesus has promised us, where—in the words of St. Paul—eye has not seen nor ear heard what God has prepared for those who love Him16; and—in the words of St. Peter—where there awaits us an eternal weight of incalculable glory17; because—as the canon of the Mass for the dead says—“there God will wipe away the tears from our eyes and there will be no more grief or pain.”

[Peroratio] Dear brothers and sisters, the Gospel account we have heard is deeper, because it ultimately reveals how we must walk through this life. Let us know, then, how to listen to the words of Jesus: not to be scandalized by Him in the face of the difficulties that may arise and will arise along the way; to be faithful like John, a man of integrity, faithful to his mission; and to work doing violence to ourselves, that is, demanding much of ourselves in order to enter Heaven, where Jesus awaits us. As St. Alphonsus Maria de Liguori said: “everything is little, not to say everything is nothing, so long as we attain paradise.” Remember always what the Gospel of St. John says: your sadness will be turned into joy18; “consequently let us lift our eyes to heaven and always console ourselves with the hope of paradise … there the saints await us, the Most Blessed Virgin and Jesus Christ Himself with the crown to make us kings of that heavenly kingdom.”

“Have faith in your priesthood”
St. John Paul II

“In cycles of five hundred years,” said Archbishop Fulton Sheen, “the Church has been attacked in different ways. During the first cycle of five centuries, the Church had to combat the heresies that revolved around the Historical Christ: his Person, his Nature, Intellect and Will. In the second cycle it was the Visible Head of the Church that was denied. In the third cycle it was the Church herself, or the Mystical Body of Christ, which was then divided into two sections. In our days, the attack is secularism, and it is directed against holiness, against sacrifice, against self-denial and against kenosis”1—all of these are intrinsic characteristics of the Catholic priesthood. That is why, he goes on to say, “the new enemy of the Church is ecological; it belongs to the environment in which she lives”2.

It is true that, perhaps never before as now, the Catholic priesthood is being attacked on so many different fronts. And that is why one of the messages St. John Paul II repeated most insistently to priests was precisely: “have faith in your priesthood”3.

May these lines serve as a heartfelt tribute and a great encouragement for all the priests of the Institute who, in the midst of great struggles, strive to be “other Christs”4.

1. The priesthood of Christ

Let us say, in the first place, what the priesthood of Christ is like.

“By reason of the union of the Word with human nature and of capital grace, Jesus Christ is constituted true High and Eternal Priest.

  • No one is so truly a Priest as He: we are in his image.
  • No one is more High Priest than He: we participate in him by derivation.
  • No one is an eternal Priest as He is, who will perpetuate his sacrifice on the altars until the end of the ages and will consummate his priesthood in heaven: You are a priest forever…5, High Priest6, Priest forever7”8.
  • “His priesthood has superabundant power to expiate for all the sins of men: by his wounds we have been healed9”10.
  • “He is the most perfect mediator between God and men: For God is one, and one also is the mediator between God and men…11, He is mediator of a more excellent covenant12”13.

Understood in this way, then, we must say that “Jesus Christ has no successors. No one succeeds Jesus Christ. No one replaces Him. There cannot be a more perfect priesthood. The Apostles and their successors—bishops and priests—do not multiply the one Priesthood of Jesus Christ; only the subjects who participate in the one Priesthood of Jesus Christ are multiplied. […] Jesus Christ succeeds no one, because his priesthood is New. Every priest depends on Jesus Christ, whether as a figure in the Old Testament or as a reality in the New, because his priesthood is Supreme. Jesus Christ is succeeded by no one, because his priesthood is Eternal”14.

Therefore, let us say it once more: the priesthood of Christ is eternal; it is immutable; it is a reality that endures for ages of ages; it is for all peoples, for all humanity, throughout all the centuries, for all races, in all cultures and in all languages15.

In this supreme, eternal, perfect, immutable, superabundant and universal priesthood we participate, we who through the laying on of hands have received holy orders.

Consequently, it is fitting here to repeat the words of the apostle St. Paul to his disciple Timothy: I exhort you to rekindle the gift of God that is in you through the laying on of hands. For God has not given us a spirit of timidity, but of strength and of love and of self-control… Do not be ashamed, then, of the testimony you must give to our Lord… but rather share in my labors for the cause of the Gospel through the power of God16.

2. The friends of Jesus

What is this gift of which the apostle speaks?

“The character of Holy Orders configures one to Christ the Head,” we read in the Directory of Spirituality, “giving the ministerial priest power over the physical Body of Christ and over his Mystical Body. [This] enables him to act ‘in persona Christi’17 […] The members of our Institute who are ministerial priests must return again and again to this ineffable reality, which produced in them an ontological change by likening them to Christ the head, and no lay spirituality must reduce their presbyteral spirituality”18.

Hence the Spiritual Father of our Religious Family, almost every time he addressed priests, exhorted them to ponder the gift of God with these or similar words: “Dear priests… do not cease to look at yourselves interiorly with eyes of faith renewed each day. You are the chosen ones, the friends of Jesus, the servants of his plan of salvation. Dispensers of the mysteries of God on behalf of your communities; enriched with powers that surpass your persons, by virtue of the power received through the laying on of hands19, you are the arms, the voice, the heart of Christ, who continues to save the man of today through your ecclesial ministry”20. “You are my friends… because all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you21. How these words must encourage you in your loneliness in remote villages, which fraternal consolation hardly reaches! How they must encourage you in your anguish before ‘the tragedy of the concrete man of your countryside and cities, threatened daily in his very subsistence, overwhelmed by poverty, hunger, sickness, unemployment’22! How they must comfort your priestly heart before every form of injustice, of abuse by the powerful, of violence that mistreats the weak and the little ones, of loss (in certain sectors) of moral values!”23.

How peaceful it is for our priestly soul “always to consider the unheard-of grace of our priesthood”24 and “to gather, with a renewed awareness of this grace, the strength to continue generously on the path undertaken”25. That more than well-known formula, the priest is another Christ, is not a metaphor, but a marvelous, astonishing and consoling reality26 which likewise denotes tremendous responsibilities.

Indeed, “the character is a continual call to the life of grace and holiness; it is an ontological sanctification of the soul, through the image of Christ that it imprints on it and that marks it with Christ, and which demands a moral sanctification27, in order to avoid a contradiction in being; that is, one's habits and the exercise of the virtues (especially charity) must correspond to the mark on the soul”28. This implies living as if Christ himself lived in us; reflecting him constantly in our behavior, in the sacred ministry, in service and in our dealings with others. In this sense Fr. Alfredo Sáenz wrote: “If we did not strive to reproduce in all our conduct the true face of the eternal Priest, we would represent Christ by our priestly functions, but we would deny him by the rest of our life, which would imply a kind of inner divorce, a sort of spiritual schizophrenia”29.

We must be aware that holy orders has destined us for a specific mission. Therefore each one of us priests has an irreplaceable role and a qualified post to occupy, identifying ourselves with Christ in his threefold mission of sanctifying, teaching and shepherding.

Although we will not now dwell on the different priestly functions, we must say that “the priestly character is a continual disposition and call to priestly and pastoral functions.”

The proclamation of the Gospel, of the whole Gospel, to every kind of Christian and even to non-Christians, so that “all men may discover the attraction of divine beauty and long for it”30, occupies an important place in our life. Indeed, the Proper Law says that it is a vital function of ours31. Souls have a right… they have a right to have the truth ‘proclaimed’ to them. In this ministry of the word of God, “teaching the Word in season and out of season32, whether in preaching, in teaching, in writing or research, in evangelization or in catechesis”33 stands out notably.

We must enlighten consciences, know how to persuade with the truth, and for that very reason we must master the art of correcting one who errs, with charity and at the opportune time, but without fear of “crossing a friend, avoiding some problem or, at times, gaining an advantage through silence or applause”34. Archbishop Fulton Sheen used to say: “If you want people to stay as they are, tell them what they want to hear. If you want to help them improve, then tell them what they ought to know.”

The worthy celebration of the sacraments, the dispensing of the mysteries of God, is equally central in our priestly life. And that is why we must always take eager care to prepare the faithful to receive them, so that the sacraments we administer may bear fruit. It is worth highlighting that if the priest is to act in persona Christi, his principal act is the immolation and oblation of the sacrifice of the Mass35, and in our case, it is the most important thing we have to do36. All other pastoral activities are oriented, above all, to the Eucharist.

Finally, but not for that reason less importantly, we must say that the “spiritual power” that has been given to us37 has been entrusted to us to build up the Church, to lead her like Christ the Good Shepherd, with humble and disinterested dedication, always welcoming, with readiness to take on the different ministries and services that are needed, with a great willingness to collaborate among ourselves. That is, the exercise of authority—which is a service for others—is a duty for the priest. Souls look for leadership from their pastors. Now, if we priests, out of a “spirit of timidity,” shrink from such service, the souls we were supposed to lead end up astray and can turn from sheep into wolves, even devouring their own shepherds. That is why, as John Paul the Great said: “Your authority in the exercise of your functions is bound up with your fidelity to the Church who has entrusted them to you … Your field of action, which is vast, is that of faith and morals, in which you are expected to preach both with a decisive word and with the example of your life”38. Have faith in your priesthood…

“It is beyond doubt that the pastoral function demands the exercise of authority: the pastor is head, guide, teacher; but immediately there comes a second demand, and that is service. Authority, in the mind of Christ, is not for the benefit of the one who exercises it, but for the good of those to whom it is directed. Authority is a duty and, above all, a ministry for others, in order to lead them to eternal life. This pastoral function, if carried out in this spirit, leads to its fullest expression, that is, to the fontal gift of self, to sacrifice; precisely as Jesus said and did: The good shepherd lays down his life for his sheep39. Contained in this vision is a whole set of pastoral qualities: humility, disinterestedness, tenderness; but also a set of requirements of pastoral art, such as the study of pastoral theology, of psychology, of sociology, in order to avoid ‘superficial facility’ in relations with each soul and with communities”40.

With sapiential accuracy the following observation has been made to priests: “The faithful of the parish, or those who take part in the various pastoral activities, see—they observe!—and hear—they listen!—not only when the Word of God is preached, but also when the various liturgical acts are celebrated, in particular Holy Mass; when they are received in the parish office, where they hope to be attended to with cordiality and kindness41; when they see the priest eating or resting, and are edified by his example of sobriety and temperance; when they go to look for him at his house, and rejoice at the simplicity and priestly poverty in which he lives42; when they see him neatly dressed in his own habit, when they speak with him, even about unimportant things, and feel comforted on seeing his supernatural outlook, his delicacy and the human refinement with which he treats even the humblest people, with authentic priestly nobility”43. Do not be ashamed, then, of the testimony you must give to our Lord…

This program implies, among many other qualities, availability, which carries implicitly the idea of sacrificing oneself44. The Incarnate Word said: Unless the grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies it will bear much fruit45. Hence the great ‘charism’ of a priestly, religious and missionary life such as ours is generous love—generous love for Christ and for the members of his Mystical Body. That love is expressed in service and consummated in sacrifice. Hence our readiness for self-giving will be proportional to our love, and when love is perfect, the sacrifice is complete.

3. The frailty of nature

Now, any one of us, whether newly ordained or with years of priestly life behind him, will not have taken long to notice his own human limits and the frailty of nature: tiredness, lack of inventiveness, deficiencies of character, poor health, the great discouragements that come from indifference or criticism or lack of support… To this let us add the ‘unfavorable’ circumstances for the priestly mission in our time—something undeniable—together with the countless apostolic tribulations that follow one after another and seem to take away all our hope.

In fact, who among us has not at some time experienced discouragement? How many of us, because of the tree of difficulties, lose sight of the forest of things that are going well46 and fall into the most bitter pessimism. It is a reality that the Magisterium itself has echoed in speaking of “sad and discouraged evangelizers”47. They are the priests who worked with zeal at the beginning. They brought into play the resources of their apostolate. They preached. They gave alms. They thought they were doing all they ought. But a calumny, a twisted interpretation of their acts, the indifference with which their labors were received, perhaps the hatred they aroused, the advice of those prudent according to the flesh “not to press too hard,” “not to overdo it,” about “the need to hold back a little,” that “whatever one does is useless,” that “everything stays the same,” etc., etc., and above all the purifying experience of the lack of fraternal support, gradually brought down their spirit, slackened their arms, extinguished their enthusiasm, closed and darkened their horizons, and so they ended up doing the ordinary and falling into a sad discouragement.

Within this panorama there is another element that we cannot leave aside—and I would like to emphasize this—and it is the superabundant strength of the divine power of the priesthood of Christ in which we participate. It is a question of faith. And that is why I repeat here once again the words of John Paul II: “Have faith in your priesthood. It is the priesthood of always, because it is a participation in the eternal priesthood of Christ, who is the same yesterday, today and forever48”49. We cannot lose sight of that… Share in my labors for the cause of the Gospel through the power of God, said the apostle.

We cannot let ourselves be carried away by discouragement in the face of an apparent failure in the apostolate. We cannot so easily give up the sublime work of evangelization that has been entrusted to us. We must always preserve in our soul the memory of the Lord's benefits and walk in hope. God does not repent of his gifts. Certainly, on the path of priestly life, as could not be otherwise, we will meet the cross and, evidently, like Christ we will have to suffer the obstacles found in the preaching of the Gospel. But we must lift up our gaze with trust to the One who has called us and dwells in us and acts in us and through us thanks to the Holy Spirit. Rekindle the gift that is in you… Do not be ashamed, then, of the testimony you must give to our Lord…

Not because there are difficulties, not because of ‘a lack of talent,’ not ‘because the times are bad,’ not because ‘nobody listens to me,’ not because ‘I am alone’ (and so many other real or apparent excuses we tend to make) must we stop sowing. “The priestly ministry is called to an activity that knows no weariness,” said John Paul II, “… the grace of God is the principal and incomparable support”50. “Whoever has a missionary spirit feels Christ's ardor for souls… is moved by the impulses of zeal for souls”51. And the Holy Father goes on to say: “Indeed, how can we rest, if all those whom Christ desires to call his own have not yet heard of his love?”52. That is why the Proper Law concludes by quoting the Mystical Doctor: “The soul that walks in love neither tires others nor grows tired”53.

The Incarnate Word has chosen us! Christ, the most perfect Mediator, High and Eternal Priest, who identifies himself, in a certain sense, with our humble persons, has called us and entrusts to our poor lips the divine power of his word. How wholesome it is for the priestly soul to strengthen itself and take root ever more deeply in that sacred reality!

From a human perspective, difficulties, interior tribulations, adverse circumstances, coming up at every step against one's own weakness, want to make us take our hand from the plow and look back54. How easy it becomes in those moments, and how careful we must be not to fall into the temptation of becoming that priest “who sows stingily, doing as little as possible on the excuse of not falling into activism, or because the times are bad, or because the family does not form as it used to, or because of the unhealthy influence of the social communications media…, [and who] only knows how to lament: ‘nothing can be done here’”55.

The response befitting our priesthood is different: it is that of faith in the Incarnate Word and trust in his word, which once again says to us: Cast the nets56.

We cannot let ourselves be carried away by discouragement. We are missionaries and, therefore, men of faith! So, in moments of grief and helplessness, we must lift up our gaze and say to Christ: Lord, I trust in You! And in your Name I will keep on casting the nets; even at the cost of sacrifices and misunderstandings, I will keep on proclaiming without any fear the complete and authentic truth about your Person, about the Church that You founded, about man and about the world that You redeemed with your Blood, without reductionism and without ambiguity57; I will keep on seeking the lost sheep with insatiable thirst; I will not let my arms drop. For God has not given us a spirit of timidity, but of strength and of love and of self-control…

St. Manuel González, with masterly pen, puts these words on the lips of Christ, addressed to us, his priests: “Man of little faith!… You know the history of those words, don't you? […] That scene of my apostle sinking into the waters for lack of faith in Me is repeated so often! And I have had, and still have, to repeat to so many from my Tabernacle, while I give them my hand so that they do not drown: Man of little faith!… I find so little living faith around me that sometimes, many times, my Evangelists could write again that desolating phrase: For not even his brothers believed in Him58.

Could one explain in any other way so much discouragement among my own, so much human or earthly criterion in matters supernatural by their very nature, so much eagerness for earthly reward, earthly comfort, earthly honor, earthly life, so much lamenting and growing sad and despairing as if I were not I and were not where I am, so much counting on man and on his poor and puny power and so little counting on Me, so much love of self and so little love of Me? …”59.

“Priest, who in your visits so often lament the fruitlessness of your labors, the sterility of your sacrifice for your people, the discouragement of your soul before so much desertion…

Priest, who fold your arms or are about to let them fall because you can do nothing. Pastor who do not preach on feast days because few listen to you, who do not teach catechism because few children come, who do not sit in the confessional early because no penitents come, who abandon the works of zeal you undertook and undertake no new ones because so little or nothing is achieved! Have you meditated on my parable of the grain of seed? Have you noticed the miracle that I have worked so many times, and am ready to repeat just as many times, of making great everything small that is sown in my field?

You would like to do great things and you cannot? And it is true: only I do what is great. You do your part. Small things? Those are what I ask of you. My priest, go and sow your little grain!, among many or among few, with success soon, late or never…! The rest… I will do”60. Have faith in your priesthood…

Perhaps now we understand a little better the depth or the scope of that fatherly exhortation of St. John Paul II. We must have faith in the power of Christ who acts in us and through us, in spite of what we are, and making his way through our many weaknesses and miseries, “even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”61. “Without faith, the Missionary cannot be understood, does not exist, and, if he exists, he is not the true Missionary of Jesus Christ. The Missionary who wishes to live and remain at the height of his vocation must nourish himself constantly on the spirit of faith”62, Blessed Paolo Manna told his own. “If faith becomes clouded, zeal also diminishes in intensity; then, even in the strongest, weariness and depression appear, and one can reach the point of despair and the loss of one's vocation. If the Missionary lives by faith, then he is great, he is sublime, he is divine; the Church and souls can hope for everything from him; no labor, no difficulty frightens him, no heroism is beyond his strength; if the spirit of faith in him is languid and weak, he will bustle about nonetheless, he will work, but his toil will profit him little or nothing, and the meager success of his half-hearted works will increase his distrust and depression”63.

In fact, our duty as priests is to bear witness to the faith. A faith manifested, among other things, in the effort to sanctify oneself and to serve Christ in others through works of charity; giving oneself generously to the ministry of the word, seeking the most suitable means so that the Gospel may truly reach every creature64; being channels of grace to communicate divine life through the assiduous celebration of the sacraments; placing oneself totally at God's disposal so that “He may transform us into a permanent offering”65; being, finally, men of prayer, because “our poor breath is fruitful and irresistible only if it is in communication with the wind of Pentecost”66.

With what insistence St. John Paul II urged priests to rekindle “the enthusiasm, the hope, the grace received in priestly ordination. Remember—he told us—that you act so many times in persona Christi, in virtute Spiritu Sancti. An interior strength that surpasses human capacities and that must lead you—with humility, but with great confidence—toward your own interior fullness, become maturity of life in Christ”67. And again: “I earnestly ask you to continue to be enthusiastic in your pastoral tasks”68.

Never must weariness or disillusionment dim the freshness of self-giving that the priestly vocation demands.

4. Trust

Likewise, inevitable difficulties must not diminish our trust. The priestly vocation implies a participation in the sufferings of Christ; therefore, we too must be purified in the crucible of apostolic tribulations. That is why “neither failure nor success should lead us to forget our vocation as servants. We must abandon ourselves to God so that He may give growth to our sowing how and when He wills. Unless the grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit69.

“Trust,” said our Spiritual Father, “is the summit of hope founded on the word of Christ, who has promised his presence and his help”70.

What does this trust mean?

  • It means, above all, having trust in the work of grace that acts in the depths of consciences. What matters is that we be docile and fit instruments of grace, even though the effects of our ascetical effort and of our apostolate are not always visible.
  • It means, moreover, having trust in the power of prayer. However painful our frailty may be, we can at least pray and love. “The history of the Church is stormy in every age, because it is a struggle of good against evil, and the life of the Christian also turns out to be distressing and difficult, because he walks uphill, carrying his cross each day. Interior strength comes from prayer offered in Christ and with Christ and, therefore, with humility and with a spirit of obedience and dedication”71.
  • It means, finally, having trust in our very dignity as priests, as religious. Keeping far away every feeling of vain pride, but also without being frightened by the noise of the threats and pretensions of the world with its errors and pressures.

All of us, to a greater or lesser extent, know the tribulation that comes from being few in a mission place and being overloaded with work; we know—and not by hearsay—what lack of means is; we know the danger of infidelity in a world that rejects us; we have experienced the laceration of unjust criticism and the file of misunderstanding…

Nevertheless: “Sower, sower, every time you hear the doors of the Tabernacle creak as they turn on their hinges, imagine that from in there they are saying to you:

Sower, sow today as well…

Sow in spite of the wicked who yesterday persecuted you openly; in spite of the good who do not understand you, misinterpret you and try to wear you out by dint of grumbling, reticence and outbursts of bitter zeal; in spite of the ailments of your years and your health and the weariness and inconstancy of your assistants and helpers…, in spite of all that and, above all, of your wounded and humiliated self-love, keep on sowing today with the same peace as on the day of your most abundant harvests”72.

“Trust, yes, because He wills it. Trust, because his victory is the pledge of our victory. Trust, because if what you cannot do is much, what you can do is much, very much more”73.

That is the secret of our trust: when we are weak, then we are strong; and the weaker we are, the stronger, because the more we let the presence and power of Christ, High and Eternal Priest, shine forth. It is the all-embracing logic that springs from the cross.

* * * * *

To all the friends of Jesus who today are discouraged, full of fears, complaints and disenchantment:

Take courage! Let us not cease to thank God for the gift of the priesthood he has given us, and let us not be discouraged in the face of obstacles. Certainly the harvest is great and the laborers are few, there is still much to be done for the cause of Christ, and much more than we could ever manage to do; but once we have surrendered our weakness to the strength of God and have entrusted ourselves to the protection of our Most Holy Mother, let us advance with confidence!

We must be aware that we cannot do everything; therefore we must not be discouraged if we do not achieve all that we desire. But on the other hand we must be convinced that we can do more than we ordinarily believe we can, so as to preserve or recover courage, and with it the joy that work always produces when one knows it serves some purpose.

Let us expand our hearts with a solid and encouraging hope founded on the words of Christ himself: behold, I am with you always, until the end of the age74, without worrying along the way about what the world considers success or failure; and thus we will be faithful to the priestly heritage of so many others who before us, being very few, with scant resources and in the midst of great difficulties, “risked their lives so that others might have life and hope”75.

I conclude with this well-known prayer of the holy Bishop Manuel González, who devoted himself so eagerly to the “work of resurrection”—as he called it—of discouraged priests whose hearts had been killed by pessimism:

Immaculate Mother! May we not grow tired!

Our Mother! One request! May we not grow tired!

Yes, even though discouragement at the little fruit or at ingratitude assail us,
even though weakness soften us,
even though the fury of the enemy persecute and calumniate us,
even though money and human help fail us,
even though our works should fall to the ground and we had to begin again…

Beloved Mother!… May we not grow tired!

Firm, determined, encouraged, always smiling,
with the eyes of our face fixed on our neighbor and his needs, to help him,
and with the eyes of our soul fixed on the Heart of Jesus who is in the Tabernacle,
let us take our post, the one God has marked out for each of us.

No turning our face back!
No folding our arms!
No sterile laments!

As long as we have a drop of blood left to shed,
a few coins to share,
a little energy to spend,
a word to say,
a breath of our heart,
a little strength in our hands or in our feet,
that can serve to give glory to Him and to You and to do a little good to our brothers…

My Mother, for the last time! Rather die than grow tired!

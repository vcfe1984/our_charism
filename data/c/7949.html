One of the healthy traditions we have in the Institute is Friday pizzas. Generally on Fridays, after the traditional fast at midday, the pizzas at night are a treat. And Friday nights are also nights of eutrapelia, when there is a somewhat longer recreation, and they are usually great fun.

This, which seems a simple custom, a simple detail of our life, has much to teach us, and it is fitting that we learn it well; it is what Venerable Fulton Sheen (an American archbishop who died only in 1979) calls the “philosophy of pleasure.”

If we are asked whether we want to be happy, we will all say yes. And right away someone may imagine that being happy is something like an endless belly laugh, or eating ice cream forever and ever, or seeing one’s soccer team always winning (that wouldn’t be bad)… however, Fulton Sheen says that there are three laws by which that pleasure associated with happiness is obtained much more easily.

First law

The first of these laws is that, if we intend to have a good time, we cannot plan our life only with “good moments,” fun and relaxed ones. Pleasure is like beauty; it is conditioned by contrast. Fireworks, for example, would not delight us if they were set off at noon; they are appreciated precisely because they are set off at night. If a woman wants to show off her black dress, and she is intelligent, she will not stand in front of a black curtain, but in front of a white one. That is, contrast is necessary to help us see each thing vividly.

Pleasure, fun, delight, according to the same principle, are best enjoyed when they come to us as ‘a little treat,’ a gift, a rest, in contrast with experiences that are less pleasant or that require greater effort: fasting, study and the work of a long week… do you understand? We would make a great mistake if every night were a party night. No one would enjoy pizzas so much if he ate pizzas every night; no one would enjoy the fireworks at Christmas and New Year if we had fireworks every night.

What is funny, the enjoyment of a funny situation, stands out by contrast against something that is not funny. If, for example, a distracted master of ceremonies put a bishop’s miter on sticking out to one side (instead of nice and straight), we would laugh; but it would not strike us as funny at all that all bishops always wear the miter. That is why when a priest, for example, who never dresses up in costume appears in costume, we die laughing; but if he were always in costume, it would no longer surprise us and would not strike us as funny.

That is why we enjoy life more if we include that spiritual requirement of including a certain mortification and self-denial in our lives. That keeps us from getting fed up with fun things; it is as if it preserves the freshness and the joy of living. Self-discipline, interior penance, self-denial make one enjoy much more afterwards any simple thing that Providence offers us.

Second law

The second law is very simple and consists in this: what is pleasant is more pleasant after we have survived a moment of tedium or of pain, and this makes any small pleasure we may have –having reached the top of the mountain, winning a game, passing an exam, seeing people receive the sacraments in the popular mission, etc.– last us a great while. And for that one has to make an effort in whatever we are asked to do and not abandon it at the first difficulty. You will have experienced it on the outings or the excursions: one begins to enjoy the climb once one has gotten past those first moments of feeling exhausted. Or one enjoys and begins to take an interest in the job after having overcome that impulse to drop everything.

Third law

The third law of pleasure is the following; pay attention: pleasure, fun, delight is a by-product (a secondary product), not an objective. Happiness in this world should be like our bridesmaid, not like our bride. Many people in the world –and it can happen to us too– make the grave mistake of aiming directly at attaining pleasure in this life, forgetting that pleasure comes from the fulfillment of a duty or from obedience; because every man must inevitably obey the laws of nature, just as one obeys the law of gravity. We enjoy having an ice cream because in some way we are fulfilling that obligation or that duty imposed by our nature, which is eating. But if we eat more ice cream than our body can handle, there is no longer pleasure; there is a stomachache. That is why seeking pleasure, fun, enjoyment, while forgetting obedience and one’s own duty, is to lose it, not to enjoy it.

I mention this to you because, as you well know, joy is “essential in our spirituality”1, as is also “the practice of penance”2, above all interior penance –our Proper Law clarifies– that is, the penance that comes from the silent sacrifice of fulfilling one’s own duty, from the practice of generous charity, from the penance that daily life together brings with it… do you understand me? That is why Fulton Sheen said that if joy is not common nowadays, it is because there are timid souls who do not have the courage to forget themselves and sacrifice themselves for their neighbor. Do you see the relation between joy and sacrifice? Hence the Directory of Spirituality reminds us that we are called to the practice of apparently opposite virtues

That is why the joy that must characterize us is a spiritual joy, and therefore it can be experienced both in prosperity and in adversity, in pleasant moments as well as in the hardest moments. If it is in prosperity, our joy does not consist in delighting in the goods we enjoy but in those eternal goods we hope for, which are those that the moth cannot eat, nor rust corrupt, nor thieves steal from us. And even in adversity, in trial, in difficulties, this joy can be experienced, knowing that Christ died on the cross as the condition for his Resurrection and recognizing that, as our Proper Law says, “his triumph is our triumph, his victory is our victory” and “there is no reason for greater joy”3, and with that always moving forward, ‘putting a brave face’ –as we say– on every trial. That is the joy that allows us to live with festive joy4.

With the hymn to the Virgin, Cause of our joy, we ask her for the grace to live like her in permanent joy5, to know how to rejoice together6, and not to spare ourselves sacrifices so that we ourselves may be a cause of joy for others, without being discouraged by trials or by crosses, but on the contrary learning to engrave in our hearts the joy of the cross.

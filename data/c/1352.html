“The soul that has attachment to anything…, will not reach the freedom of divine union”2, wrote St. John of the Cross. And what he wrote is exactly what he himself practiced and what he tried so eagerly to instill in his dealings with souls. For that is the Christian vocation of the new man3, a vocation to freedom, since for freedom Christ has set us free4.

Therefore, and as could not be otherwise, the whole aspiration of our spirituality is directed entirely to the conquest of freedom. Precisely because “authentic freedom is identified with holiness, with the New Law, with the Christian faith, with charity; it is the freedom… of the children of God5”6. For that is why the Son of God became incarnate: to free those who through fear of death were subject to bondage all their life7, as the apostle St. Paul says.

Hence, then, what is proper to the members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word is “to live in the freedom of the children of God, who do not let themselves be enslaved: neither under the elements of the world8; nor under the letter that kills9; nor under the spirit of the world10; because we must not submit to the yoke of slavery… (otherwise) Christ would be of no benefit to us”11. Since “slavery can have no part with freedom”12.

Consequently, our Constitutions make manifest the call to the conquest of spiritual poverty or nakedness when they say that in our Institute “the fourth degree of poverty can be practiced more intensely, thus conquering total detachment, not only from material goods—the proper object of the virtue of poverty—but from everything that is not God Himself, which presupposes the perfection of charity and complete and consummate holiness”13, which, as we have said before, is identified with freedom of spirit and is the path to union with God.

Given that we, as members of the Institute, are exhorted to form ourselves according to the magisterium of that great master of the spiritual life who was St. John of the Cross, since his teachings are “a limpid fountain of the Christian sense and of the spirit of the Church”—as Pius XI proclaimed when he honored him with the doctorate—we wish to present in these few lines the example of life and the wise teaching of the Mystical Doctor in order to attain “this noble good of the soul, so necessary for serving God, which is freedom of spirit”14.

1. Freedom of spirit

St. John of the Cross was a religious of the Carmelite Order for 28 years (1563-1591), although he lived only 23 years in the Discalced Carmel (1568-1591). In any case, we must say that even before becoming a religious, but especially during all those years and in not a few circumstances, he showed that the struggle to attain the authentic freedom we have been speaking of is the business of our life.

▪ He decides to become a Carmelite

On finishing his studies at the College of the Society of Jesus, at 21 years of age, Juan de Yepes was “sought after by all, loved and wanted by many religious orders, for his virtue and the signs of holiness he gave”15. And although he was sought after by the Benedictines of San Bartolomé, the Dominicans of San Andrés, the Franciscans of Santa Clara, the Calced Trinitarians of La Concepción, the Premonstratensians of San Saturnino, and the very Jesuits with whom he had studied, he set all those offers aside and chose to enter the Carmelite Order16.

His brother Francisco de Yepes testifies: “He took refuge in what was safest and, determining to enter religion, set his eyes on that of Carmel, and so he went very secretly to the Carmelite convent of Santa Ana in this town (Medina), where he asked for the habit, and the prior and friars gave it to him at once with great joy”17. He showed thereby that self-giving to God—who calls souls by the free initiative of His love18—also demands a free response on the part of the religious; and so, turning a deaf ear to the solicitations of the other religious orders, he wished “to correspond freely and generously with the prompting and grace of the Holy Spirit”19 to God’s call, and entered the Carmelite Order.

That same act of freedom implicit in his entry into the Carmelites he maintained explicitly throughout his whole religious life, as we shall now see.

▪ He leaves the Carmelite Order of the mitigated rule

Thus, for example, after having made profession as Fray Juan de Santo Matía, he was somewhat disillusioned with his Order and was tempted to go to the Carthusians. St. Teresa herself tells it: “I praised Our Lord, and on speaking with him, I was very pleased with him, and learned from him that he too wanted to go to the Carthusians”20. It is she herself who, in that same conversation, convinces him “to wait until the Lord gave them a monastery, and [of] the great good it would be, if he was to better himself, to do so within his own Order, and how much more he would serve the Lord. He gave me his word to do so, provided it did not take long”21. Let us underline here the freedom of spirit of Fray Juan who, although he consents to the foundress’s proposal, sets her the condition “that it not take long”22. For freedom of spirit asks for docility and promptness in carrying out what the Holy Spirit asks; that is why our Proper Law not in vain warns us that “slow calculations are foreign to the grace of the Holy Spirit”23.

And so, on November 28, 1568, the renewal of Carmel officially began in Duruelo (Ávila), Fray Juan adopting from then on the name of Fray Juan de la Cruz—John of the Cross.

▪ Lordship over men

The Directory of Spirituality teaches: “to the extent that the religious gives himself generously to the service of Jesus Christ, the only King who deserves to be served, he acquires an effective, though spiritual, kingship over men, even over those who have power and authority, and even over those who abuse it. For they take upon themselves the burden of their sins and their sorrows, through a humble and serving love that goes as far as the sacrifice of oneself”24.

Accordingly, with the same freedom with which Fray Juan de Santo Matía decided to leave the Carmelite Order of the mitigated rule to begin the discalced reform, with that same freedom he let himself be subjected and submitted to various torments and vexations after measures were taken against the Discalced at the General Chapter of the Order held in Piacenza on May 21, 1575; but he did not draw back from his purpose, that is, he did not give up remaining firmly and faithfully a Discalced Carmelite.

That is how he suffered his first imprisonment: simply for being discalced. For some Calced religious, taking advantage of the climate of hostility against the Discalced, took justice into their own hands, as was the case of the prior of Ávila, Fr. Valdemoro, who took John of the Cross and his companion Fray Francisco de los Apóstoles prisoner and locked them up in the convent of Medina del Campo. They left there by order of the nuncio Ormaneto.

One cannot forget the dignified and noble behavior of St. John of the Cross when, years later, Fr. Diego de Cárdenas, provincial of the Calced Carmelites of Andalusia, along with other religious accompanying him, came to Baeza to make the canonical visitation of the College where John was rector. The provincial was bringing a vexatious brief (something like a decree of reprimand) for the Discalced. The fact is that before the visitation began, the ordinary ecclesiastical court appeared and imprisoned the provincial and his companions. Fray Juan de la Cruz presented himself before the ecclesiastical judge who held them prisoner and begged him to release them. And the judge did so. Once they were out of prison, he took them to his College, prepared a banquet for them and sent them off in great peace. That is how the saints take ‘revenge’! For Cárdenas had forgotten that he no longer had any jurisdiction over the Discalced25.

The second imprisonment that St. John of the Cross suffered took place on the night of December 2 to 3, 1577. “The crime” of which they accused him is unknown to this day. What is certain is that they gave him the treatment prescribed in the Constitutions for “those guilty of the crime of rebellion,” and so he spent a little more than eight months in the conventual prison of Toledo. Some argue that very probably the reason for his imprisonment was that he was judged an accomplice in the rebellion of the nuns of the Incarnation when they elected St. Teresa as prioress against the instruction and command of the provincial who presided over the election. Others think that perhaps it was the fact that he was living outside the convent, in the little house of La Torrecilla26, while both John of the Cross and his companion Germán de San Matías were conventuals of Mancera. In any case, that does not concern us here.

What we do want to point out is the princely spirit with which the holy religious conducted himself during the time he was in captivity, suffering not a few vexations: the daily disciplines, the narrowness and extreme poverty of the cell, the meager diet to which he was subjected, which was meant rather to finish off his health than to keep him alive, the darkness, the intense heat and the penetrating cold, the “torment [of] the lice,” the impossibility of saying Mass, the merciless reprimands to make him abdicate his principles and his discalced way of life, etc. Even so, every day when they brought him down to the refectory to administer the discipline to him, the other friars marveled that John of the Cross remained so solid and unbreakable and that he gave no sign whatsoever that he was going to change. Indeed, as time went by, seeing him ever more invincible in enduring, suffering and keeping silent, the Calced contemptuously called him a “silent file” (lima sorda), because that is what one calls a file that is dulled with lead and makes little or no noise when it files. For those who behave as lords, however reduced they may find themselves, stand out above the rest by their self-possession and by holding honor as dear as life27. However, that ‘ill-treatment’ did not afflict him as much as hearing his jailers say that the Reform of the Order had its days numbered.

Fray Juan never bore a grudge against the prior of that convent who caused him so much suffering. “This is how he behaved in matters concerning his neighbors; and so he suffered the trials God sent him, without admitting human relief or consolation”28, testified Ana de Jesús, a Discalced Carmelite in Segovia. Indeed, he himself, as one who had endured the file and abandonment on the part of superiors, would later write in the second precaution against the devil—which our Constitutions29 make their own: “never look upon the superior with any less regard than upon God, whoever the superior may be, for you hold him in His place. And so watch with great vigilance that you do not look at his temperament, nor his manner, nor his appearance, nor other ways he has of proceeding; for you will do yourself so much harm that you will come to change obedience from divine into human, moving or not moving only because of the ways you see visibly in the superior, and not for the invisible God, whom you serve in him. And your obedience will be vain, or all the more fruitless the more you grow heavy because of the superior’s unfavorable temperament, or light because of his good temperament”30.

▪ Lordship over oneself

In our Proper Law we read that the religious exercises a certain dominion over himself “to the extent that man triumphs over sin, masters the incitements of the flesh, and governs his soul and body. The religious, to the extent that he fully subjects his soul to God, arrives at a state of indifference and detachment toward the things of the world, which does not mean impotence but, on the contrary, a masterful and free will, capable of dedicating itself to things without letting itself be dominated by them”31.

An example of this is the following episode in the life of St. John of the Cross. Knowing the spiritual stature of St. John of the Cross and the lordship he possessed, it is laughable to read that his captors in the conventual prison of Toledo wanted to ‘buy’ him by offering him a good library, a good cell, a priorship and even a gold cross. Faced with such an offer, Fray Juan de la Cruz answers categorically: “He who seeks Christ naked has no need of gold jewels”32. And so later he would write in his second Precaution against the world that “it is necessary […] to abhor every manner of possessing, and let [your soul] have no care about it: not about food, not about clothing nor any other created thing, nor about tomorrow, employing that care in something higher, which is in seeking the kingdom of God, that is, in not failing God; for the rest, as His Majesty says, will be added to us33, since He who takes care of the beasts will not forget you”34.

And with that same lordship which goes hand in hand with forgetting everything that is not God for the love of God, he escaped from prison. For, according to what some witnesses state, “after having commended this to our Lord for some days, [Fray Juan] felt in his soul a great impulse to go, that our Lord would help him.” And so he did.

Certainly the painful experience of prison marked Fray Juan de la Cruz deeply and taught him the science of the cross: “God did well; for, after all, abandonment is a file, and suffering darkness is for great light”35. Because, “What does he know who does not know how to suffer for Christ?”36; “The purest suffering brings and carries with it the purest understanding”37.

That same science of the cross and freedom of spirit is what he transmitted to all the souls with whom he dealt, whether religious or lay people. Indeed, most of his subjects highlight his admirable talks, with which he encouraged them to be perfect and taught them that, in order to climb to perfection, they were to want neither goods of earth nor of heaven, but only to want and seek nothing but to seek and want in all things the glory and honor of God. And they say that those who dealt with him most made the most progress38.

His subjects recount that “he had no things to which he could be attached. And he sought this same thing in his religious, even in things of devotion, insofar as they were curiosity, teaching them to keep their spirit free, and not to feed it on trifles”39. And he gave them this example: “For it is all the same to me whether a bird is tied by a thin thread or a thick one, because, even if it is thin, it will be as tied to it as to the thick one, as long as it does not break it to fly”40. Likewise, in his frequent conversations with the religious he “insisted on faith and nakedness and detachment from creatures. And ‘that in prayer we should not have our spirit measured out and determined to ascend to God by one path, but should leave ourselves in His hands, according to the spirit with which His Majesty might visit us; and that we should rejoice greatly in dryness, wanting nothing but God’s pleasure’”41.

St. John of the Cross was a man with the spirit of a prince, one of those who “have set a style in their conduct”42, and therefore what he taught others he first demanded of himself.

For example: the freedom of spirit of this man is striking with regard to what the rule prescribed concerning prayer in common. In the Constitutions of Fr. Gracián, 1576, it says: “prayer will be in choir, all being together.” In the Constitutions of Alcalá, 1581, the following is prescribed in the clearest and most detailed way: “Prayer shall be held in choir, where, the brothers being together, the Antiphon Veni, Sancte Spiritus and the prayer Deus, qui corda fidelium shall be begun. And then there shall be a little reading from some devout book that may be matter for meditation. And when it is finished, all shall remain praying in silence until the hour is over…”43.

If we had no testimony to the contrary, we would believe that John of the Cross had observed what the rule prescribed to the letter and with exemplary scrupulousness. However, his subjects recount how he would go out into the countryside to pray and had them do the same, and they did so with great devotion and quiet. Thus the aforementioned freedom of spirit of Fray Juan is shown: he safeguards prayer in common, but interprets broadly the place in which it was to be made.

He was also supremely free in his practice of charity. That is why in the first precaution against the world he advises: “toward all persons have equality of love and equality of forgetfulness”44, and he certainly gave proof of it. Thus, for example, when María de la Cruz45—a Discalced Carmelite nun whom the saint held in great esteem—fell ill, without caring about ‘what people would say,’ Fray Juan “divided the poor food they gave him in the convent of Granada and sent some of it” to the monastery of the Discalced nuns, something the sick nun prized and kept as a relic and as a thing come from heaven. Likewise, they recount that a very needy old woman usually came to the door of the convent to ask for food and that, if she ever failed to come, he would send two religious to find out why and to take her whatever she needed46. One more example: when Fray Juan de la Cruz was prior of Granada, contrary to what some might think of as ‘favoritism,’ he did not hesitate to call his brother Francisco de Yepes, an expert in masonry, to give him some work; he paid his travel expenses, and Francisco lived with them for some months in the convent47. For St. John of the Cross was one of those men capable of giving things that no one requires and of abstaining from things that no one forbids48.

Even more admirable is the indifference and detachment the saint displays when, at the General Chapter of the Discalced in Madrid (1591), even though he was the first general definitor (what for us would be vicar general), “they afflicted him greatly”49 because John of the Cross expressed himself about the matters being dealt with in the Chapter50 “in the way he always did, not like some who in the conclave, before Doria—the superior—, applauded his decrees and outside murmured against them”51. For he greatly lamented that “in chapters no one objects, but everything is granted and they go along with it, each one only looking to get his own bite; whereby the common good suffers gravely and the vice of ambition is bred, [and he said that this] had to be denounced, without fraternal correction, as a pernicious vice opposed to the universal good”52. In accordance with this, and rather than betray his conscience, he spoke out with great vehemence, for he had nothing to do with the “law of whim that guides many ignorant men who pride themselves on being clever” (as the Quixote says). This earned him, at that very chapter, being given a patent to go to Mexico and being left without office.

So it was that, in view of preparing his departure for the Indies53, he went off to the solitude of La Peñuela. Thus was fulfilled the fervent petition he had so often made to God: “I only desire that death find me in a remote place, far from all dealings with men, without brothers in the habit to direct; without joys to console me, and tormented by every kind of sorrow and pain. I have wanted God to test me as a servant, after He has tested in labor the tenacity of my character; I have wanted Him to visit me in sickness, as He has tried me in health and strength; I have wanted Him to try me with disgrace, as He has done with the good name I have had before my enemies. Deign, Lord, to crown with martyrdom the head of Your unworthy servant”54 .

▪ Lordship over the world

Our Proper Law says that our religious are to exercise lordship over the world in two ways: one of them, collaborating with the world of creation through work and with the world of redemption through the apostolate55.

St. John of the Cross was tireless in his labor, for he was a man skilled in many fields. His spiritual work of directing souls, his handling of the pen, his gifts for government, his skill in prose and art, did not cancel him out in other areas such as material works, laying floors, putting up partition walls, etc.

Thus, for example, when he was already Definitor General, we can say that he built the ‘generalate house’—even while entangled in a thousand concerns of government. For he himself took charge of drawing up the plans, which included the present church and the square cloister. Pablo de Santa María, an eyewitness, recounts: “Father Fray Juan de la Cruz was very affable and cheerful with everyone, and austere and penitent with himself; and in the harshest part of winter and with much snow he went with nothing on his feet to the quarry where the stone was extracted, to be overseer of the laborers, and with it snowing and hailing on his uncovered bald head, he seemed to set everyone on fire. And many days, being of advanced age, he ate at one in the afternoon having had no breakfast but the Most Blessed Sacrament, so that he seemed more of bronze than of flesh”56. Another of the brothers employed in this work also affirms that when they went to work in the morning they already had their tools prepared (laid out), and it was as if someone had gotten their work ahead for them and they had gained half a day.

But he was also a man lavish in his apostolates: now with the nuns, now with families, now with the children he catechized, now with the gentlemen, now with the young people in the colleges where he was, now with those he met in his path in the street, now with all kinds of clerics who came to consult him.

Likewise, when John of the Cross was at the College of Baeza, in order to be able to attend fully to the people who came to confession, he changed the timetable so that they made the two hours of prayer early in the morning, in order to be free and attend to the people throughout the day.

Another of the ways of exercising lordship over the world, our Proper Law affirms, is “by rejecting the world, whether out of loyalty to the world itself, which must be held as a means and not as an end, or out of loyalty to God, resisting the concupiscences, temptations and sins of the world; being independent in the face of the maxims, mockery and persecutions of the world, depending only on our upright conscience illuminated by faith; ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God, which constitutes the full and total rejection of the evil world”57.

When John of the Cross was prior in Granada, the vicar provincial, Diego de la Trinidad, turned up there and began to pressure and pester Fray Juan, telling him that as superior he had to visit the authorities of the city, principally the president and judges of the Audiencia. And he said this, reproaching him that “if he did not visit the important people of the city, he could not sustain his convent.” Obedience calls. Fray Juan calls Jerónimo de la Cruz and tells him: “Let your reverence take your cloak, for they say we must make visits.” They go down to the city and make their visit to the president of the Audiencia, during which Fray Juan tells him that if they had not visited them before, it was in order to fulfill the obligation of religious recollection. The president answered them that they should go on fulfilling their duty to our Lord in that way, and that in fact they themselves hardly had time to rest, giving them to understand that they were not at fault for not visiting them.

The moral: the saint draws it immediately, for “on leaving, he said to me [says Fray Jerónimo de la Cruz]: ‘Our Lord has made it clear to us that He does not want us to fulfill courtesies with men in the world, since there are so many who take care of that, but to be with His Majesty alone.’ And we went straight back to the convent. And I do not remember seeing him make another courtesy visit.” That is to say, he did it once, as the superior commanded him, but against all human respect he did not do it again.

We have already mentioned the vexations Fray Juan de la Cruz suffered for being faithful to his vocation as a Discalced Carmelite, and how much he had to endure to remain free and independent in the face of the maxims, mockery and persecutions of the world, not caring that because of this he was left without office or that his honor was trampled by the most infamous persecution. But we have not said with what eagerness St. John of the Cross very ordinarily told his religious “that the honors of the world were to be fled like the devil, because they were all full of poison”58 . And at the same time as he told them this, he gave them an example of it, for “he was a great enemy of outward show and of being considered a saint, nor did he like others to make such a demonstration.” Proof of this was when Diego Evangelista59 brought a defamatory process against the saint to discredit him, going as far as the convents to make inquiries—wanting to prove that he had had base habits60—and becoming very angry when he could not get to hear what he wanted. Many religious wrote to Fray Juan de la Cruz urging him to complain to the vicar general and the definitory about those inquiries and to give an account of himself, but the saint ‘answered all with great serenity that he was a worm, and that therefore no one did him any wrong, and that whatever his Creator disposed in his regard, it was for him to embrace it for His love and nothing else’61.

Such was the spiritual nakedness of Fray Juan de la Cruz that this same man who, being at prayer, in answer to Christ’s question: ‘Fray Juan, ask Me for whatever you wish, and I will grant it to you, for this service you have done Me’62, is capable of answering: ‘Lord, what I want You to give me is trials to suffer for You, and that I be despised and held of little account’; is the same who later writes with great nobility of soul: “Love greatly those who contradict you and do not love you, for in this way love is begotten in the breast where there is none, as God does with us, who loves so that we may love Him by means of the love He has for us”63.

“That is to have a princely spirit; [that] is to direct the soul toward great acts… [that] is to be concerned with great things… it is to accomplish great works in every virtue. [That] is to be noble”64.

“For to love is to labor to strip and denude oneself for God of all that is not God”65. In such a way that we do not care at all about the esteem and good opinion of men, about bodily health and strength, about the posts or offices that may be given to us or taken from us, about the prosperous or adverse events that may befall us, about dying young or old66. “Whence it is clearly seen that not only must the soul go unencumbered of everything that comes from the side of creatures, but also of everything that comes from the side of its spirit it must walk dispossessed and annihilated”67. For in order to conquer freedom of spirit, one must work so that the heart is not held back by any human thing68. Since, as the Mystical Doctor writes:

When you dwell upon something,
you cease to cast yourself upon the all.
For to come wholly to the all
you must deny yourself wholly in all.
And when you come to possess it wholly,
you must possess it wanting nothing.
For if you wish to have something in all,
you do not have your treasure purely in God69.

In this sense the saint affirms with complete conviction: “happy the soul that loves, for it holds God as its prisoner, surrendered to all that it may wish”70; for that is why he says: when you come to possess it wholly, you must possess it wanting nothing.

That is why our Constitutions exhort us in a fatherly way “to love all that God wants us to love, without being slaves of our affections for creatures, that is, to love without chaining ourselves, to possess without being held captive, to use without selfish enjoyment, to preserve complete independence, to seek in all and through all the glory of God”71.

2. The teaching of St. John of the Cross on freedom of spirit

We could almost say that the teaching of St. John of the Cross consists in disposing the soul to conquer freedom of spirit and to consolidate it therein.

Without attempting in the least to make an exhaustive analysis of his very abundant doctrine, we give here only a few brushstrokes that we consider of great help so that the heart may not go about in base things72 and may rise to the summit of the Mount of perfection, where the saint says: “Here there is no longer any path, for for the just man there is no law”73.

Let us begin by saying that this great Master of the faith teaches that “the soul will not be able to reach the true freedom of spirit which is attained in its divine union”74 if it submits to other things that are not God and His Divine Will, that is, if it lives attached. And the saint insists on this: “the soul that has attachment to anything…, will not reach the freedom of divine union”75. Simply “because slavery can have no part with freedom, which cannot dwell in a heart subject to desires, because that is the heart of a slave, but in a free heart, because it is the heart of a son”76.

From this it follows:

a) That if there is not this true evangelical renunciation—detachment from creatures—neither is there any setting out on the way toward union with God. For no one will find the new life if he is not willing to walk in the denial of himself. But if one goes by the way of denying and losing one’s own life, following the Incarnate Word, then one will not only find true life, but will enjoy many benefits that are drawn from denial: interior peace, poverty of spirit, and the hundredfold of true possession of God and of all that one has previously denied oneself. It is here that the dark nights, the active and passive purifications through which we must pass77, take on their full meaning, because it is they that prepare us “to remain in the supreme nakedness and freedom of spirit that are required in order to reach divine union”78.

b) Every attachment—whatever it may be—implies slavery. This is contrary to the doctrine of Christ, for, as we have already said: for freedom Christ has set us free79. The condition, then, for arriving at true freedom of spirit is to overcome the slavery to which the appetites subject whoever lets himself be guided by them. “For until the appetites are lulled to sleep by mortification in sensuality, and sensuality itself is at rest from them, so that it wages no war on the spirit, the soul does not go forth into true freedom, to enjoy union with its Beloved”80.

And why do we say that it is slavery? The Mystical Doctor explains it: because “the affection and attachment that the soul has for the creature makes the soul equal to the creature, and the greater the affection, the more it makes it equal and similar, because love creates likeness between the one who loves and what is loved. […] And so, he who loves a creature remains as low as that creature, and in a certain way lower; because love not only makes equal, but even subjects the lover to what he loves. And hence it is that, by the very fact that the soul loves something, it becomes incapable of pure union with God and of transformation in Him”81.

St. John of the Cross gives us examples so as to make us see it even more clearly, saying:

  • “All the beauty of creatures, compared with the infinite beauty of God, is supreme ugliness […]. And so the soul that is attached to the beauty of any creature is supremely ugly before God; and therefore this ugly soul will not be able to be transformed into the beauty that is God.
  • And all the grace and charm of creatures, compared with the grace of God, is supreme gracelessness and supreme insipidity; and therefore the soul that is captivated by the graces and charm of creatures is supremely graceless and insipid in the eyes of God.
  • And all the goodness of the creatures of the world, compared with the infinite goodness of God, can be called wickedness. […] therefore the soul that sets its heart on the goods of the world is supremely wicked before God.
  • And all the wisdom of the world and human skill, compared with the infinite wisdom of God, is pure and supreme ignorance. […] Therefore every soul that would make much of all its knowledge and skill in order to come to be united with the wisdom of God is supremely ignorant before God, and will remain very far from it.
  • And all the lordship and freedom of the world, compared with the freedom and lordship of the spirit of God, is supreme slavery, anguish and captivity. Therefore the soul that falls in love with positions of superiority, or other such offices, and with the liberties of its appetite, is held and treated before God not as a son, but as a base slave and captive, for not having wished to take up His holy doctrine, in which He teaches us that whoever would be greater, let him be lesser, and whoever would be lesser, let him be the greater82.
  • And all the delights and sweetness of the will in all the things of the world, compared with all the delights that God is, are supreme pain, torment and bitterness. And so he who sets his heart on them is held before God as worthy of supreme pain, torment and bitterness.
  • All the riches and glory of all created things, compared with the riches that God is, are supreme poverty and misery. And so the soul that loves and possesses them is supremely poor and miserable before God”83.

With this the saint wants to make us see that “the appetites and gratifications, as with snares, ensnare the soul and hold it back [so] that it does not go forth from itself into the freedom of the love of God”84. But also that the nights, although they darken the spirit and the senses, do so only to give it light on all things; and although the night humbles a person and makes him miserable, it is only to exalt and raise him up; and although it impoverishes him and empties him of all possession and natural affection, it is only so that he may divinely reach out to enjoy and taste all things above and below, being, with freedom of spirit, universal in all85.

c) The conquest of this freedom is a source of lordship over all things. It is worth noting how the saint contrasts this lordship with that of any kingdom or lordship of the world when he says that temporal lordship and temporal freedom “before God are neither kingdom nor freedom”86. That is why the radicalness of the freedom that our Proper Law and St. John of the Cross propose to us is that of interior freedom, since that is the spirit of our Institute: “to live fully the freedom of the children of God”87. “From the negative point of view, it is liberation from sin and from the appetites that enslave man. Positively, it is the capacity for self-mastery and decision in view of one’s own personal fulfillment, which obviously takes place in communion with God, for which man has been created and positively ordered. On it the Mystical Doctor bases freedom of spirit, making it coincide with divine union”88.

Indeed, at the summit of the Mount of perfection, where this union is attained, he writes these significant words, which our Proper Law quotes in the paragraph where it speaks of freedom: “Here there is no longer any path, for for the just man there is no law”89.

That is to say, this freedom is not only “libertas a malo,” but fundamentally “libertas ad bonum.” It is to pass from the slavery of sin to the service of God, from the old man to the new. Although it is defined as the passage from one servitude to another90, paradoxically true freedom lies in it91.

That is why it is not surprising that our Proper Law has included martyrdom when speaking about lordship and inviting us to be “ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God, which constitutes the full and total rejection of the evil world”92; and when it affirms immediately afterward that “the greatest grace that God can grant to our tiny Religious Family is persecution”93. For the soul that knows itself called to be deiform94 must not fear to walk along the narrow path that leads to union with God, for which it is necessary to go “naked of everything, wanting nothing”95, which consists, says St. John of the Cross, in “knowing how to deny oneself in earnest, outwardly and inwardly, giving oneself to suffering for Christ and annihilating oneself in everything, because by exercising oneself in this, all the rest and more than that is accomplished and found in it. And if there is a failing in this exercise, which is the sum and the root of the virtues, all those other ways are beating about the bush and making no progress, even if one has considerations and communications as lofty as the angels’”96. That is why the Constitutions, quoting the Mystic of Fontiveros, urge us vividly to the “nothing, nothing, even to leaving one’s skin and all the rest for Christ”97. Therefore the desire of that martyr who said must grow to giant proportions in our breast: “Let fire, the cross, packs of wild beasts, lacerations, amputations, the wrenching of bones, the severing of limbs, the crushing of my whole body, all the cruel torments of the devil come upon me, provided only that this serves me to attain Christ”98.

That is to say, one must know how to renounce, how to sacrifice whatever must be sacrificed, but not compromise in anything our march toward the conquest of freedom. Rather, one must truly resolve to walk along “the way of perfection in all humility and detachment within and without, not with a childish spirit, but with a robust will”99. Putting all one’s effort into denying oneself, “knowing that this is to follow Christ”100, and “if at any time anyone, whether a superior or anyone else,” warns the Master of the faith, “should urge upon you some lax doctrine, even if he confirms it with miracles, do not believe or embrace it; but rather more penance and more detachment from all things; and do not seek Christ without the Cross”101. That is to be “God’s unconditional ones”102; that is “to prefer nothing to His love”103. And that, and no other, “must be the priestly attitude of every member of our small Religious Family. […] An attitude in which one must live permanently, without diminutions or retractions, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without retreats or slowness. As much in the commitments of the inner life as in high historical undertakings: [because] whoever is not capable of setting fire to his ships when he lands is not capable of building empires”104.

In this way, the path of freedom is the path of “nothing” that leads to the all: “to come to possess everything, desire to possess nothing in anything”105. After renouncing all things, the soul has them all with it and enjoys them with freedom of spirit: “Stripped of every manner of natural affection,” it can now “commune freely, with breadth of spirit, with divine Wisdom, in which, through its purity, it tastes all the savors of all things with a certain eminence of excellence”106. And all this without the sensitive part coming to hinder it107.

The saint expresses this very beautifully in his Canticle, when the Bride (who is the soul) has now attained union with the Divine Bridegroom and hears His voice without anyone disturbing her, in breadth of spirit: “free of all temporal disturbances and changes, and stripped and purged of the imperfections, hardships and mists both of sense and of spirit, she feels a new springtime in freedom and breadth and joy of spirit”108.

That is why this “happy freedom, desired by all”109 of which we have been speaking “is a good that contains within itself all the goods of the world; it is a great lordship”110. To attain it and live it in fullness is our program.

So that what is proper to the members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word is to subject themselves to Christ, to His Divine Will, because in this lies true freedom and the fullness of our happiness; and we want to do so following the path that Christ Himself followed, which was none other than that of the cross. In such a way that, even if they made us lords of all the created things of this world, and even if they threatened us with the loss of bodily life111, and all our works came to nothing, we must have the manliness to make the “formal decision not to make pacts, not to compromise, not to capitulate, not to negotiate, not to concede, nor to strike deals with the spirit of the world”112, since otherwise we would be failing God and would ourselves suffer great slavery and misery.

The watchword is simple: “Show by the depth of your convictions and by the consistency of your actions that Jesus Christ is our contemporary”113.

How? By removing from oneself “totally whatever is repugnant to and does not conform with the divine will”114, until one is “dissolved into nothing”115, until one becomes “fools for Christ”116, which in the language of St. John of the Cross is the fullest “freedom of spirit”117.

They will drag us here and there, they will laugh at us and take us for dull, backward and even feeble-minded; nevertheless, we must know how to bless those who curse us and laugh along with those who laugh at us and mock us, like children who do not understand; and even when we are beaten, persecuted and martyred, we must give thanks to God who found us worthy118.

Understood in this way, to be God’s unconditional ones is to make effective the “offering of greater worth”119 that we make each year in the Spiritual Exercises. “It is to be noble. It is to be men of heart. Men who have something for themselves and for others. They are those born to command. They are those capable of punishing themselves and of punishing. They are those who have set a style in their conduct. They are those who ask not for freedom but for hierarchy. They are those who set themselves laws and keep them… They are those who hold honor as dear as life. Those who, because they possess themselves, can give themselves. They are those who know at every instant the things for which one must die. Those capable of giving things that no one requires and of abstaining from things that no one forbids. They are those who always consider themselves beginners: […] never ceasing to aspire to a holier and more perfect life, never stopping”120.

And for a more complete idea of freedom of spirit, let us conclude with those words that St. John of the Cross places on the lips of God the Father: “behold my Son, subject to me and subjected by my love, and afflicted”121 ; that is the image of true freedom.

For so great and sublime a conquest we are not alone; we count on the extraordinary help of the Most Blessed Virgin Mary, because no one more than she “is totally oriented toward God, and the closer we draw to her, the more intimately she unites us to Him”122. “I personally believe,” said St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, “that no one can attain an intimate union with Our Lord and a perfect fidelity to the Holy Spirit without a very close union with the Most Blessed Virgin and a true dependence on her help”123. That is why he recommended that we renew our consecration to Mary every day. “And the more you repeat it,” he said, “the sooner you will be sanctified and will arrive at union with Jesus Christ. A union that always follows union with Mary, since the spirit of Mary is the spirit of Jesus”124.

May the Incarnate Word, who found His freedom in letting Himself be enclosed in the womb of Most Holy Mary, find us each day “more the slaves” of so very sweet a Mother.

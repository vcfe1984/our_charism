Homily preached on the occasion of the 35th anniversary of the founding of the SSVM

[Exordium] We find ourselves here today, under the shelter of the Immaculate Heart of Mary, to celebrate the beautiful feast of St. Joseph, her most chaste Spouse; an occasion that becomes even more festive as we also celebrate the 35th anniversary of the founding of our sisters, the Servants of the Lord and the Virgin of Matará.

Among the many titles with which we invoke St. Joseph, adoptive father of the Incarnate Word, are the titles of Guardian of the Redeemer, Servant of Christ, Minister of salvation, Support in difficulties, Patron of exiles, Patron of the afflicted, Patron of the poor (which are the litany titles added by Pope Francis in May 2021). Each of them reveals in some way a ‘mission’ of this great Saint. However, there are other titles that reveal to us the heroism of his virtues precisely so that we may imitate him and invoke him when we ourselves are in need of practicing that virtue. Thus, then, we have the litany invocations that honor St. Joseph by calling him: Joseph, most just; Joseph, most chaste; Joseph, most prudent; Joseph, most valiant; Joseph, most faithful.

In this homily −if you will allow me− I would like to invite you to reflect on the title of Joseph, most valiant.

Strong men and women

The Latin text of the litany of St. Joseph uses the expression Joseph fortisimo, which refers to the physical strength that the young spouse of the Virgin Mary must have had. Just imagine: he walked on foot from Israel to Egypt. But the title Joseph fortisimo does not refer only to that, but rather to the spiritual dimension that this title evokes, to the cardinal virtue of fortitude on which courage rests.

The translation of this invocation as Joseph most valiant takes us to the root of the word, which is valens, meaning strong, robust, the one who has no fear. The valiant man is the one who loves the good more than he fears evil and suffering.

When one thinks of a strong man, of a valiant man, perhaps someone may call to mind a soldier who defends his homeland, exposing himself to danger in the midst of a war. And, yes, that soldier is valiant. But it is also true, as the great Patriarch St. Joseph shows us, that in time of peace we also need fortitude.

In fact, a great witness of fortitude or of courage is given to us by someone who risks his own life to save someone who is about to drown, for example, or by a firefighter who does not hesitate to throw himself into the flames to rescue a person, or by the men who lend their help in natural disasters, as we have seen on television during the earthquake in Syria and in Iraq.

Truly, there are many examples of courage that occur even in the everyday reality of our lives. I think, for example, of a woman, mother of an already large family, whom many “advise” to eliminate the new life conceived in her womb and to undergo an “operation” to put an end to motherhood; and she answers firmly: “No!” Of course she realizes all the difficulty that this “no” means: difficulty for her, for her husband, for the whole family; and yet, she answers: “No.” For the baby she carries in her womb is something too great, too “sacred,” to give in to such pressures. Another example might be that of the man who is promised freedom and even a good career and an even better salary, on condition that he renounce his principles or approve something contrary to his honesty toward others. And he too answers “no,” even in spite of threats on the one hand and flattery on the other. That is a valiant man!

According to the doctrine of St. Thomas, the cardinal virtue of fortitude is what lies at the basis of the practice of courage. And this is found in the man:

– who is ready to face dangers;

– who is ready to endure adversities for a just cause, for the truth, for justice, for fidelity to one’s given word, and so on.

That is why courage always requires a certain overcoming of human weakness and, above all, of fear. Because we, by nature, spontaneously fear danger, unpleasantness and suffering, the unknown.

To be truly valiant men and women we must “surpass” ourselves, overcome our own limitations, even running the “risk” of finding ourselves in a difficult, unknown situation; it further implies not being afraid to take the risk of being looked down upon, the risk of exposing oneself to unpleasant consequences, to insults, to demotions, to material losses, and perhaps even to the risk of going to prison or suffering merciless persecutions. To attain such fortitude, such courage, the man/the woman must be sustained by a great love for the truth and for the good to which he or she is devoted. Because, as St. John Paul II rightly said, “the virtue of fortitude walks in step with the capacity to sacrifice oneself”1.

And for those of us who are religious of our beloved Religious Family of the Incarnate Word this is very important and reminds us of that line of the Directory of Spirituality that says: “This is the resounding idea: to sacrifice oneself”2. Which is the same as saying: Be valiant! Be strong!

St. Joseph’s example of courage

Let us look, then, at St. Joseph’s example of courage.

St. Joseph was not only strong or valiant, but he was so to a heroic and superlative degree: most strong, most valiant. Why? Because St. Joseph was not only valiant or strong on certain occasions, which we know from the passages of his life narrated in the Gospels, but also in that part of his life which we do not know he led a heroic life over many years, with determination −whatever it might cost−, with courage for the struggle, with great constancy and endurance to carry the effort through to the end without laying down his arms in the midst of combat3. He was the man of the duc in altum, because he was a man called “to accomplish great works, extraordinary undertakings”4.

For example: St. Joseph always showed himself strong in self-mastery, living a life of singular chastity and showing great fidelity in fulfilling his promises. When he was young, the just Joseph asked Sts. Anne and Joachim, her parents, for the hand of the Virgin Mary in order to marry her, and this alone, nowadays, can be considered an act of courage, because his desire was to love his wife forever and he wished to do so rightly before God and before men. When she was later found to be with child, St. Joseph suffered great uncertainty, but he did not draw back and chose once again to love the Mother of Jesus, and what is more, moved by a true purity of heart, he received the Virgin Mary into his home.

“He had the courage to assume the legal paternity of Jesus, to whom he gave the name revealed to him by the angel: You shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins5”6.

Shortly afterward the spouses had to travel to Bethlehem, with all the insecurity of not having a place to stay in that city, and with the hour of the birth very near. Yet St. Joseph carried his worries alone; he kept them in his heart, wishing not to trouble the Mother.

But, I believe, the episode in which St. Joseph’s courage stands out most was when they had to set out for Egypt. As you know, it was in the middle of the night that Joseph received from the angel the order to leave in order to protect the Child Jesus from Herod. We can imagine Joseph waking the Virgin in the middle of the night to tell her the news… Bl. Anne Catherine Emmerich in her book relates that “then [after giving Mary the warning] Joseph went into a stall (stable) where he kept the donkey and passed into a room where there were various objects, and he arranged everything for a prompt departure. Mary, for her part, got up and dressed for the journey.” “For those holy persons the will of God came first,” St. Catherine continues in her account, and although “they were very moved and grieved, they did not let themselves be carried away by sadness and prepared what was necessary for the journey. Mary took almost nothing of what they had brought from Bethlehem.” Let us imagine how painful was the farewell between the Holy Spouses and the parents of the Virgin, with whom they were living, for they did not know whether they would see each other again.

It was still night when they set off. They say that the Virgin wrapped the Child in her mantle to protect Him from the cold, and they began to walk; it was a long, uncertain road; serpents appeared to them, fierce animals, even robbers… they suffered all kinds of privations, they suffered thirst −in fact at times they had to beg for water−, they suffered great fatigue and they were sad. At times, too, they got lost, because they had to take out-of-the-way paths and avoid villages and public inns, since they were fleeing. Anne Catherine Emmerich says that “Mary was very afflicted and wept.” And yet, led by the hand of St. Joseph most valiant, they pressed on.

Imagine what it was for the Holy Family to settle in a completely unknown country, deprived of all human help. They say that they went to live in the ruins of a building to which Joseph added a wooden structure. There the Virgin sewed and wove while St. Joseph, outside in the courtyard, worked the wood with the few tools he had been able to bring. They endured many humiliations and many hardships among unknown, pagan people, with a culture and traditions entirely foreign to their own.

How much patience and serenity of spirit St. Joseph showed in the face of all the sorrows and adversities that besieged them, seeing himself persecuted by the powerful, unknown to all, without human resources, in a life so full of privations, and with those two beings so dear to him threatened by the bitterness of danger. How many times must St. Joseph have felt that the task was beyond him, as he tried to fulfill as best he could his role as foster father of the Son of the Heavenly Father.

And then he had to return to Nazareth! And make the same journey. And once “back in his own land, he lived a hidden life in the small and unknown village of Nazareth, in Galilee −from where, it was said, no prophet arises and nothing good can come7−, far from Bethlehem, his city of origin, and from Jerusalem, where the temple was”8. Nevertheless, there, in that place insignificant in the eyes of the world, he persevered in the loving care of his most beloved Spouse and of her Son, the Incarnate Word. How many times, in the intimacy of the family, must they have spoken about the Passion, because they recognized that the birth of Emmanuel, the God-with-us prophesied by Isaiah, had been fulfilled in them, and they anticipated in faith the fulfillment of what had been announced about the Suffering Servant, the Man of Sorrows. Nevertheless, we can say that it was from St. Joseph that Jesus learned the courage to face His hour, the hour of the Passion.

How many deaths the heart of Jesus’ adoptive father suffered thinking of the sufferings that awaited the Son entrusted to him. And there, in the intimate familiarity and serenity of the hidden life in Nazareth, the Guardian of the Redeemer renewed daily his yes to the Lord, living only “for Jesus and for Mary”9 and doing everything “for Jesus and for Mary”10.

Let us think of the affliction that must have pierced the heart of St. Joseph when the Child was lost during the pilgrimage to Jerusalem. Let us imagine the grief of this father, so protective of his family.

Yet in all adverse and difficult situations, the carpenter of Nazareth knew how to turn the problem into an opportunity, always putting trust in Providence first. “Joseph was not a man who passively resigned himself. He was a courageous and strong protagonist”11; he had a “creative courage,” as Pope Francis points out.

“This creative courage arises especially when we encounter difficulties. In fact, when we face a problem we can stop and give up, or we can find some way around it. Sometimes difficulties are precisely what bring out resources in each of us that we did not even think we had. […] If at times it seems that God is not helping us, that does not mean He has abandoned us, but that He trusts in us, in what we can plan, invent, find”12.

[Peroratio] From St. Joseph, then, we learn that the truly valiant man does not present himself presumptuously; he does not raise his voice or shout in order to humiliate others; he does not have a haughty look nor present himself with the arrogant attitude of a ‘winner,’ but knows how to master his temper; he does not exalt himself, but rather avoids imposing himself on others. St. John of the Cross said that “to go about losing, and to let everyone get the better of us, belongs to valiant spirits, to generous hearts”13. The truly valiant one, who knows how to put out into the deep, also knows how to recognize in humility that his strength comes from the grace of God and, following the example of St. Joseph, “constantly keeps his soul ready for whatever God may dispose”14, without reserve, without conditions, only to please Jesus and the Most Holy Virgin, even when this means a continual dying to self so that God may be loved and served.

St. Joseph was prudent in making his decisions, but he never retraced his steps, because wherever he discerned the will of God, there he went without delay and with confidence, keeping everything in his most chaste heart. It was the immense love he kept there for the Incarnate Word and His Most Holy Mother that gave him the strength to bear courageously the “most difficult situations”15 and to live in “the most adverse conditions.”

In short, St. Joseph most valiant teaches us that without love one cannot be truly valiant. Let us ask today that his powerful intercession grant us a stronger love, so that we may learn to be, like him, most valiant.

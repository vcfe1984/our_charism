Why do we read and study Cornelio Fabro?

St. John Paul II wrote: “Philosophy is like the mirror which reflects the culture of a people”1. Therefore the study of philosophy is for us –dedicated specifically to the evangelization of culture2– of singular and eminent importance. For, as our Constitutions rightly point out, it is “philosophy that leads us to a deeper knowledge and interpretation of the person, of his freedom, of his relationships with the world and with God”3 “in the face of an altogether particular cultural situation, which exalts subjectivism as the criterion and measure of truth”4. Thus it necessarily falls to us, religious and missionaries of the Incarnate Word, to have a “certainty of the truth,” which is given only by a sound philosophy grounded in the objective reality of things5.

That is why our intellectual and philosophical formation is clearly and intentionally Thomist, as the Church commands6 and as the most worthy task of “inculturating the Gospel” requires7. For it is “from the philosophy of being that man can find his true foundation, which is being; he can find his ultimate end, which is Being by Essence; and he can also find his depth, which is freedom. And in this way discover the true cultural values”8.

In this sense, the Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen wrote: “It is only accidental that St. Thomas belongs to the thirteenth century. His thought is no more confined to that period of human history than the multiplication tables are confined to the past. Truth is eternal, even though its verbal expression is localized in time and space. If need creates a reality, then St. Thomas was never more real than he is today. If reality makes modernity, then St. Thomas is the prince of modern philosophers. If a progressive universe is a contemporary ideal, then the philosophy of St. Thomas is its greatest realization. Modern idealism needs the complement of his realism; empiricism needs his transcendental principles; philosophical biologism his metaphysics; sociological morality his ethics; sentimentalism his theory of the intellect; and the world needs the God whom he knew, loved and adored”9.

Therefore, there is no room for us in a vulgarized, textbook Thomism, as happens with those who know ‘something’ of it, generally superficial and skin-deep, and almost always steeped in the formalist or essentialist scholasticism that exchanged esse for existentia, and from which sprang formalist or essentialist ‘spiritualities’ and ‘pastoral approaches,’ without bite and without biting into reality10. Rather, we strive to acquire an authentically metaphysical intelligence, one that enables our religious to know reality, so that they may be capable of making precise diagnoses and applying the opportune remedies. That is to say, our task is to acquire a metaphysics ‘with bite,’ one that bites into reality, one that is functional for the evangelization and renewal of the world. That metaphysics can only be that of the actus essendi (esse ut actus), that of being, which in the words of Blessed Paul VI is “the natural metaphysics of the human intellect”11.

That is why our Proper Law emphatically invites us to “go beyond the textbook method through constant recourse to the reading of the great philosophical works of antiquity; with a living Thomism, which implies: direct contact with the Aquinate himself, in his principal and secondary works, thus arriving at the authentic thought of St. Thomas, to the point of being able to think from within it, entering into dialogue and debate with contemporary problems and thinkers. A living Thomism, opposed to a formalist and fossilized Thomism, which is what Fr. Cornelio Fabro calls ‘essential Thomism’12”13. “Through the reading of the great commentators of St. Thomas”14, among whom our Proper Law explicitly mentions Fr. Cornelio Fabro, arguing that he is more important than all the commentators of the past “inasmuch as he is acquainted with all of them and possesses authentic texts and more advanced historical studies on the Aquinate, which put him in purer contact with the original thought of the Angelic Doctor”15. And lastly, “through the study of modern philosophy: since it is to the questions and challenges of modern authors that we must respond. In particular, it is fundamental to know critically the thought of Kant and Hegel”16.

That is why all the Chapter Fathers17, in keeping with the thought of our Founder and with what is mandated by our Proper Law, have always stressed –as an element attached to the non-negotiable charism–our clear intention to follow St. Thomas and, within this framework, Fr. Cornelio Fabro, who is in our view “the most profound connoisseur of St. Thomas of all time”18. He has likewise been recognized as such by many, among them Cardinal Georges M. M. Cottier, O.P., who wrote in the preface to the second, expanded edition of the “Introducción a Santo Tomas,” published after Fabro’s death: “Cornelio Fabro reveals himself as a sure exegete of the work of St. Thomas, which he places in its historical and doctrinal context. His knowledge extends to the history of scholasticism and of the Thomist currents. Among other things, starting from assiduous study of the great works, he understood the greatest dangers of modern thought from Descartes to Kant, and on to Hegel and Heidegger.”

1. A brief sketch of Cornelio Fabro

Who was Cornelio Fabro? Father Cornelio Fabro (August 24, 1911 – † May 4, 1995) was an Italian priest of the Congregation of the Sacred Stigmata of Our Lord Jesus Christ who, by the age of 25, already held four academic degrees: in Philosophy (granted by the Pontifical Lateran University), a licentiate in Theology (granted by the University of St. Thomas Aquinas, the Angelicum), in Natural Sciences (granted by the University of Rome), and finally a doctorate in theology, which he obtained with a theological dissertation on “The Metaphysical Notion of Participation according to St. Thomas Aquinas” at the Pontifical University of St. Thomas Aquinas. At that same age he began his career as a professor, being the youngest member of the Angelicum’s faculty at the time.

His thirst for Truth, from which his intellectual vocation was born, led him to a profound and scientific investigation of the works of St. Thomas, after “having helped to accelerate and in a certain way to bring to completion the movement of renewal of Thomistic metaphysics”19. He received numerous national and international awards and honors, outstanding among which are the “Aquinas Memorial Medal” (1974) granted by the American Catholic Philosophical Association (Washington, USA), the gold medal conferred on him by the “Societé Philosophique de Louvain” (1954), and the national gold medal for Catholic culture (Rome, Italy) in 1989. Moreover, he served as a member of the preparatory commission and as a peritus of the Second Vatican Council, and collaborated in the preparation of several documents of the Second Vatican Council, among them the Decree on Priestly Training “Optatam totius.” He was likewise a collaborator of the Congregation for the Causes of Saints (with studies on St. Edith Stein, St. Josemaría Escrivá de Balaguer, St. Gemma Galgani, etc.) and was a consultor of the Sacred Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, having been confirmed in that office three times by Paul VI and John Paul II.

Father Fabro was –in the words of the experts– a “gift of God to humanity and to the Church”20, who through his scientific study of St. Thomas and of all the other ancient and modern philosophers made great contributions to philosophical thought, the principal of which are:

  1. The primacy of the actus essendi, with the grasp of esse ut actus, of being as the act of all acts. The esse ut actus is the most singular contribution that St. Thomas has made to the philosophy of all time, and in which he surpasses all the thinkers of all time, including Aristotle and Plato and all the moderns, including Hegel and Heidegger. Fabro said: “Once one recognizes, as one should, the theological derivation and deviation of the metaphysics of immanence with its various ramifications, the task of the Thomism of the future seems to be that of penetrating into the demands of that proclaimed immanence, bringing it within the essential problem of thought, which is the foundation of the finite in the Infinite: thus clarifying the very principles of the “metaphysics of act,” not as an isolated cultural figure, but as the perennial substance of human philosophizing, in which the deficiencies and deviations of the systems vanish. Therefore it is to Thomism, more than to any other school of Christian thought, that this mission belongs: the unification of human consciousness, of the fragments of its historical becoming, in its universal theoretical structure”21

2. To refute most wisely the concretion of the principle of immanence, with its most baneful logical consequence: atheism. Fabro affirmed: “The situation of God (on God’s part) toward man has not changed and cannot change; on the other hand, the situation of man with respect to God has changed radically in modern thought with respect to preceding epochs and with respect to man’s very natural tendency […] The critique of modern atheism does not concern the problem of God but the problem of being, that is, the “problem of the beginning” (starting point) posed by Descartes and proposed anew time and again by Locke, Hume, Kant, Schelling, Hegel, Heidegger…, that is, the problem of the relation consciousness-being with respect to the foundation. Only one who begins from the being [ente] and rests on being [ser] can arrive at the Absolute of being, which is God; one who starts from the foundation of consciousness is bound to let himself be absorbed by the intrinsic finitude of his horizon, that is, to lose himself in the nothingness of being”22 .

The modern principle of immanence is intrinsically atheistic because it coincides with the radical affirmation of the I as substratum or horizon and principle of every affirmation-positing of being and of non-being in the strict sense, that is, being and non-being are resolved into the presence or absence of consciousness”23.

  1. His studies on the emergence of the will, recovering the most genuine metaphysical reflection on freedom. Thus he spoke of freedom: “The problem of freedom is therefore the problem of constitutive transcendental subjectivity, understanding ‘transcendental’ in the modern sense, precisely as constitutive of the very act of the subject. That is, any other human activity that is not referred to freedom is no longer human; any act of freedom that is not referred to the perfecting of the self as an original, non-derivable principle constitutive of itself, is not free. This is the ‘transcendental’ that I accept as legitimate in modern thought. Therefore, in this sense, freedom is the only point that is not derived and not derivable, not founded and not foundable, because it is freedom that founds itself. Freedom is that certain ‘quid’ which, in order to be able to express it, one must first engrave it in one’s own self, and it is always in experiment, always in tension, always at risk”24. He went so far as to affirm that “Freedom is the universal language of the human spirit; it is that language which speaks from the depth of its silence in the radical summons: freedom is what we most have in common, and the exercise of freedom is what most differentiates and distinguishes us”25.

And as the axis of these three great themes, he went deeper than anyone into the notion of participation.

Only the full rediscovery of being and of the first principles of being and of thought allows man to ascend validly to Subsistent Being itself, his principle and end, and the supreme guarantor of his inalienable freedom. As Fabro says: “The present crisis of theology, and by reflection of the post-conciliar Church, is metaphysical in nature: it is the obscuring, if not the explicit rejection, of the presence of the absolute on the horizon of the consciousness of contemporary man: a crisis that has been transferred to theologians through a ‘collision of sympathy,’ as Kierkegaard would say. Without reference to the absolute no value can exist; deprived of the metaphysical reference, the subject himself does not manage to constitute himself as a responsible operative center, and is overturned by the irrational play of the passions and of the forces of history. Without a transcendent God, creator of the world and of man, there exists no I as an unshakable core of freedom. Without the God-Man, redeemer and sanctifier, immanent in history as true man and transcendent in eternity as true God, according to the Chalcedonian formula, there exists no hope of salvation. Without metaphysics, then, there is no theology, there is no meaning or consistency to theology, since without the absolute foundation theological work dissolves into the precariousness of the method of the so-called ‘human sciences,’ into the insignificance of impression, of feeling, of semantic play, of empty emphasis. Without the absolute of metaphysics man lacks the foundation of ‘pietas’; the soul hardens in the pride of what is transient and the will is corrupted by the suggestion of the instincts: revolution as permanent protest, or suicide”26.

From what has been said thus far, one can gather how urgent and necessary, then, is the call our own Constitutions make to us to acquire “a profound knowledge of the philosophy of being”27.

For, as the Directory of Evangelization of Culture says most beautifully: “it is the philosophy of being that allows full and global openness to all of reality, surpassing every limit and allowing one to reach Him who perfects all things. […] Being able to discover the true cultural values. Because ultimately, all cultural values –of science, of art, of laws and customs, etc.– inasmuch as they are objective expressions of beauty, truth and goodness, are founded on the act of being28”29.

Our “pastoral care of culture will be able to offer a positive and effective response to the great challenges or even dramas of ‘post-modern’ man, principally on the basis of the metaphysical instance, by means of the philosophy of being. For the nihilist position, the present horizon of many philosophies that have strayed from the meaning of being, denies all objective truth and consequently that which grounds human dignity and freedom30. Hence the urgency of recovering the metaphysics of being, a dynamic philosophy that allows full and global openness toward the whole of reality, until arriving at Him who perfects all things31”32.

2. The Cornelio Fabro Cultural Project

That is why, although we are still in the initial phases, our Institute has embraced with great zeal and appreciation what has come to be called the “Cornelio Fabro Cultural Project.” For we understand –and it is undeniable– how privileged and important this Project is as a means to attain our specific end. The same was recognized by the General Chapter of 2016 itself when it “voted in favor of designating this project as a priority”33.

When this project began, some of the reasons for such an undertaking were set out, highlighting the intrinsic value of Cornelio Fabro’s work. Now, 15 years later, having published 25 books (two more are about to come out before December of this year), with the studies and doctoral dissertations of several of our religious, we can surely add several more.

3. Reading and studying Cornelio Fabro

For all that has been said above, it is eminently important and imperative that our religious, seminarians, sisters and lay people read, or at least try to read, Cornelio Fabro, according to their interests and abilities or the stage of formation in which they find themselves. Not everything of Fabro’s is difficult… Besides his great studies, there are also spiritual and formative writings, introductions to problems, etc. There are translations of more accessible works in several of our languages (Italian, English, Spanish, and, in the process of publication, Russian as well).

That is why I would like to add some reasons why we consider the task of reading and studying Fabro unavoidable. Two of them are certainly his Thomist philosophy and his knowledge of the modern world, as we have been saying. But I would like to put first one of a more particular character.

– First of all, it is a question of “family”; “noblesse oblige.” Fabro is no stranger to our congregation. Fr. Julio Meinvielle34 –whom we shall never cease to thank for having made Fr. Fabro known to us– was his personal friend and on several occasions gave him direct help. When Europe was living through the poverty of the Second World War, Fr. Meinvielle gave him the money needed to publish the second edition of “Noción Metafísica de Participación.” In those years, Argentina sent him the plane that brought Fr. Fabro and other important philosophers to the International Congress of Philosophy in 1949. Fabro’s works, both the philosophical and the formative ones, immediately became known in Argentina. For that reason, Fr. Buela and many of the priests influential in Argentine intellectual formation were formed, and formed many others, in the light of his works. So certain and thoroughly scientific are Fr. Fabro’s teachings that Fr. Buela himself adopted them from the beginning when, as a professor of Theodicy, he had recourse to his explanation, for example, for the study of the fourth way of the existence of God.

So much is this the case –and this must not escape our notice– that numerous works of Fr. Cornelio Fabro form an integral part of our Proper Law35. It is therefore definitely important to steep ourselves in their study.

Even more: this friendship between Fr. Fabro and Fr. Meinvielle, and with Argentina, was confirmed to us by Fr. Fabro himself. When in 1991 Fr. Buela, together with a group of student priests in Rome, visited Fr. Fabro, he had very laudatory words about Fr.’ Meinvielle: “I share all his theses,” he said. On that occasion, and at the special request of Fr. Buela, Fabro authorized Fr. Buela to publish all his works. I myself and several of us were able to take part in his funeral. On that occasion sour Rosa Goglia came to know us, and shortly afterward she entrusted to us all that today constitutes the Cornelio Fabro Cultural Project. We can therefore affirm that Fabro is very close to the origins of our Family. And… noblesse oblige!

– On the other hand, thanks to the work done in these years and to our dedication to the study of Fabro and the publication of his works, we have on several occasions been asked whether Fabro belonged to our Institute. Fr. Fabro, as you all know, did not belong to the IVE. Nevertheless, his erudite scientific study of St. Thomas makes him for us the sure shortcut to attain that “certainty of truth” of which we spoke at the beginning. As we have already mentioned above, as regards our intellectual formation we are Thomists. And as the Chapter Fathers rightly acknowledged at the last General Chapter, “we have received the immense grace of Thomism, of being inserted into the current of perennial philosophy. And with it a great responsibility, that of forming a school”36. This constitutes one more reason to dedicate ourselves to the study and diffusion of Fr. Fabro’s magnificent work.

I would like to add that Fr. Fabro’s collaboration in the preparation of the Second Vatican Council –working personally on the preparation of the reasons on which was based the position that St. Thomas Aquinas should be named the intellectual guide for the studies of priests and of teachers in Catholic schools–, his fidelity in the interpretation of Thomism, and his labors so that St. Thomas might be known and studied, all come together to form a very strong reason that increases still further our confidence in the solidity of his doctrine when it comes to reading and studying his works.

– A third reason is the serious concern Fabro had to know and to evangelize our world, this modern world: to know the root of the atheism that sadly permeates the modern mentality, its metaphysical root, which he called the “principle of immanence.”

According to Fr. Fabro, this principle cuts off every kind of transcendence: reality is immanent to man’s thought and will…; the subsequent denial of Divine Transcendence is nothing but the necessary conclusion of the first denial. That is why Fabro shows that atheism is virtually present in the principle of immanence, initiated by Descartes and followed by Kant, Hegel and many more. The philosophy and culture of immanence is “anti-Christian and anti-human,” he had said in the 1950s. Near the end of his life he inverted the formula, saying that it is “first of all anti-human and therefore anti-Christian.” But Fabro did not content himself with denouncing modern philosophical atheism and, by derivation, theological atheism (such as the Death of God theology… or Karl Rahner’s anthropological turn); rather, for many years he strove to show the necessity of the right use of freedom and the value of the person who must “choose” before God, a concrete subject called to run the “risk” of choosing God as his concrete existential end, to “constitute himself” in this end and make it the determining value of his life.

We want to evangelize this world, and therefore we must know it from its roots. It is telling that when Paul VI, in a private audience, asked him about the problems of the Church, Fabro took the liberty of suggesting to the Holy Father that “according to the spirit desired by the Second Vatican Council of going out to meet the modern world, it seemed (to him) necessary that in the Church modern thought be known directly, and that therefore candidates for the episcopate should hold doctorates in modern philosophy”37.

As a Religious Family whose “specific and singular end is to dedicate ourselves to the evangelization of culture”38, we cannot be ignorant of the world in which we live. For we do not want to evangelize the culture “with a superficial veneer, but in a vital way, in depth and right to its very roots”39. We must therefore know reality in the same way, that is, from its very philosophical roots, and in this sense the help that Fabro can give us is first-rate.

To this we can also add the extraordinary help that Fabro’s profound reflection on freedom, as the ultimate expression of the person and the radical possession of one’s own self, means for our spirituality and for our pastoral service in the Church.

The merciful providence of our Lord willed to place this treasure and this mission in our hands. His works –books, articles, courses, recordings– are a true treasure that belongs to us and that deserves to be shared with the world. That is, this treasure becomes in turn an unavoidable mission: to work so that this material may be available to all “men of thought or intellectuals”40. This falls squarely within what we call “our proper apostolates,” since to work at that important turning point of culture which is thinkers and scholars “constitutes at present the privileged path for the creation and transmission of culture”41. It is a mission in which we glimpse unimaginable fruits, and which from now on we abandon trustingly to the divine economy.

So dear is this project to our Institute that the Chapter Fathers, speaking of it, affirmed: “It is a project of enormous scope, whose fruits may be lasting and of an importance that today we are not able to gauge fully. We can barely glimpse it from the good that knowing Fabro’s works has done for us members of the Institute. […] It would be most important to dedicate ourselves more to the translation of Fabro’s works, especially into English, which has already begun”42. Indeed, the Chapter itself voted in favor of designating this project as a priority43.

* * * * *

Dear All: This is a treasure that has been enriching us since the beginning and of which we can be proud and deeply grateful. How beneficial it is –today and always– to cultivate one’s own identity: identity at the religious level, at the pastoral level, at the missionary level and also – and no less important – at the level of intellectual formation: we are Thomists, but not of a scholastic current; and we are Thomists, but not in just any way, rather as heirs of a living Thomism capable of biting into reality, founded on the primacy of act, of the “act of being” that derives from God, Pure Act, and of the “act of rightly choosing” God as our ultimate end.

Just as the names of Peter and Paul, of St. Thomas Aquinas, of St. John of the Cross and of St. Teresa of Avila, of St. Ignatius of Loyola and of St. John Bosco, and the names of all our patron saints give us a clear and indisputable identity –each in its order and hierarchy– so too the name of Cornelio Fabro helps to shape our identity. And on some occasions the name of Fabrian Thomism presents itself as clearer, because it is more concrete and closer: those who do not share this line of formation know it, and it is right that we should know it ourselves… and value it, and make it bear fruit… and among ourselves in the first place.

For all these reasons it is most fitting that our religious men and women, the members of the Third Order and our friends come to know and benefit from the thought of Cornelio Fabro.

Let us always be faithful to this mission and wisdom that has been bequeathed to us and that constitutes a mark that adorns and honors our beloved Religious Family. Fr. Fabro himself declared: “The next millennium will be the millennium of St. Thomas”44. This means that we must put all our strength into knowing St. Thomas very well and making him well known, as is fitting. For his study has a colossal importance. Because God has willed that by the strength and truth of the doctrine of the Angelic Doctor “…all the heresies and errors that should follow, confounded and convicted, might be dispelled…”45.

I would like to conclude with a marvelous paragraph from the Directory of Evangelization of Culture: “To the challenges that come from the shadows and imbalances of present-day culture –the rejection of every possibility of foundation and truth, ethical disorientation and loss of meaning, the affirmation of the ephemeral and of a totally autonomous conscience, etc.– one must respond positively with an incisive and long-term pastoral care of culture. On the basis of an authentically metaphysical vision, one must make the effort to pass from phenomenon to foundation, grounding and bearing witness to the certainty of the truth about man and his freedom, and about the global and transcendent meaning of human existence. […] To the thought that shuts itself up within the limits of its own immanence and abandons the search for the transcendent and absolute, with all the tragic consequences this entails for the life of man, one must respond that authentic respect for personal dignity, which entails the defense and promotion of human rights, “demands the recognition of the religious dimension of man,” for “the relationship with God is a constitutive element of the very ‘being’ and ‘existing’ of man: it is in God that we live and move and have our being (Acts 17:28)”46. The glory of God is the life of man47. The riches of the Gospel must be offered generously and abundantly to every man of good will, so that he may freely welcome the truth that saves and makes man truly free.

A renewed pastoral care of culture must be carried forward, for culture constitutes the privileged place of encounter with the message of Christ.

This is not a task for specialists only, but for everyone”48.

[Exordium] 747 years ago, on the morning of Wednesday, March 7, 1274, St. Thomas Aquinas received extreme unction right here, in the Cistercian monastery of Fossanova.

Since then the Saint of Aquino has been acclaimed by pontiffs, academics, religious and non-religious alike as the “Prince and Master of all the scholastic doctors”1, as the “model for teachers and model for students”2. For his doctrine has earned him the titles of “Doctor Ecclesiae” and “Doctor Angelicus” (both titles granted by St. Pius V); the title of “Patronus caelestis studiorum optimorum” (conferred by Pope Leo XIII), and he is the one whom the Church calls “Doctor communis” (Common Doctor). St. John Paul II more recently gave him the title of “Doctor humanitatis,” arguing that St. Thomas “was always ready to welcome the values of all cultures”3.

And particularly today, when we are gathered as a Religious Family to honor our Holy Patron, I would like to speak of the importance of Thomistic doctrine when it comes to the evangelization of culture.

1. A non-negotiable element

Many times, throughout the length and breadth of our Proper Law, we are exhorted to give the doctrine of St. Thomas a preferential place4, to be formed under his magisterium5 in a profound way6, “to think starting from him, entering into dialogue and debate with contemporary problems and thinkers”7. And one may ask: why?

To be formed according to the doctrine of St. Thomas is not mere devotion, although his virtues, the sublimity of his thought and the holiness of his life move us to devotion.

Nor is it one option of ‘discipleship’ among so many others we might have chosen, although it is definitely a conscientious and very firm choice, for we have “the clear intention of following St. Thomas as the Church commands”8.

Nor is it an accessory, temporary or decorative element simply ‘because it suits us well.’ The formation we give and receive, our way of doing apostolate, of living community life, and our spirituality are inherently and fully informed by Thomistic doctrine, which is −let us say it once again− “the doctrine most in conformity with the Magisterium of the Church”9 and without which there can be no transformation of persons and therefore of cultures; much less can there be a perfecting of them10 (so closely is St. Thomas bound to our charism).

That is, we cannot fulfill the specific end for which God has raised up our little Religious Family in the Church, which is precisely: “to inculturate the Gospel in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man, in accordance with the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church”11, without being steeped in the philosophy of being12. For is not culture a specific mode of man’s ‘existing’ and ‘being’?

Put in a few words, one cannot be a member of the Institute of the Incarnate Word and have as one’s charism “to make Jesus Christ Lord of all that is authentically human”13 and of every “legitimate manifestation of human life”14 in the strictest fidelity to the Magisterium of the Church15 without embracing the “golden wisdom of St. Thomas”16.

Precisely because of that “openness to the whole of reality in all its parts and dimensions, without reductions or particularisms (without absolutizing a particular aspect), as the intellect demands in the name of objective and integral truth concerning reality.

This openness is also a significant distinguishing mark of the Christian faith, of which catholicity is the specific sign. This openness has its foundation and its source in the fact that the philosophy of St. Thomas is a philosophy of being, that is, of the ‘actus essendi,’ whose transcendental value is the most direct path for rising to the knowledge of subsistent Being and pure Act, which is God”17.

That is why it is entirely logical that following Thomistic philosophy should be for us one of the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism, and that it should be for us a most vivid sign of fidelity to the truth and of fidelity to the voice of the Church (and of apostolic fruitfulness).

That is why I believe it can be said with all rigor that anything that attempts to set aside, diminish, dilute or render “wavering and superficial” our Thomistic philosophical formation is nothing other than a departure from the charism, a betrayal of the truth and ultimately of the Church and of the cause of Christ.

And it is important that every member of the Institute keep this very much in mind, whatever his mission, whether he is in formation, holds an office of government, or is a contemplative: We are Thomists because we belong to the Incarnate Word and devote ourselves to the evangelization of culture, and without the realism and objectivity of Thomistic philosophy our efforts are in vain, ineffective, or rather, they become paralyzed.

2. Genus humanum arte et ratione vivit18

In this regard, I would like to point out the following: “St. Thomas, commenting on Aristotle, says that ‘the human race lives by its art and its reason’”19 (I am quoting our Directory of Evangelization of Culture word for word).

“Relying on these words, St. John Paul II affirmed that culture is a ‘characteristic of human life as such’20; ‘it is a specific mode of man’s ‘existing’ and ‘being’’; man alone –because he transcends matter by virtue of the life of his spirit– ‘is the only ontic subject of culture’ and is ‘also its only object and its end.’ That is, culture is of man, by man and for man. It embraces the whole activity of man, his intelligence and affectivity, his search for meaning, his customs and his ethical resources. Culture is so connatural to man that his nature does not attain its full expression except through culture. Therefore, ‘culture is that through which man, as man, becomes more human, ‘is’ more, has more access to ‘being’’”21.

“Hence the irreplaceable importance of establishing the true foundations so as to be able, starting from them, to discern which manifestations of man are truly culture and which manifestations are not”22.

This can only be achieved from a philosophy of being, which is what will allow man to find his true foundation, which is being, to find his ultimate end, which is Being by Essence, and also to find his depth, which is freedom. Thus he can discover the true cultural values. Because, in the final analysis, all cultural values –of science, of art, of laws and customs, etc.– as objective expressions of beauty, truth and goodness, are grounded in the act of being23.

That is why we must realize that each one of us, and those who will come after us, will only be able to “offer a positive and effective response to the great challenges or even tragedies of ‘postmodern’ man principally starting from the metaphysical level, through the philosophy of being24.

You know well that the great cultural challenge of today lies in the sphere of man’s openness to truth and to the good, and ultimately to transcendence and to the mystery of God. That is why the central point of every culture is occupied by the attitude man takes before the mystery of God25.

[Peroration]That is why let no one who devotes himself to the evangelization of culture say ‘St. Thomas is not for me,” since Thomism is an integral part of our vocation; it is the bulwark that defends us from the errors that swarm in our society; it is the proof that we keep intact the spirit in which we were conceived and the best guarantee of our fidelity to the Church, Teacher of Truth.

By vocation and by the mission we have received, it falls to us to have a strong cultural formation, one that takes a stand before modern culture26, a “metaphysics with grip,” that bites into reality.

Today, in one of the rooms of this very abbey, more than seven centuries ago, the earthly existence of the one who was called “Luminary of the Church and of the whole world”27 came to an end. Nevertheless it is necessary, and I would say urgent, “to propose anew the thought of the Angelic Doctor in order to recover a use of philosophy in keeping with the demands of faith”28, to “develop that part of Thomistic doctrine which deals with humanity, since his affirmations about the dignity of the human person and about the use of his reason, perfectly in accord with faith, make St. Thomas a supreme master for our time”29.

Let us hold high the torch of the light of the Sun of Aquino30, who illumined the Church of God more than any other doctor31, and “let us illumine the darkness of the world like suns”32.

May the words that were written long ago on the walls of this “cell” remind us of our “mission” in this place

Occidit hic Thomas lux ut foret amplior Orbi et candelabru sic nova fossa foret

Here Thomas died so as to become [from this place]

the most splendid light of the world
so that Fossanova might be the lampstand that holds up that light

Editus ardeti locus et non fossa lucerna, hac igitur fossam quis neget esse novam?

This place has been exalted so that this light may shine and not be hidden,
so that
who could deny that this pit is truly new?

Almost fifty years ago the seventh centenary of the death of St. Thomas Aquinas was celebrated, and Pope St. Paul VI decided to visit the Thomistic sites; he wanted to make a personal pilgrimage but could not, because many people from the area came to meet him at the various places.

He left by helicopter very early, from the summer residence of Castel Gandolfo, on September 14, 1974, and the first place where he stopped was here in Fossanova (there should be a plaque around here recalling where the Holy Father landed). He arrived here in the morning, celebrated Holy Mass (in the afternoon he would go by car to Aquino, where he would also celebrate Mass) and delivered a brief but heartfelt homily. There, among many very beautiful things, he said these words:

“[In a place like this we cannot avoid] asking ourselves: why are we here?… Certainly it is not only to perform a gesture of religious veneration, as if, before the appearance of the Saint on the screen of our conscience, we did not bow, trembling and happy, before his great and hieratic figure.

Rather, his figure, made present and alive by the communion of Saints, immediately provokes in us a bold question: Master Thomas, what lesson can you give us [today]?

To us, in this brief and intense moment that is the present; to us, who are seven centuries removed from your school; to us, who are galvanized by modern culture; to us, who are proud of our scientific knowledge; to us, distracted by ‘the fascination of frivolity,’ the fascinatio nugacitatis,of which the book of Wisdom speaks (Wis 4:12). A fascination we experience today, giving prevalence to sense knowledge over intellectual and spiritual knowledge; a dizzying fascination or dizzying spell; to us, subjected to the anesthesia of antireligious secularism; to us, St. Thomas, before whom your figure grows greater, as philosopher and as theologian, on the horizon of a thought eager for certainty, for clarity, for depth, for reality; to us [Thomas], even if with only one word, what can you say?”

And the holy Pope continues: “St. Thomas does not answer us now with words, although there are many that nevertheless come to our ears at this moment through his works. He answers us [nevertheless] with the reflection of his figure and of his teaching, from which we seem to hear a lesson that exhorts us [… that cries out]: faith… confidence…confidence,in the truth of Catholic religious thought, as it was defended, expounded and opened to the cognitive capacity of the human mind by him [himself, by St. Thomas Aquinas]”33.

To these words follows, and to this is dedicated, the whole rest of the homily: to the confidence we must have in Thomism. There he says very beautiful words, of which I mention only a few: faith and confidence in St. Thomas:

  • Because “his work stands attested in the history of thought, both philosophical and theological, as a synthesis of what other eminent masters before him studied and left as an inheritance to universal culture”;

  • Because “he assimilated the treasure of the most significant knowledge of his time (a time incomparable for the breadth and acuteness of its speculative study); [because he not only assimilated it, but] he refined it with the most rigorous intellectualism, the Aristotelian… so as to bring it into harmony with our rigorous modern scientific mentality”;

  • Because this thought, his thought, “he submitted without prejudice to the dialectical discussion of an honest and rigorous rationality”; and “therefore he opened it to every possible progressive acquisition, should it ever be called for by the discovery of a further truth.”

  • Because his thought “helps us to resolve the conflict, so loudly proclaimed and radicalized in our time, between the two forms of knowledge available to the mind of the believer, faith and reason (fides et ratio)”;

  • Because he resolved everything “taking as his starting point the revealed word of God, sustained by reasonable motives of credibility, committing the human mind to study it with its own proper and original principles and methods, in such a way that the resulting theology may, without presumption and without superstition, reach a true and marvelous level of vera scientia Dei.”

  • Faith, because St. Thomas is for us “one of the most authoritative and convincing witnesses to the providential existence of that magisterium, entrusted by Christ to His Church, which does not exclude the paths of knowledge, but opens them, rectifies them and defends them” [and which does not withhold the light of life-giving Truth for those initiated into the labors, the ascents and the acrobatics of thought, but offers it with humble and sublime catechesis to all those in the Church herself who recognize themselves as disciples, and reserves the revelation of the highest and most saving mysteries of the faith to the little ones, to the simple, to the poor, to the People unversed in difficult speculations, but docile and open to the ineffable dialogue of the Word of God]”34.

It is this faith and this confidence in St. Thomas that we ask in this Holy Mass of our Lord (whom St. Thomas loved so much) through the intercession of the Blessed Virgin (whom he also loved greatly), Mother of the Incarnate Word, and through the intercession of the Angelic Doctor himself.

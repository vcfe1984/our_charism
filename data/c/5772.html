This coming May 8 the Church throughout the world will celebrate the World Day of Prayer for Vocations, which will happily coincide with the beautiful feast of our Most Holy Mother, Our Lady of Luján, and with the 5th anniversary of our offering of the Golden Rose “in perpetual testimony of our love and gratitude for the vocations that She sends to our Religious Family… and as a pledge of confidence for the many that through her intercession we hope to conceive”1. How much we have to thank Our Lady for!

From the last General Chapter (July 2016) until the present, our small Institute has given the Church 103 priests! for the greater glory of God. That is a very great grace for the Institute. Just to grasp the scale of the blessing this represents, let us consider that there are very important dioceses that have had no priestly ordinations, or very few, in the last 10 or 20 years; the same unfortunately happens with religious congregations of great prestige and tradition.

Moreover, today we have 503 vocations in formation2, of which only 16% are Argentine vocations and 84% come from other countries3, which speaks of the fruitfulness with which God has been pleased to crown the evangelizing efforts of our missionaries throughout the length and breadth of this world, so that the message of Christ may reach more effectively the heart of each of its cultures.

Likewise, we consider it no minor detail that Our Lady of Luján has sent the Institute vocations coming from the same family. For double family ties –by blood and by the spirit– not only contribute to the unity and cohesion of the Institute, but are an important apostolic witness for other families and, to tell the truth, for other Christians.

Therefore, in view of celebrating next May 8 the World Day of Prayer for Vocations within the setting of the Solemnity of the Most Pure Conception of Luján, we would like to address in these lines the priority commitment of each member of the Institute to promote vocations, and one of the means of promoting vocations, which is precisely “family ministry, which is of itself vocational”4.

1. A priority commitment

“Because Christ is one, we want to work with all our strength… So that all men may confess the adorable Name of the Lord Jesus, fulfilling his commandment: Go into the whole world and preach the Gospel to every creature5”6.

Now, without enough laborers for the harvest, it will not be possible for us to carry out Christ’s mandate –Go into the whole world and preach the Gospel to every creature7– which is the very reason for the existence and mission of the Institute in history, nor to renew each day the Eucharistic sacrifice –Do this in memory of me8– “because without a priest there can be no Eucharistic sacrifice”9.

At the same time, it is well known to all that every vocation is a gift of God and, like all gifts that come from God, they arrive through many human mediations: parents, educators, pastors, some good Catholic friend, etc. But certainly, the first who must feel involved in vocational ministry are those who are themselves called to the ministerial priesthood. And if this is true for all priests, it is doubly so for the members of the Institute, since it is an “integral element of our spirituality to know how to call, teach, direct, accompany and select priestly, diaconal, religious, missionary and secular vocations”10, a task that none of the members of the Institute can evade. Even more: “the pastoral care of vocations is intrinsic to the end of the evangelization of culture and to the charism of the Institute, since the evangelical counsels are an integral part of the message of salvation and those who follow them especially manifest the ‘eschatological character of the Church’11, being the adornment of the Bride of Christ”12.

Up to here, nothing new. We know this. However, it is easy to become lethargic or to give priority to some other kind of ministry –perhaps of more immediate fruit– rather than to vocational ministry.

And how are priestly and religious vocations promoted? We know very well that at the basis of all vocational ministry, as Our Lord Jesus Christ taught, lies authentic and persevering prayer asking for more laborers for the harvest13; nevertheless, our Proper Law explicitly points out to us a necessary and consequent means: “through the faithful and joyful witness of consecrated life”14. It is what St. Paul expresses when he says: I beg you to walk as befits the vocation to which you have been called15. “Conduct that corresponds to the vocation,” says St. John Paul II, “makes new vocations spring up. This consistent conduct constitutes, as it were, the permanent basis of prayer; it prepares it, and prayer is like its development; reciprocally, prayer continually calls for such behavior”16. Therefore, before the hundreds of projects we can carry out with a view to vocational ministry, the first, together with prayer, is that of priestly and religious witness. We cannot ignore that our life is always a significant presence beside young people: it encourages or discourages, it awakens the desire for God or constitutes an obstacle to following Him. That is why consistent and joyful witness represents the first vocational proposal within reach of any of our members.

If we truly give a joyful witness of serving Christ “carrying out our proper apostolates with competence and generosity”17, if we are capable of radiating hope because of the faith that dwells in our breast despite all the weariness of the struggle, if we truly give ourselves to souls without neglecting prayer, how can we doubt that vocations will arise around us? The vocations that God has sent us through Our Lady of Luján from countries with a Christian minority such as Tajikistan, the Gaza Strip, Egypt; or from places where the presence of the Institute is scarce, as in Papua New Guinea, or nonexistent, as in Sri Lanka, India, Guatemala, Slovakia, etc., prove that prayer united to the witness of a consistent life always bears fruit where God wills and as God wills.

Nevertheless, none of us is unaware, however few years of religious life he may have, that missionaries are lacking in the outskirts of the great cities, in rural areas, among the inhabitants of the high mountain regions and in the immensity of the jungle. There is a lack of priests to devote themselves to the young, to families, to the elderly and sick, to workers, to intellectuals, to professionals and to the uneducated, to artists, to the rich and to the poor, to those of our own homeland and to immigrants… It is our experience that a greater number of priests and religious is urgently needed in parishes, in parish groups, in schools and universities, in factories, and in so many other fields… we can even say “that the ends of the earth, to which the Gospel must reach, are drawing ever farther away”18.

This lack of laborers for the harvest already constituted a challenge for Jesus himself in Gospel times. His example allows us to understand that the too-small number of consecrated persons is a situation inherent to the condition of the Church and of the world, and not merely an accidental fact due to present circumstances. However, the Incarnate Word, moved with compassion for the multitudes, gave them his teaching because He saw that they were weary and downcast, like sheep without a shepherd19, but He wanted his disciples also to take part in the solution, inviting them above all to pray20. We too can and must influence the number of vocations through prayer.

Sometimes when we say that we must pray for vocations we often think it is a general intention; however, we judge it fitting that there should not be lacking frequent and explicit prayer for vocations, especially for our Institute. Nor is it any less important to invite others to pray –as is in fact already done in various places through the “40 Hours Project” or the “priestly Thursdays” or the “rosary for vocations”– because we must be aware that vocations will not be able to take the step by their own strength, and for this reason they must be stimulated with prayer, accompanied and sustained, understanding that a vocation is a true gift that comes from heaven. We highlight here the preponderant role of the contemplatives of the Institute, since the abundance and quality of priestly, contemplative, missionary and consecrated vocations for the Institute depends in no small degree on generous and joyful fidelity to the contemplative life. It is moving to think that our Lord has willed to associate the precious gift of vocations for his Church with the joined hands of a monk and his silent immolation.

Together with this, it is almost evident that vocational ministry requires closeness to young people, without which the witness of consecrated life cannot be given. And many of them are searching for a meaning to their lives. That is why we must be lavish in inviting them to collaborate in our pastoral activities: to take part in our Masses, in the feasts of the Institute, to help in the homes, to volunteer in the various missions of the Institute, to take part in popular missions, to help in organizing Spiritual Exercises, oratories or any other apostolic activity; to invite them to be catechists, to take part in the Voices of the Word, to consecrate themselves to Jesus through Mary, to make Spiritual Exercises themselves, to invite them to camps, etc. In short, our Institute, by the grace of God, has countless opportunities and a great variety of activities to involve others in the cause of Christ, and to a great extent it depends on us how much room we give them.

It is also, and has always been, an option in our Institute to invite young people to share our religious life, taking the precautions that each case requires. Did not Christ say to Andrew and John: come and you will see21? Let it be well understood that vocational ministry cannot be exhausted in occasional and extraordinary initiatives –such as the “come and see” or the “open house” events organized by the houses of formation– but must be one of the constant concerns of the ministry of each of the priests of the Institute. Always –unless the superior disposes otherwise– the doors of our houses can be opened to young people; they can be invited to eat pizza on Fridays with the other members of the community, to go on an outing or a pilgrimage; in short, the occasions are countless.

Far be it from our members the temptation to say “I’m past that sort of thing,” “there are already the seminary people who do apostolate with young people,” “it’s not my job because I’m in a Hogarcito,” “I’m a contemplative,” “the young people here are into other things,” “I’m alone in my parish,” or some similar excuse. To all who think this way we answer with what St. John Paul II said to some contemplatives, but which we judge applies also to those in active life: “Young people today are animated by great ideals and, if they see consistent men, witnesses of the Gospel, they follow them with enthusiasm. To propose to today’s world the practice of the life hidden in Christ means to reaffirm the value of humility, of poverty, of interior freedom. The world, which deep down is thirsty for these virtues, wants to see upright men who practice them with everyday heroism, moved by the awareness of loving and serving their brothers through this witness”22. And that concerns us all. Moreover, at some moment God puts each of us in contact with a young man “made of good timber” to whom we can explicitly propose the consideration of the priestly vocation. How many of us entered the Institute because a priest had the courage and insight to propose the vocation to us with enthusiasm. We must not be pessimistic, resigned or timid in speaking of vocations. That was not the example we received. Without doubt the seed of the vocation is in the hearts of many young people and is simply waiting for a favorable occasion to germinate. “To seek vocations is also to propose them: ‘with passion and discretion’23”24, says our Proper Law.

Let us also note that our Proper Law affirms that in vocational ministry “the best energies must be invested”25. And when we say the best energies we are referring to the quality of the time we devote to vocational ministry –it is essential to be available to young people–; to the generosity with which we use all means –even financial ones– to seek, sustain and accompany vocations; to dedication to study and personal preparation in order to be able to give young people good formation, so that we ourselves may know how to discern well, etc. All, and very especially pastors, must conceive of vocational ministry as an obligatory dimension of the overall pastoral plan. “In pastoral action it must never be forgotten that to educate in the faith also means to develop the vocational dynamism proper to Christian life. To be a Christian is, of itself, a vocation, a call: the highest and strongest vocation, and the basis of every specific following within the ecclesial community”26. That is why the Directory of Parishes pointedly says: “Let it be remembered that vocations will be the sign of a parish’s maturity27, and that vocational work must become a pastoral priority, knowing how to foster them with generosity and to let go of them when they decide to follow Jesus Christ more closely”28.

In this sense, it seems necessary to us to make special mention of the parish, which always has a decisive role with regard to priestly and religious vocations. The parish is where young people in fact live their Christian experience; in it they hear the message of Christ and are inserted into the life of grace; it is also in the parish that they come into contact with our priests, brothers, sisters, seminarians and even occasionally with our monks. In this regard, the importance of the witness and good example of each of them is evident, as the normal instrument of God’s call to a more generous service.

It is imperative “to reactivate an intense pastoral action which, starting from the Christian vocation in general and from an enthusiastic youth ministry, may give the Church the servants she needs”29. In addition to all the means already mentioned, in our Institute we have the precious resource of the festive Oratory, which should not be lacking in any IVE parish. For “the Oratory is an incipient source of vocations”30 and very probably “the only form of complete Christian education accessible to great masses of young people”31.

An element of great importance also when it comes to awakening vocations is community witness. Experience has shown us that it is often the example of a religious, of a priest, that contributes decisively to the realization of a vocation to the priesthood or to consecrated life. Similarly, the community witness of fidelity and joy arouses an enormous attraction toward religious life and is, definitively, a source of new vocations and a support for perseverance32. In other words, “religious communities cannot attract young people except through a collective witness of authentic consecration, lived in the joy of personal self-giving to Christ and to the brethren”33. How many times young people have approached us simply because of this witness of joy, because they have seen us give ourselves generously to apostolic work in the missions ad gentes, or simply because they met us while climbing some mountain or on some other community outing. For this reason it continues to be very important to cultivate joy in the religious community34.

Finally, we must highlight the importance of family ministry as one of the means of promoting vocations. Our Proper Law points this out in several of its documents, and it is what we will address in the second point.

2. Family ministry is of itself vocational

The Directory of the Third Order, quoting St. John Paul II, says: “The family, the domestic Church, is the first field in which God cultivates vocations. For this reason it is necessary to understand that a correct and careful family ministry is, by itself, a vocational ministry”35. Indeed, “God has willed to bless us, from the very beginnings of our Institutes, with large families of sound human and Christian formation, from which numerous vocations to consecrated life or to the priesthood have sprung”36. And while this has been so since the beginning, we have all seen it in the recent priestly ordinations. Therefore, a careful dedication to family ministry, and to the Third Order in particular, ensures the life of our Religious Family, preparing the ground and hearts for the call of the Incarnate Word37.

The family is one of the turning points of culture38 and, therefore, family ministry concerns us all: not only the one in charge of the Third Order, not only the one who is a pastor, not only the one who is a priest, not only the specialists39, but all, because we are all members of this Institute whose charism has as its first field of action “to prolong Christ in families”40.

Having said this, let us now turn to the close relationship that exists between families and the priestly and religious vocation.

The Spiritual Father of our Religious Family, in a message for the World Day of Prayer for Vocations, wrote the following: “The family, to the extent that it becomes aware of this genuine vocation [apostolic vocation through the sacrament of matrimony] and responds to it, the family in which one learns to live meekness, justice, mercy, chastity, peace, purity of heart, becomes what St. John Chrysostom, in other words, calls the domestic church, that is, the place in which Jesus Christ lives and works the salvation of men and the growth of the kingdom of God”41.

To help this come about, and so that families may truly be helped and instructed in fulfilling their own duties and Christian life may be fostered within families, pastoral work is necessary. That is why, among the very varied means and opportunities that God gives us to carry out this apostolate, our Proper Law exhorts us to visit families as an occasion to share in family concerns, to pour out upon them the superabundance of Christ through the timely word, and to correct them paternally if they stray from good conduct42. It must be acknowledged that God has often made use of this contact on our part with families to call many priestly and religious vocations. Indeed, not a few members of the Institute themselves considered the vocation or simply drew close to the Religious Family through these visits to families –families of the parish, of the Third Order, of the catechism children, of the school students, of benefactors, of the young people of the Oratory, etc. Visits to families remain an ever-current apostolate in our Institute that should not be neglected.

“The task of Christian parents,” John Paul II goes on to say, “is very important and delicate, because they are called to prepare, cultivate and defend the vocations that God awakens in their family. They must, therefore, enrich themselves and their family with spiritual and moral values”43. But if there is no one to offer them these values, to teach them or remind them of them, how do we expect that tomorrow the vocations that may arise in that family will not be stifled? And do we still dare to be surprised and even complain if families let themselves be swept along by the consumerism, hedonism or secularism that disturb and hinder the realization of God’s plan, if we ourselves do not exhort them to the contrary? If we do not form parents in generosity toward God when He calls one of their children, and even to ask, for the good of the Church, the inestimable gift of a vocation for their children, are we amazed that vocations do not arise from our parishes, from our missions, from our jurisdictions? With great sorrow John Paul the Great wrote: “How can children, left morally orphaned, without educators or models, grow in esteem for human and Christian values? How can the seeds of vocation that the Holy Spirit continues to place in the hearts of the young generations develop in such a climate?”44. St. Manuel González already said it: “it is not that vocations are lacking, but that Christian homes are lacking, which are the ovens that give warmth, atmosphere and life to the vocations that God gives”45. We must become aware that the family is the privileged place for authentic vocational growth.

It is for this reason that we say that “vocational ministry finds its first and natural setting in the family. Parents, in fact, must know how to welcome as a grace the gift that God gives them in calling one of their children to the priesthood or to consecrated life. Such a grace is asked for in prayer, and it is positively welcomed when children are educated to understand all the richness and joy of consecrating oneself to God”46.

This requires forming families in the knowledge of this important aspect of their mission, which makes necessary a ministry directed toward spouses knowing themselves to be cooperators in the mission of the Church, creating a family climate of faith, charity and prayer that leads the children to be available and to accept God’s plan for the life of each one. In this sense it is important that we know the problems families face so as to be able to instruct spouses, through the proclamation of the Word of God, in their specific responsibilities, so that, well formed in the faith, they may know how to accompany their children, possibly called, to give themselves to God without reserve. In fact, it is through the apostolate we carry out with families that many children have found the support of their parents to be able to realize their vocation.

“Parents who accept with feelings of gratitude and joy the call of one of their sons or daughters to the special consecration for the kingdom of heaven […] discover with amazement that, thanks to the sacred vocation of their children, the gift of their love has been multiplied beyond limited human dimensions”47. How many times has it happened to us that the parents of other religious men or women receive us as if we were their own children; how many times they themselves have told us that with their son’s entrance into the seminary or their daughter’s into the convent “the family has grown bigger” … and how beautiful that it should be so. From this also follows the important duty of gratitude and exquisite charity that we owe not only to our own parents but to the parents of the other religious, especially those who do not see their children so often, who are older, who are going through various hardships and who belong to our parishes, to our jurisdictions, etc.

3. Mother of the Lord and our Mother

An example that somewhat illustrates the priestly attitude in vocational ministry is given to us by St. Luigi Orione. He himself puts it in writing in a letter he wrote from Tortona to a married couple who were friends and benefactors of his, on the vigil of the liturgical feast of the Assumption in 1927. Speaking of this Marian feast he writes with great tenderness and subtlety:

“Just to think of Our Lady, of the most sweet Mother of God and our Mother, the spirit grows calm, the mind becomes serene; in speaking of Our Lady joy spreads, it is like a wave of most gentle spiritual peace, and in invoking her courage is restored and life returns to me, the highest life!”48. Then he adds with great insight: “Happy the parents who offer their children into the hands of the Mother of the Lord!”49.

Then the saint continues, giving news concerning new vocations of sisters and future priests: “Tomorrow morning, at 5:30, I will sing Holy Mass in the humble little house of San Bernardino and then I will give the habit of Perpetual Adorers of Jesus in the Blessed Sacrament to four blind women who have already been with us for years.

Then I leave for Villa Moffa near Bra, to give the religious habit tomorrow afternoon to some young men…

Then, in a few months, Rag. Adriano Callegari will also enter. Out of him, the hand of God will make good cloth”50.

Then with complete naturalness and courage he poses the question: “And you, will you give me one of your children? I will not keep him for myself; I will give him at once to Our Lady, and he will be the consolation, the glory, the blessing of your family”51.

Trusting devotion to Our Lady, the promotion of vocations and the courage to propose the vocation –things of which St. Luigi Orione gives us an example– should be corollaries of our vocational ministry.

Tender and trusting devotion to the Blessed Virgin is truly a bulwark and an incentive in our priestly ministry, especially when it comes to awakening vocations. And so it has been from the beginning. Our Founder used to say: “I am convinced of it, and I attribute to the intercession of Our Lady of Luján the vocations that God gives us.”

Regarding the role of Most Holy Mary in awakening, welcoming, accompanying and sustaining vocations of special consecration, we would like to point out a few elements.

Our Institute, being “essentially missionary and Marian”52, and because of our particular way of living our consecration to Christ through consecration in slavery to Most Holy Mary, has in devotion to Our Lady a special quality that wins over many souls. Thus, many of our members became interested in the Institute precisely because of our deep Marian devotion; others Our Lady brought to us on the occasion of some Marian feast or a visit to some Marian shrine; others through taking part in the groups of consecration to Our Lady, etc. By this we mean that the Marian stamp with which we must live and carry out our apostolate is –undoubtedly– something we cannot fail to take into account when it comes to vocational ministry. Mary’s maternal presence must be present in awakening and guiding the vocations that God has destined for our Institute.

In the apostolic exhortation Vita Consecrata we read: “All vocations are inspired by the contemplation of Christ crucified; in it they have their origin”53. Now, at the foot of the Cross stands the Mother. She is the one who shows souls the shortest and easiest way to give themselves to Christ. That is why in our Institute every vocational apostolate is also essentially Marian.

Moreover, trusting recourse in prayer to the Mother of God, who keeps nothing for herself and busily cares for us, will always provide for the needs of the family and will send abundant vocations to our Institute in due time, as in fact she has already been doing, if we remain intimately united to her. Even more, if in our priestly ministry, after having guided a vocation toward God, we want it to persevere, St. Alphonsus Mary de Liguori recommends: “let the visit to the Most Blessed Sacrament and to Most Holy Mary be made without fail every day”54.

If at the same time these vocations are faithfully Marian, Our Lady will make of them “great saints… because only this singular and miraculous Virgin can accomplish, in union with the Holy Spirit, excellent and extraordinary things”55

Finally, if devotion to Our Lady is promoted “with grit” among Christian families, defined as the first seminary and irreplaceable reserve of vocations56, there will be fostered among the children the welcoming of the Lord’s call, their generous response and their joyful perseverance, and, on the part of the parents, a generous acceptance of God’s will.

We are convinced that the ever-growing number of vocations that God has been pleased to grant us in these last years is a special proof of the maternal and ever-solicitous presence of Our Lady of Luján in the history of the Institute. And to her we must always cling.

*****

Before concluding, a word of sincere appreciation to all the parents –living and deceased– of our religious for their generous self-giving and their unequaled contribution to the cause of Christ. St. John Paul II already said it: “the cultivation of the missionary vocation in their sons and daughters will be, on the part of parents, the best collaboration with the divine call”57. “Always be holily proud that the Lord has called someone of your family to follow Him closely. But continue each day to accompany him with prayer, so that his commitment of consecration may always be persevering and fervent”58. Know that your support through prayer, and the understanding, help and love you give us, are of incalculable value for our missionaries.

Now, to finish, we would like to include an extract from the book Un sueño pastoral by St. Manuel González concerning the topic we have been addressing. The saint says:

“Ordinarily, when this point [vocations] is spoken of, all eyes are fixed on the pastor, and the only point of agreement is how much he can foster vocations through his constant vigilance and zealous work of selecting, educating and preserving those who may become or already are seminarians.

That is most certainly true. But it is no less true that the secret of the effectiveness of the pastor’s action must not be a secret belonging exclusively to him, but to all priests.

Observe in general the qualities of those pastors who are skillful hunters and fosterers of vocations. You can sum them all up in this one: he is a priest conscious of his dignity. A worthy priest. There lies the secret.

He may not be an eloquent orator, nor a brilliant writer, nor a first-rate intellect, nor a prodigy of extraordinary things. It does not matter. It will be enough for him to live and present himself to his people as befits a priest. Meek and affable in his dealings. Respectful toward those above without servility. Approachable to those below without vulgarity. Always found when sought in his church, at the bedside of his sick, in the children’s school or in his house, and never in the club, nor in taverns, nor in the gatherings of the powerful or of the idle. Generous without squandering. Studious and fond of learning without arrogance, as well as ready to teach without envious rivalry. Immovable as a rock before tyrants. Soft as wax toward one who commands him in the name of God or asks him for charity. A child with children. Sick with the sick. Weak with the weak. Joyful with those who laugh and sad with those who weep. And, in sum, made all things to all men, to win all for Jesus Christ. This is the secret, and if you will let me say it, the great secret of attracting men to the priesthood.

Place anywhere a priest, whether a pastor or not, who conducts himself with this awareness of his dignity, and I pledge you my word –and neither God nor logic will let me fail– that not much time will pass before shoots have sprung up around that tree.

A worthy clergy is the best and most effective populator of a seminary. A worldly clergy, forgetful of its dignity, is condemned by God, by logic and by the moral sense, to the most shameful sterility. Hard is the punishment, truly, but as inevitable and just as it is hard”59.

To the Pure and Clean Conception of Luján our most humble gratitude for each and every one of the vocations she has been pleased to send to the Institute. May she, Virgin Mother of the Incarnate Word, help us “to be able to discover and guide so many vocations that we could fill all the good seminaries and novitiates of the whole world”60; may the young people who come into contact with us, drawn by the beauty and gentleness of this Most Holy Mother, be docile to the call of Christ; and may parents, contemplating and imitating the assiduous prayer of the Holy Family, be sure guides for their children toward spiritual and eternal goods.

Grant us, Holy Virgin of Luján, Mother of vocations61, to be able to see in our days too the wonders of the mysterious action of the Holy Spirit, who calls multitudes of souls to make them witnesses of the beatitudes.

Virgin of Luján, you are now and always all our hope.

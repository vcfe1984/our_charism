[Introduction] When I was thinking about what topic we could address in this conference, I came across a passage from Fr. Buela's book Juan Pablo Magno which says: “John Paul II has been and continues to be the inspiring source of the apostolic endeavor of our Congregation and he stands before our eyes as a singular example of holiness, inviting us to open wide the doors of our hearts to Jesus Christ.”

Within the framework of those words, and given that we are members of a single missionary religious family that recognizes St. John Paul II as its Spiritual Father, it seemed to me that it could be useful to consider just 10 points that St. John Paul II asked of women religious so that they might be good spouses of Christ. So that they might be “perfect-ten nuns” or “ten-point nuns”; that is where the title of this talk comes from.

For this work I have relied largely on the messages he addressed to the International Assembly of Superiors General in Rome during the 15 years from 1978 to 19931. In addition, of course, to the Proper Law and the writings of our Founder.

One clarification before beginning to treat these points: although the Holy Father was speaking to the superiors general, it is understood that the message was to be passed on to the other sisters in each congregation. With that same intention I speak to you, who have been called to make a valuable contribution to the government of the congregation, a contribution that in several cases can be decisive for overcoming difficulties, for achieving better results, for consolidating very good elements that are already present in the life of the Institute. What is more, you must realize the preponderant role you have at this moment as regards ‘safeguarding’ the charism and “fostering in all the sisters fidelity to the charism of the Founder”2 with all its patrimony.

That is why, as this talk unfolds, I think it is good for each of you to be thinking about the practical and concrete side of what is said here, so as to talk it over among yourselves afterwards if you like. Mind you, much of this is already being done, but as the Directory of Spirituality says, “ours is to live in the more and the above and beyond”3 and “what matters is to take a step, one step more; it is always the same step beginning again”4.

1. Radicality

The first of the elements that John Paul II insistently asked of women religious in order “to be perfect-ten nuns” is radicality.

Thus, for example, in 1989 he told them: “The vocation to consecrated life is characterized by the call to be disciples of the Lord in a very special way… This vocation leads to the total gift of oneself to serve the Lord, through the profession and practice of the evangelical counsels, which bind one for all of life”5. And on another occasion he told them: “Choices such as yours presuppose the gift of one's own life without reserve”6.

Fr. Buela, already in the first book of the Servidoras, has a whole chapter devoted to this theme. It's superb! I recommend it to you. There, speaking of evangelical radicalism, he says: “they are particularly unusual, hard and absolute demands… It is committing oneself to an unforeseeable path… it is to be decentered from oneself. […] It is the radicality of life of one who does not measure, but sells all that he has. […] It is that of the servant who will ask for nothing! He will keep on working, considering himself an unprofitable servant! who by his work merits no special reward”7. And Father concludes by saying that God has called us to live these radicalisms and that the vows we profess in this Religious Family are a sign that we want to live these radicalisms8.

I mention this to you because of the Servants (and what I say here for you I also say for the IVE) God does not ask just any effort, but the consecration of their whole existence to the service of the Incarnate Word. It is not a matter of something sporadic –reduced only to office hours and asking not to be disturbed on one's day off– but of a self-giving that fills everything, every day and every night. Hence the Proper Law affirms that if there is anything that should distinguish us, it is humble service and generous self-giving, in the gratuitous gift of oneself through a love to the end9.

And yet it may be that sometimes and in some cases –fallible men and women that we are– we do not notice that we are falling into the excuse of ‘false balance’, which is nothing other than the temptation of mediocrity, by which all magnanimity seems pride, all heroism seems extremism, all generosity seems exaggeration10. There may even be those who, under the pretext of various fallacies and with a thousand subtleties, hinder the sisters or draw them away from the true radicalism with which they must serve, imitating the self-emptying of Christ and his condition as a servant11.

Our dear Fr. Buela, with his particular genius and the special unction that comes from being the Founder, says this: Some do give, yes, but “they are stingy in giving and hard toward those who need help”12.

That is why John Paul II told the superiors: “The Church and –let us say it– the world too, need more than ever men and women who sacrifice everything to follow Christ like the Apostles”13. Sisters are needed who involve themselves fully in the cause of the Institute, which is none other than the cause of Christ.

All the Servants, those of now and those who will come in the future, must be persuaded that what is proper to them is to be “like another humanity of Christ”14; therefore, and here I continue quoting Fr. Buela: “what is required on our part is not a halfway self-giving; not a self-giving in some places or in some things only; not a self-giving at some moments; but a total and full self-giving, in whatever place and time it may be”15. Because what is proper to the Servants, in imitation of their Spouse, is “the oblation of self”16, and this both individually speaking and as an Institute.

Thus Fr. Buela told them with all the force of his fatherly affection: “none of so much selfishness, so much pettiness, so much calculation! We must go to total self-giving!”17.

Note that this radicality “drives to irresistible demands of sacrifice out of love for Christ, which at the same time is an act of integral faith and total self-giving, with the formal decision not to make pacts, not to compromise, not to capitulate, not to negotiate, not to concede, nor to make deals with the spirit of the world”18. And there will always be temptations, excuses for not doing ‘more’ than the indispensable, for giving but also being given to, for concentrating on ‘one's own work’, on one's own projects, and not paying attention to the works of the Institute or of the Religious Family as a whole…

St. John Bosco told his own very plainly: “Let us renounce individual selfishness; consequently, let us never seek private advantage or our own, but let us work with great zeal for the common good of the Congregation”19.

On this point I close with John Paul II: “the Church always needs radical and total gifts of self, new and courageous impulses”20. And the Institute of the Servants would not be faithful to its “essentially missionary”21 nature if it did not continue forming sisters and sending sisters who are convinced that self-giving to the mission involves their whole person and their whole life, all their energies and all their time.

2. Fidelity

Fidelity, especially to the charism of the Institute and, as a consequence of this, to the Founder, who is the primus receptor of this charism. In fact, it is what John Paul II asks of women religious most extensively, again and again.

His messages become like a supplicating litany of fidelity. I quote only two examples here:

“[Superiors]: you will fulfill your mission much better if you yourselves are imbued with a filial spirit… you are also daughters of your founders …you are daughters of your congregations, which have begotten you to religious life and daily sustain you in your personal sanctification… Maintain, make flourish again, consolidate the choices of the founders! In the pressing needs of today, your apostolic service must take concrete form according to the specific purpose of your institute…”22.

And he goes on to say: “Each institute must take care to maintain its own physiognomy, the specific character of its own reason for being, which has exercised an attraction, which has aroused vocations, particular attitudes, giving a public witness worthy of esteem. It is naive and presumptuous to believe, in the end, that each institute must be the same as all the others, practicing a general love of God and neighbor. Whoever thought so would forget an essential aspect of the mystical body: the heterogeneity of its constitution…”23. These words teach us that just as there is a harmful and dangerous self-referentiality, there is a positive and necessary self-referentiality in every religious Institute.

This is a subject on which all of you are well forewarned, I imagine. But it becomes extremely important given the special circumstances of our Religious Family. The Proper Law explicitly teaches and prescribes it in several of its documents. “Fidelity and love, we were saying, to the Founder and also to the Constitutions. They are the ‘model’ on which the religious must configure his life. That is why he must ‘know them, love them, and, above all, live them with generosity and fidelity’24”25, affirms one of the directories.

Even more: I would like to mention here what Fr. Pombo cites in the Prologue of the fourth book of the Servidoras about certain pontifical documents. He says there: “the document Mutuae Relationes n. 11 affirms that the very charism of the Founders is revealed as an experience of the Spirit, transmitted to their disciples to be lived, safeguarded, deepened and developed by them constantly in harmony with the Body of Christ in perennial growth. Therefore the charism must be safeguarded by all, [and] in the first place by the Institute itself, which is the depositary of the gift of the Spirit.

If the charism of the founder (Fr. Pombo goes on) endures through time in his sons and daughters, it is they who in a particular way must safeguard it through a life of total fidelity to that charism. That is why canon 578 imposes on religious: ‘All must faithfully observe the mind and designs of the founders, sanctioned by competent ecclesiastical authority, regarding the nature, end, spirit and character of each Institute, as well as its sound traditions, all of which constitute the patrimony of the Institute.’ This means that religious have a true obligation to know the founder, to come to understand and penetrate his mind and designs, which are nothing other than the concrete way in which the founder understands religious life must be lived in the Institute he founded, and through which concrete means and works the Institute must attain and accomplish the end for which it arose in the Church.

How does one come to know the mind and designs of a founder? Mainly through his writings. Now we have Father with us, but the day will come when not everyone will live at the same time as the founder. Even today not everyone has the opportunity to hear his own words. Without doubt, the writings constitute the most effective means a religious has to come to know the mind and designs of his founder.

We in particular have the grace of having received from our founder an immense and rich body of material, as a precious inheritance: the Constitutions of the Institute, the Directories, books, sermons, conferences, retreats, spiritual exercises, travel chronicles, audiovisual materials, etc. That is, these are works whose author is our founder, which gives them unequaled importance for us, inasmuch as they reflect, in some way, the very charism of the foundation”26. Thus far Fr. Pombo.

I would like to add that the life and fruitfulness of the Institute over time will depend on fidelity to the charism. And that “it is a grave obligation of the superiors, and therefore they must consider it of primary importance, to foster by all the means within their reach the fidelity of the religious to the charism of the Founder”27.

I would like here to refer to the publication of Fr. Buela's ‘complete works’. What I would like to emphasize at this gathering, knowing that I am before the superiors of the Institute and the general councilors, is that these works have to/must be known by all the sisters and by you yourselves. It is not enough to study the directories in the Novitiate. It is not enough with what is given at the Formation Meetings. I know of sisters, even superiors, who have barely read one chapter of any book of the Servidoras, who nevertheless read every author under the sun but know nothing of what the Founder says. There are also those who do not consult the priests of the IVE in order to learn or apply criteria according to our spirituality (or who consult and systematically do the opposite). And it cannot be that way.

The charism, the spirit of the congregation, our rule must be the air we breathe; the criterion of our action, the fundamental book for our spiritual education. St. Peter Julian Eymard told his own: “The law of our holiness, as well as the power of endurance and of action of the Congregation, consist in the observance of the rule… you cannot become saints without practicing the rule perfectly… All men are obliged to know and practice the gospel; but for us it is enough to know and practice our rule, which is our gospel… It is not your name that will give the institute nourishment and long life, but the practice of the rule”28. For which it is evidently necessary to know it, to transmit it in all its purity, to make it evident in our lives and in our works, to make it available to everyone in all the languages needed, and to educate the sisters to do the same. And you must be the leaders in that.

You learn so many things! How is it possible that you do not learn by heart numbers 30 and 31 of the Constitutions, where the charism of the Institute is spoken of? How is it that you cannot list the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism? [And you require 90 questions of the catechism kids…].

In the Conclusions of the last general chapter of the Servants one reads: “It is to the religious Superior that it belongs in a particular way, through the exercise of his government, to guarantee that religious life is lived according to the charism and the proper modality of the Institute”29. Always keep it in mind.

I end this point with what Don Bosco recommended to Don Giovanni Bonetti, who was spiritual director of the Daughters of Mary Help of Christians: “Throughout your life always preach: The Rules are not to be reformed, but practiced. Whoever goes looking for reforms deforms his way of living. Constantly recommend the exact observance of the Constitutions”30, and that is what I recommend today and always to all the Servants.

3. Promoting the union of spirits

This is another of the things that John Paul the Great asked of women religious. And it seems to me more than opportune to mention it here, right after the theme of the charism. Even more, it seems to me supremely necessary given the moment our Religious Family is going through.

As you well know, it falls to us priests of the IVE to work “with special diligence” so that the sisters of the Institute of the Servants may be informed by the genuine spirit of our family31. It is in this sense that the Conclusions of your last chapter acknowledged: “The unity between both Institutes comes through the charism. That is how we were founded; that is how the Will of God was seen by our Founder. Neither can do without the other without gravely injuring the charism. This implies responsibilities for both parties: responsibility on the part of the IVE to assist the Servants, and on the part of the Servants to let themselves be assisted”32. That on the one hand.

But also on the other hand, as the same document rightly adds later: “The pastoral work [of the SSVM] with the IVE brings the charism to fullness and therefore produces great fruits of fruitfulness… Hence we must not be stingy with our efforts to work well, in mutual collaboration. Fr. Buela has seen the spiritual unity between the two branches of the Religious Family as a source of vocations”33.

That is why the words of the Polish Pope apply to us: “[There must be] one and the same endeavor… through the diversity of works and activities… according to the charism of the founder”34. “This is not a simple disciplinary dependence, but a reality of faith. We must remind ourselves unceasingly that we are in the Church, intimately incorporated into her, ordered to her mission, inseparable from her life and holiness”35 . And we participate in the evangelizing mission of the Church as “a single family, united by the same faith, the same ends, the same mission, the same charism, the same character and the same spirit”36. Therefore, although John Paul II was speaking of unity within the same Institute, in which there are one's own superiors, it applies with special analogy to the unity of the Religious Family, which is so strong and intimate by reason of the foundational charism, notwithstanding the juridical independence of the two institutes.

That is why it is fitting, and very much so, not only to know the spirit and charism of the congregation thoroughly, not only to grow fond of the Religious Family, to watch over its interests –which are everyone's–, to defend it, but also to be consistent with all of that. That is: to give the Family the priority place that is due to it and to work eagerly under the same banner, knowing how to build in unity. It cannot happen –as in fact it does happen in some places– that the fathers and the sisters work with a different agenda, with almost no point of contact.

Look: it is true that “no priest is Superior of the sisters”37, but we are a Religious Family. Therefore we must function with esprit de corps, where the members are structured and organized, where what happens to one also affects the other.

Far be it from the Servants to be always centered on their own, not showing interest and effective enthusiasm for the works of the Religious Family. They must know how to be attentive to the works of the Institute, to anticipate being asked, to involve themselves fully and with the best resources, to safeguard them yourselves, to promote them and make them grow (in this regard one must be careful not to fall into an immature spirit of competition).

“Whatever it costs, we must be united,” said Blessed Giuseppe Allamano. Union is strength. That is why St. John Paul II also said: “If union in the religious family is a powerful evangelical witness, division among brothers, among sisters, is a stumbling block for evangelization”38.

That is why in the following paragraph the document of the Conclusions affirms: “For this reason it continues to be a priority to found in the places where the fathers of the IVE are, without ruling out the possibility of founding also in some other places considered important”39.

So although “our little Religious Family must never be turned in on itself, but must be open like the arms of Christ on the Cross,” it is necessary to plan the expansion of the Institute jointly, since this affects in no small measure the relationship of the Institute of the Servants with the IVE.

One of your sisters wrote me not long ago the following: “By founding in places where our priests are not, most of the time, obviously, the sisters end up doing spiritual direction with other priests who do not share our spirituality; they are left subject ‘to the good judgment’ of a local superior (who is not necessarily right), and they are exposed to the risk of weakening their fervor for what is our own, of getting used to another way of doing apostolate that is not ours, of operating with different criteria… and afterwards that is something difficult to correct. The danger is much greater when sisters who have just finished their formation are sent to that kind of foundation, because they think that is the way one works in the Institute, and afterwards, when they are sent to a parish or mission where the IVE is, they do not know how to work with our own, or they take so long to adapt…”.

In this sense, I would like to recommend the following to you –take it for what it is worth from the one it comes from–: 1. Consult before and during the time of planning the expansion; not when the decision has already been taken, the bishops have been visited, the means have been procured, no… consult beforehand. 2. In the planning, give priority to the works of the Religious Family. Do not let yourselves be tempted by more ‘showy’ or ‘better paid’ apostolates, or not so much, but far from the fathers; because the way the Servant Sisters do mission is “to carry out apostolates jointly with the Priests of the Incarnate Word”40. And when I say give priority I do not mean that you are the servants of the IVE (one must be careful not to formulate false dialectics, fruits of a worldly and horizontal view of religious life), because there has been no lack of someone who feels ‘used’ by the IVE, as if the end of the SSVM were different from that of the IVE. Giving priority means giving the works of the Religious Family the preponderance they deserve, investing the best resources, making available everything needed, like one who invests for heaven, not setting ridiculous conditions, not expecting anything in return… it is the Gospel thing: it is selling everything to buy the field where the pearl is buried.

4. Availability

“Absolute availability to serve the human family”41. The Pope constantly asked this of women religious: availability for service.

It is what Fr. Buela and the Proper Law also tirelessly ask of you. In Servidoras II one reads: it is necessary that the religious have “the interior, spiritual availability to do it as well as possible for the good of the brethren”42. And earlier Father also told them: “One of the characteristics of woman is to make herself a gift… This being a gift points to the service of God and of men –souls– and often implies sacrifice. To make oneself a gift is to give one's own life to the other, so that the other may decide what he wants to do with the giver. It is a total service accompanied by a high degree of forgetfulness of self, in order thus to be totally free in the service of the other”43.

What is proper to the Servants is to give with complete and long-suffering self-giving –without reluctance, rather with the attitude of the third class of persons–, without reserve, knowing how to immolate themselves in a real way each day out of love for Jesus Christ44, and to hold as the greatest joy in this life being able to give one's life for the Incarnate Word whom one serves. Did not Christ himself invite them to this when he said: whoever loses his life for my sake, that one will be saved45?

When the sisters are aware of this, they are generous in service and one can ask them anything because they will do it with a smile and well and responsibly; it is a pleasure to work with them. When that is not the case, when a thousand conditions are set… then the difficulties arise, the estrangements. You must be aware of the leading role your service can come to have, and you yourselves must be the first to stand out in that availability for service which in many cases and in so many of our missions turns out to be an irreplaceable support.

This reminds me of the example of St. Marianne Cope; I do not know if you know her. She was born in Germany but raised in New York. She was provincial of her congregation for two terms. And one day a letter came to her from Fr. Damien de Veuster asking her for sisters to work caring for the lepers of Molokai –she was still the provincial. She put the matter to a vote of the provincial council, where there were nine sisters; given the difficulties, 8 voted against, only one vote in favor, hers. Do you know what her response was then? She resigned her office as provincial and went herself to care for the lepers. Bear in mind that more than 50 religious communities had declined the request. In a letter she wrote: “I am not afraid of the disease. For me it will be the greatest joy to serve the banished lepers…”. Seeing the example of the superior, many sisters of her community offered to go, to the point that they had to choose only a few. Marianne Cope lived thirty years on a remote peninsula of the island of Molokai, voluntarily exiled with her patients. She never returned to the USA. Her own sisters said of her: “She was a woman who did not seek the limelight. Her motto was: ‘For God alone’.” In this regard she herself stated: “I do not seek a reward. I work for God and I do it gladly.”

You too, sisters, must hold high the magnificent title of Servants. Being aware that the life of every Servant consists in living in the more and the above and beyond46, without counting how much you give so that others may give to you; without calculating how much you are going to give to secure the profit; without weighing the sacrifices, the forgetfulness, the slights of those very people whom you serve; without measuring how much one does and how much the others do. Because all the wealth of a true Servant consists in giving herself to the Word47, and that is and must always be “her ‘particular style of sanctification and apostolate’”48. Which leads me to speak to you of the fifth thing John Paul II asked of women religious if they want “to be perfect-ten nuns”:

5. Primacy of God

Thus the Holy Father expressed himself: “Each religious must bear witness to the primacy of God and devote each day a sufficiently long time to being before the Lord, to tell him her love and, above all, to let herself be loved by him”49.

“No movement of religious life has any value unless it is simultaneously a movement toward the interior, toward the deep ‘center’ of your existence, where Christ has his dwelling. It is not what you do that matters most, but what you are as women consecrated to the Lord”50. “In this way, being in the fullness of one's strength or being weak, young or advanced in age, efficient or without any direct activity, does not matter: evangelization is real and deep to the extent that the life of Christ is reflected through one's personal life. The great evangelizers were eminently souls of prayer, interior souls: they have always known how to find time for prolonged contemplation”51.

You know well what the Proper Law teaches about this, for already from the first lines of our Constitutions we are told of the “primacy of the spiritual in all our thinking, feeling and acting”52; of preferring nothing to the love of Jesus Christ53, and so on throughout the length and breadth of all our documents.

A primacy that must be manifested –if we are to be consistent– in our way of living, of praying, of doing apostolate, of making decisions, of facing problems, of practicing the vows, etc. Which implies a confrontation, plain and simple, with the evil world, and demands on our part that we conduct ourselves with the spirit of a prince.

Fulton Sheen tells that one day a Jewish man came to see him to sell him hundreds of silver crucifixes. He asked him where he had gotten them, to which the man replied: ‘from nuns who no longer want to wear them’.

Why do I tell you this? Because this primacy of God in our lives is not a poetic metaphor. That God be the first to be served necessarily brings the Cross with it. A Cross to which the Servants must be firmly attached.

The nuns in the anecdote show by that gesture a betrothal no longer to the Crucified but to the world.

Open your eyes: Because many will come telling you –sometimes covertly and other times openly– that one must give “primacy to nature over grace, as all progressivism wants”54; that doing apostolate in parishes is no longer the done thing, because people nowadays move from one place to another constantly; and they end up shepherding themselves and hindering the mission. They will tell you that if you do not have vocations, not to worry, because otherwise you would be falling into an “inferiority complex”; that in the name of transparency (which is not that) you should denounce, speak out, that what matters is “to express what you feel”, and so they justify themselves in divulging what the common good requires to be kept in silence, and they feed daily on gossip, tittle-tattle and idle talk55. And there will be no lack of those who tell you to forget about the charism because in the end all charisms have the same evangelical richness, and then they complain and speak of the “winter of consecrated life”, spreading a gloomy and depressive mantle over consecrated life. Ah! But of course, the families are to blame because “they are no longer what they used to be”, because the founders are being investigated, because “it is a risk to admit to religious life young people who take on a commitment before mature age”, because they say contemplative life is finished because what matters nowadays is ‘to go to the periphery’, and they talk of closing monasteries as if they were closing a little kiosk… Some also argue that the sisters have to ‘be open-minded’, not firmly convinced of the truth, and they end up being hollow and therefore vulnerable to any ideology, and so they are incapable of offering positive solutions, or of convincing anyone, because they themselves are not convinced! And then, if we say that “all true renewal of religious life consists in a continual return, with the necessary adaptations, to the spirit bequeathed by the Founder and to the fulfillment of the Constitutions”56, they say we run the risk of falling into “Restorationism” (?!). And so we could go on with a long list of absurdities that in the end are summed up in the come down from the cross and we will believe57.

Realize that the primacy of God in our life, in our Institute, brings the Cross with it. And in that regard, far be it from us to be ashamed; we must cling to it even more!, in such a way that our proclamation of the truth, our way of sanctification and apostolate, may be in everything ‘non-negotiable’. That, sisters, demands –I repeat it to you again– not negotiating with the world.

From which it follows that formation in its four aspects is extremely important. Why is it important? I read you what Fr. Buela says: “Because with silly nuns we accomplish nothing, and that is the truth. Because silly nuns, what do they produce? Other silly ones, because operare sequitur esse; from silly ones, silly ones will come. They have to be ‘sharp’ nuns, who realize what times we are living in, what the attacks are, who the enemy is and where he is, what must be done”58. It has been said; what is lacking, if anything is lacking –as St. John of the Cross says– is to act59.

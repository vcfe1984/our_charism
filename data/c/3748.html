INTRODUCTION

  1. The Christian faithful, “incorporated into Christ through Baptism, are constituted as the People of God and, made sharers for this reason in their own way in the priestly, prophetic and royal function of Christ, are called, each according to his own condition, to exercise the mission which God entrusted to the Church to fulfill in the world”1. Thus the People of God has a fundamental unity, into which the diversity of its members is integrated: “By divine institution, among the faithful there are in the Church sacred ministers, who in law are also called clerics; the others are called laity”2.
  2. What place do religious occupy in this fundamental structure of the Church? Canon law explains it thus: “From both of these groups there are faithful who, through the profession of the evangelical counsels by means of vows or other sacred bonds recognized and sanctioned by the Church, are consecrated to God and contribute to the saving mission of the Church in the particular manner proper to them; their state, although it does not belong to the hierarchical structure of the Church, nevertheless belongs to her life and holiness”3.
  3. Thus “the state of consecrated life, by its nature, is neither clerical nor lay. A clerical institute is one which, by reason of the end or purpose intended by its founder or by legitimate tradition, is under the direction of clerics, assumes the exercise of Sacred Orders, and is recognized as such by the authority of the Church…”4.
  4. Nevertheless, although consecrated persons do not form a “third group” in the hierarchical structure of the Church, “consecrated persons who embrace the evangelical counsels receive a new and special consecration which, without being sacramental, commits them to embrace—in celibacy, poverty and obedience—the form of life practiced personally by Jesus and proposed by Him to the disciples. Although these various categories are manifestations of the one mystery of Christ, the laity have as their particular, though not exclusive, characteristic their secular character, pastors their ministerial character, and consecrated persons their special conformity to Christ, chaste, poor and obedient”5.

PART I

THE COADJUTOR BROTHERS

OF THE INSTITUTE OF THE INCARNATE WORD

Chapter 1: Dignity of religious life

Article 1: Religious life as a state of perfection

  1. The coadjutor brothers of our Institute have the quality of making manifest the excellence proper to religious life. Because they are religious who do not have priestly orders, religious consecration stands out in them.
  2. “In the Tradition of the Church, religious profession is considered to be a singular and fruitful deepening of baptismal consecration, inasmuch as, by means of it, the intimate union with Christ already begun in Baptism develops into the gift of a configuration more fully expressed and realized, through the profession of the evangelical counsels”6.
  3. Through the profession of the evangelical counsels, the religious brother is established in a state of perfection; that is, he binds himself by vow to seek Christian perfection, which consists in charity, according to the teaching of St. Thomas: “we consider the religious state as an exercise by which one tends toward the perfection of charity”7.
  4. St. Thomas teaches that the religious, because he is in a state of perfection, has the obligation to strive seriously for holiness; not to be holy at a given moment of his life, but indeed to become holy: “The state of perfection consists in nothing other than the perpetual obligation to devote oneself to the things that pertain to perfection”8.
  5. “In such a way that we are firmly resolved to attain holiness. A religious who is not willing to pass through the second and third conversion, or who does nothing concrete to achieve it, even if he is with us in body, does not belong to our spiritual family”9.
  6. Pope John Paul II speaks of consecrated life as a conforming adherence to Christ: “To consecrated life is entrusted the mission of pointing to the Son of God made man as the eschatological goal toward which all things tend, the splendor before which every other light pales, the infinite beauty which alone can fully satisfy the human heart. Therefore, in the consecrated life it is not only a matter of following Christ with one’s whole heart, loving Him more than father or mother, more than son or daughter (cf. Mt 10:37), as is asked of every disciple, but of living and expressing this by ‘conforming’ one’s whole existence to Christ, in an all-encompassing commitment which anticipates, to the extent possible in time and according to the various charisms, the eschatological perfection”10.
  7. Besides the pursuit of personal perfection through the charity that unites to God, consecrated life has a clear aspect of witness: “the first objective of consecrated life is to make visible the marvels that God works in the frail humanity of those who are called. More than in words, they bear witness to these marvels by the eloquent language of a transfigured existence, capable of amazing the world”11. The “particular mission of consecrated life is to keep alive in the baptized the awareness of the fundamental values of the Gospel, by bearing ‘magnificent and extraordinary witness that without the spirit of the beatitudes this world cannot be transformed and offered to God’”12.

Article 2: The dignity of the coadjutor brothers

  1. John Paul II treats excellently of the diversity and dignity of vocations in the Church13. “The equal dignity of all the members of the Church is the work of the Spirit; it is founded on Baptism and Confirmation and strengthened by the Eucharist. Yet the variety of forms is also the work of the Spirit. He constitutes the Church as an organic communion in the diversity of vocations, charisms and ministries”14, so that “in the unity of the Christian life the various vocations are like rays of the one light of Christ, ‘whose radiance shines on the face of the Church’”15.
  2. “As an expression of the holiness of the Church, an objective excellence must be recognized in consecrated life, which reflects Christ’s own way of living. Precisely for this reason, it is an especially rich manifestation of Gospel values and a more complete realization of the Church’s end, which is the sanctification of humanity”16.
  3. We take for our coadjutor brothers the words of St. John Paul II referring to congregations of religious brothers, whose consecration “has a value of its own, independently of the sacred ministry, both for the person himself and for the Church”17.
  4. “Following the teaching of the Second Vatican Council, the Synod expressed great esteem for this kind of consecrated life, in which religious brothers carry out many valuable services within and outside the community, thus sharing in the mission of proclaiming the Gospel and bearing witness to it with charity in everyday life. Indeed, some of these services can be considered ecclesial ministries entrusted by legitimate authority. This requires an appropriate and integral formation: human, spiritual, theological, pastoral and professional”18.
  5. We desire that all the members of our Religious Family also have great esteem for the coadjutor brothers and for their consecration, which distinguishes and ennobles them in their mission within the Church and the Institute.

Chapter 2: Lay religious in our Institute

Article 1: Situation of the brothers in the Institute

  1. The Code of Canon Law recognizes the distinction between clerical institutes and lay institutes19. Our Institute is clerical, but, as the Constitutions note, “without excluding the possibility that religious who are not priests be harmoniously integrated into our family”20. Therefore, among its members there are those who are not clerics but lay religious, and thus full members of the Institute: as members of the People of God they are lay, but by virtue of their consecration they are religious. The Institute of the Incarnate Word counts religious brothers among its members. They do not form a separate branch, and they are governed by the same Constitutions and by this Directory in what is specific to them21.
  2. “Different is the vocation of brothers in those institutes which are called ‘clerical’ because, according to the design of the founder or by legitimate tradition, they provide for the exercise of Sacred Orders, are governed by clerics and as such are recognized by the authority of the Church. In these institutes the sacred ministry is an integral part of the charism and determines its specific nature, purpose and spirit. The presence of brothers represents a differentiated participation in the mission of the institute, with services rendered in collaboration with those who exercise the priestly ministry, whether within the community or in the apostolic works”22.
  3. Therefore, the coadjutor brothers of our Institute are religious and, as such, share in the essential elements of the religious state: stability through vows and fraternal life in common.
  4. Stability through the vows: the profession of the evangelical counsels as a means of attaining Christian perfection is essential to the religious state. This profession is made by means of public vows, which give religious life the firmness of a state. Public vows are those accepted by a legitimate Superior in the name of the Church.
  5. Fraternal life in common: the whole of life is practiced in common with one’s brothers in religion, living under the same roof and subject to a common discipline under the same Superior. “For consecrated persons, who have become one heart and one soul (Acts 4:32) through the gift of the Holy Spirit poured into their hearts23, it becomes an inner requirement to hold everything in common: material goods and spiritual experiences, talents and inspirations, apostolic ideals and services of charity”24.
  6. “Living in a special way this aspect of life that is at once Christian and consecrated, ‘religious brothers’ effectively remind religious priests themselves of the fundamental dimension of brotherhood in Christ, which they are to live among themselves and with every man and woman, proclaiming to all the word of the Lord: And you are all brothers (Mt 23:8)”25.

Article 2: Equal dignity of the religious of the Institute

  1. All religious, clerics and lay, enjoy a fundamental equality. The brothers do not constitute a second rank; rather, they are true religious, bound to the same perfection and to exercise the identical apostolate which forms the essence of our Institute. Therefore, the one religious consecration and the identical apostolic mission are the foundation of the equality between the brother and the priest; where this equality does not exist, there cannot even be an authentic community life.
  2. The desire of the Church is that in mixed institutes—made up of religious priests and lay religious—“all religious be recognized as having equal rights and obligations, except those deriving from Sacred Orders”26. The dignity is equal, although the tasks will be different.
  3. All the members of the Institute contribute their service to the community. In turn, the priests render a differentiated service through the pastoral charity that flows from the sacrament of Holy Orders. In the same way, the coadjutor brothers serve the community in a great variety of ministries, especially those most in keeping with their lay vocation of sanctifying temporal realities. The diversity of tasks carried out by each member of the Institute thus corresponds to the vocation of each. “The Spirit Himself, moreover, far from separating from human history the persons whom the Father has called, places them at the service of their brothers and sisters according to the modalities proper to their state of life, and directs them to carry out particular tasks, in accordance with the needs of the Church and of the world, by means of the particular charisms of each Institute”27.
  4. It is necessary that the members of our Institute understand the need to foster vocations of coadjutor brothers, since their presence is a good for the Religious Family. Let us remember that the Constitutions name them among the “key pieces”28.

Article 3: Characteristics of the vocation of the coadjutor brother

  1. The coadjutor brother is a religious by his consecration and a layman by his condition. His vocation, therefore, has features that are proper to his lay condition, but with the demands his consecration implies. As such, the lay brother (layman):
  2. Devotes himself to temporal things in order to order them according to God, but without forgetting that his consecration impels him to seek the Kingdom of God. “Indeed, the vocation of consecrated persons to seek first the Kingdom of God is, above all, a call to complete conversion, in the renunciation of self in order to live fully in the Lord, so that God may be all in all”29. In many spheres he will be able to play an irreplaceable role: “There are things that neither priests nor clerics can do, and you will do them,” Don Bosco used to say30.
  3. The baptismal priesthood is proper to the layman, and it also includes the threefold function (munus): the cultic function, which for the religious brother is expressed fundamentally in his consecration, “by which the religious is something sacred, destined for divine worship, the property of God”31. He carries out the prophetic function through the witness of a life totally given to God and to his brothers… “Consecrated persons make visible, in their consecration and total self-giving, the loving and saving presence of Christ”32. Moreover, consecrated life bears “prophetic witness to the primacy of God and of Gospel values in the Christian life”33. The lay brother carries out the royal function in the imitation of Christ, obedient unto death34. “Christ has communicated to His disciples the gift of royal freedom, ‘so that they might conquer in themselves, by self-denial and a holy life, the reign of sin’: ‘He who subjects his own body and governs his soul, without letting himself be carried away by the passions, is master of himself: he can be called a king because he is able to rule his own person; he is free and independent and does not let himself be captured by a culpable slavery’”35.
  4. In his condition as a religious of our Institute, the lay brother must show the following qualities:

[Aspiration to holiness]

By the very fact of being members of the Church they have this most lofty vocation: “all in the Church, whether they belong to the hierarchy or to the flock, are called to holiness, according to the saying of the Apostle: For this is the will of God, your sanctification (1 Thess 4:3; Eph 1:4)”36. “All the children of the Church, called by the Father to ‘listen’ to Christ, must feel a profound need for conversion and holiness. But, as was emphasized in the Synod, this need concerns consecrated life in the first place”37. This special demand is manifested in a singular way in religious brothers, for it is their proper distinguishing mark in the Church and in the Institute. They bear witness before the other members that the call to holiness must receive a radical response: “consecrated life continually stirs up in the consciousness of the People of God the need to respond with holiness of life to the love of God poured into hearts by the Holy Spirit”38. “Throughout the history of the Church, consecrated life has been a living presence of this action of the Spirit, as a privileged space of absolute love for God and neighbor, a witness to the divine plan of making all humanity, within the civilization of love, the great family of the children of God”39.

[Observance of their vows]

  1. The profession of the evangelical counsels as a means of attaining Christian perfection is something essential to the religious state as a state of perfection. The vows constitute the best means of professing the evangelical counsels and give religious life the firmness of a state.
  2. St. Thomas Aquinas teaches how the vows dispose the religious to strive for the perfection of charity40: “it is necessary that the religious detach himself from everything that could prevent his love from being directed wholly to God, in which the perfection of charity consists. The obstacles can be three: love of external goods, which is destroyed by the vow of poverty; the desire for sensible pleasures, which is destroyed by the vow of chastity; and finally, the disorder of the human will, which is remedied by the vow of obedience. In this way we consider the religious state inasmuch as it is an exercise by which one tends toward the perfection of charity”41.
  3. “All religious must not only fulfill fully and faithfully the vows they have made, but also order their life in conformity with the Rules and Constitutions of their own religious institute, and in this way strive for the perfection of their state”42.

[Fidelity to the founding charism]

  1. Consecrated life is a following of Christ; such following is carried out in a particular institute, according to its own charism of religious life. Growth in holiness requires, above all, “fidelity to the founding charism and to the consequent spiritual patrimony of each institute. Precisely in this fidelity to the inspiration of the founders and foundresses, a gift of the Holy Spirit, the essential elements of consecrated life are more easily discovered and more fervently revived”43. In every founding charism there is “a profound concern to be configured to Christ, bearing witness to some aspect of His mystery”44. It is a “specific aspect called to be embodied and developed in the most genuine tradition of each institute, according to the Rules, Constitutions or Statutes”45.
  2. “A growing attention to the Rule will offer consecrated persons a sure criterion for seeking the appropriate forms of witness capable of responding to the demands of the moment without departing from the original inspiration”46.

PART II

THE PRAYER LIFE OF THE COADJUTOR BROTHER

  1. The spirituality of our coadjutor brothers will be the same as that which the Constitutions and the Directory of Spirituality establish for all the members of the Institute47.
  2. “The spiritual life, therefore, must occupy first place in the program of families of consecrated life, in such a way that each institute and each community may appear as schools of authentic evangelical spirituality. On this priority choice, developed in personal and community commitment, depend apostolic fruitfulness, generosity in love for the poor, and vocational attractiveness itself before the new generations. What can move the people of our time, who also thirst for absolute values, is precisely the spiritual quality of consecrated life, which is thus transformed into a fascinating witness”48.
  3. “The contemplation of divine things and assiduous union with God in prayer must be the first and principal duty of all religious. As far as possible, the members will take part daily in the Eucharistic Sacrifice, receive the Most Holy Body of Christ, and adore the Lord present in the Sacrament. They will devote time to the reading of Sacred Scripture and to mental prayer, will celebrate the Liturgy of the Hours daily according to the prescriptions of proper law, and will perform other exercises of piety. They will render special veneration, also through the praying of the Holy Rosary, to the Virgin Mother of God, model and protector of all consecrated life, and will faithfully observe the annual periods of spiritual retreat”49.
  4. The forms of prayer mentioned can be reduced to two: liturgical prayer and private prayer.

Chapter 1: Liturgical prayer

  1. Liturgical prayer occupies first place, since it is the official prayer of the Church. Made in intimate union with Christ, Head of the Church, and in union with the whole Mystical Body, it has an incalculable sanctifying efficacy: “every liturgical celebration, because it is a work of Christ the Priest and of His Body, which is the Church, is a sacred action surpassing all others; no other action of the Church can equal its efficacy by the same title and to the same degree”50. “The fundamental means for effectively nourishing communion with the Lord is without doubt the sacred liturgy, especially the Eucharistic celebration and the Liturgy of the Hours”51.

Article 1: The Eucharist in the life of the coadjutor brother

  1. The coadjutor brother must love “above all the Eucharist, which ‘contains the entire spiritual wealth of the Church, that is, Christ Himself, our Passover and Bread of Life, who gives life to men,’ the heart of ecclesial life and also of consecrated life. How could one who has been called to choose Christ as the sole meaning of his life in the profession of the evangelical counsels not desire to establish with Him an ever more intimate communion through daily participation in the sacrament that makes Him present, in the sacrifice that actualizes His gift of love on Golgotha, in the banquet that nourishes and sustains the pilgrim People of God? By its nature the Eucharist is at the center of consecrated life, both personal and communal. It is daily viaticum and the source of the spirituality of each institute. In it each consecrated person is called to live the Paschal Mystery of Christ, uniting himself to Him in the offering of his own life to the Father through the Spirit… In the celebration of the mystery of the Body and Blood of the Lord, the unity and charity of those who have consecrated their existence to God are strengthened and increased”52.
  2. Every day one must take part in the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass, in which the Sacrifice of Calvary is renewed and also our own offering as a living sacrifice, holy, pleasing to God (Rom 12:1) in Christ. Religious profession is an offering made to God and reaches its fullness in profound union with the Sacrifice of Christ53. The Eucharist obtains for us, with the greatest efficacy, sanctification in Christ.

Article 2: The Divine Office or Liturgy of the Hours

  1. “The Church, exercising the priestly function of Christ, celebrates the Liturgy of the Hours, by which, hearing God who speaks to His people and recalling the mystery of salvation, she praises Him unceasingly with song and prayer, while at the same time interceding for the salvation of the whole world”54. “Together with the Eucharist, and in intimate relation with it, the Liturgy of the Hours, celebrated in community or individually according to the nature of each institute and in union with the prayer of the Church, expresses the vocation to praise and intercession proper to consecrated persons”55.
  2. “In the liturgy the Incarnate Word prays for us—He is our Priest—, in us—He is our Head—, to Him we pray—He is our God—. We must recognize our voice in Him and His voice in us. We must pray with Him, since He prays with us; pray in Him, since He prays in us”56.
  3. The coadjutor brothers will join in the community celebration of the Divine Office. In case they cannot take part in the community prayers, they will pray Lauds and Vespers individually.

Article 3: The sacrament of Penance

  1. The Constitutions indicate for our religious: “Moreover, they must discover the beauty and joy of the sacrament of Penance, in a world that has lost the sense of sin and of divine mercy”57.
  2. And we read in Vita Consecrata: “The effort of continual conversion and necessary purification, which consecrated persons carry out through the sacrament of Reconciliation, is also intimately linked to the Eucharist. Through frequent encounter with the mercy of God, they renew and purify their hearts, while at the same time, by humbly acknowledging their sins, they make their relationship with Him transparent. The joyful experience of sacramental forgiveness, on the journey shared with their brothers and sisters, makes the heart docile and encourages the commitment to a growing fidelity”58.

Chapter 2: Personal prayer

  1. It can be affirmed that private prayer has its roots in human nature itself, for it belongs to the way of acting by which man expresses himself in relation to God, to himself and to others in experiences of profound intimacy. It is an inalienable attitude of man. That is why prayer is for every Christian a duty and an irreplaceable need. A duty and need that binds the religious much more than anyone else, since he consecrates his life to the “professional” exercise of the virtue of religion, as St. Thomas teaches: “Everything by which we show reverence to God pertains to religion. Such is the case with prayer, for by it man subjects himself to God and confesses the need he has of Him, as the author of all goods. It is therefore evident that prayer is an act proper to the virtue of religion”59.
  2. The life of prayer is a life of friendship with God, since friendship is “the perfection of charity”60. It can be affirmed that, ordinarily, “our advancement in divine love depends practically on our life of prayer”61.

Article 1: Eucharistic adoration

  1. After participation in the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass, the most eagerly awaited moment of the day must be that of holy adoration. The coadjutor brother, as far as possible, will devote one hour daily to Eucharistic adoration, a privileged place for his exercise of personal meditation62.
  2. “The worship given to the Eucharist outside of Mass is of inestimable value in the life of the Church. This worship is closely linked to the celebration of the Eucharistic Sacrifice. The presence of Christ under the sacred species reserved after Mass—a presence which lasts as long as the species of bread and wine remain—derives from the celebration of the Sacrifice and is directed toward sacramental and spiritual communion”63.
  3. “It is beautiful to be with Him and, reclining on His breast like the beloved disciple, to feel the infinite love of His heart. If Christianity in our time must be distinguished above all by the ‘art of prayer,’ how can we not feel a renewed need to spend long periods in spiritual conversation, in silent adoration, in an attitude of love, before Christ present in the Most Holy Sacrament? How many times, my dear brothers and sisters, have I had this experience, and found in it strength, consolation and support!”64.

Article 2: Lectio divina

  1. John Paul II also recommends to religious the practice of lectio divina, or meditative reading of Sacred Scripture, which belongs to the spiritual patrimony of the Church and of consecrated life in particular, and which can greatly help the coadjutor brothers as nourishment for their souls.
  2. “The Word of God is the first source of all Christian spirituality. It nourishes a personal relationship with the living God and with His saving and sanctifying will. For this reason lectio divina has been held in the highest esteem since the birth of institutes of consecrated life… Thanks to it, the Word of God reaches into life, upon which it projects the light of wisdom which is a gift of the Spirit… It will therefore be of great help to consecrated persons to meditate assiduously on the Gospel texts and the other New Testament writings, which illustrate the words and examples of Christ and of the Virgin Mary, and the apostolica vivendi forma (way of life after the example of the apostles)”65.
  3. Men and women of prayer have accomplished great works in the Church; through their “assiduous contact with the Word of God they have obtained the light needed for the personal and community discernment which has helped them to seek the ways of the Lord in the signs of the times. They have thus acquired a kind of supernatural instinct which has made it possible for them, instead of yielding to the mentality of the world, to renew their own minds, so as to be able to discern the will of God, what is good, what is pleasing to Him, what is perfect (Rom 12:2)”66.

Article 3: Prayer and life

  1. But the prayer of the religious is not limited to the times of the day set aside for personal prayer; rather, it must continue throughout the whole day and, so to speak, must take on the form that obedience impresses on it at each moment. If from Holy Mass he must go to the kitchen, his union with God will continue there; if he goes to the porter’s lodge, to the office, to class, to the sacristy, wherever it may be, the “sacrament of the present moment”67 will place him in union with Christ in those occupations. Since he is a consecrated person, carrying out his duties through holy obedience, everything he does will be directly ordered and offered to God, consecrated to Him.
  2. All the activities of the day must be done in intimate union with Jesus and solely out of love for Him and for neighbor; precisely for this reason they must be the fruit of personal prayer, of meditation.
  3. In short, this life must be a continual sighing for Jesus, with a heart always melting in love for Him68. One must always strive for a greater knowledge and love of God, as long as this life lasts: “let it be the object of our meditation and of our words; let it be the object of our love, the theme of our conversations; let our soul hunger and thirst for this happiness, let our whole being desire it”69.

Article 4: Silence and recollection

  1. Silence and recollection help the life of prayer. We must learn from Jesus Himself, who devoted thirty years of His life to recollection and silence, in order to learn to love and to live the virtues of self-emptying, humility, and remaining hidden from the sight of men. “The call to holiness is welcomed and can be cultivated only in the silence of adoration before the infinite transcendence of God: ‘We must confess that we all need this silence filled with adored presence’”70.
  2. The coadjutor brothers will have their Spiritual Exercises every year, since “it is altogether praiseworthy to make annual Exercises of eight days”71, in which, in the silence and recollection of prayer, they will strive to renew their own consecration to God and try to search out God’s will for their lives. Likewise it is desirable that each month they take a day of retreat. What is recommended is “great fidelity to liturgical and personal prayer, to the times devoted to mental prayer and contemplation, to Eucharistic adoration, monthly retreats and the Spiritual Exercises”72.

Article 4: Asceticism and spiritual combat

  1. The brothers must not forget that in the Christian life whoever does not advance falls back; that is why it is “necessary also to keep in mind the ascetical means typical of the spiritual tradition of the Church and of one’s own institute. They have been and still are a powerful help for an authentic path of holiness. Asceticism, by helping to master and correct the tendencies of human nature wounded by sin, is truly indispensable for the consecrated person to remain faithful to his own vocation and to follow Jesus on the way of the cross”73.
  2. “The path that leads to holiness thus entails the acceptance of spiritual combat. This is a demanding reality to which the necessary attention is not always given today. Tradition has often seen spiritual combat represented in Jacob’s wrestling with the mystery of God, which he confronts in order to gain access to His blessing and to the vision of Him (cf. Gen 32:23-31). In this narrative from the beginnings of biblical history, consecrated persons can see the symbol of the ascetical effort needed to enlarge the heart and open it to welcome the Lord and their brothers and sisters”74.
  3. “It is necessary to awaken in each of the faithful a true longing for holiness, a strong desire for conversion and personal renewal in a climate of ever more intense prayer and of solidarity in welcoming one’s neighbor, especially the most needy”75.

PART III

COMMUNITY LIFE

Chapter 1: Community life, essential to religious life76

  1. Community life is something essential in the religious state77. It consists in living in common with one’s brothers in religion, living under the same roof, subject to a common discipline under the same Superior, and receiving from a common fund everything necessary for food and other needs. Rooted and grounded in charity, community life must be an example of universal reconciliation in Christ78.
  2. “The Church is essentially a mystery of communion, ‘a people made one with the unity of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit.’ Fraternal life seeks to reflect the depth and richness of this mystery, taking shape as a human space inhabited by the Trinity, which thus pours into history the gifts of communion proper to the three divine Persons… Consecrated life certainly has the merit of having contributed effectively to keeping alive in the Church the demand for fraternity as a confession of the Trinity. By constantly promoting fraternal love in the form of common life, consecrated life shows that participation in Trinitarian communion can transform human relationships, creating a new type of solidarity”79.
  3. “Fraternal life plays a fundamental role in the spiritual journey of consecrated persons, both for their constant renewal and for the fulfillment of their mission in the world. This follows from the theological motivations on which it is founded, and experience itself amply confirms it. I therefore exhort consecrated men and women to cultivate it with perseverance, following the example of the first Christians of Jerusalem, who were assiduous in listening to the teaching of the Apostles, in common prayer, in taking part in the Eucharist, and in sharing the goods of nature and of grace (cf. Acts 2:42-47). Above all I exhort religious men and women and members of societies of apostolic life to live mutual love without reserve and to express it in the way best suited to the nature of their own institute, so that each community may show itself to be a luminous sign of the new Jerusalem, the dwelling of God with men (Rev 21:3)”80.
  4. In short, community life is to live the Kingdom of Jesus established on earth, which is justice, joy and peace in the Holy Spirit81. All this is identified with holiness, and if holiness is lacking, disintegration cannot be avoided82.

Article 1: Dispositions for community life

[Fraternal charity]

  1. In order to live community life in its fullness, and thus make of it a mutual help for all in the fulfillment of their own vocation83, it is necessary to live fraternal charity: holding others as more worthy; patiently bearing the weaknesses of our brothers; each one seeking the good of others before his own; putting into practice a sincere fraternal love; living always in the fear and love of God; loving the Superior with sincere and humble charity; preferring absolutely nothing to Christ84.
  2. “Love led Christ to the gift of Himself, even to the supreme sacrifice of the cross. In a similar way, among His disciples there is no true unity without this unconditional mutual love, which demands readiness for service without reserve, promptness to welcome the other as he is without judging him (cf. Mt 7:1-2), the ability to forgive even seventy times seven (Mt 18:22). For consecrated persons, who have become one heart and one soul (Acts 4:32) through the gift of the Holy Spirit poured into their hearts, it becomes an inner requirement to hold everything in common: material goods and spiritual experiences, talents and inspirations, apostolic ideals and services of charity”85.
  3. Religious, brothers in Christ, “living in a special way this aspect of life that is at once Christian and consecrated… effectively remind religious priests themselves of the fundamental dimension of brotherhood in Christ, which they are to live among themselves and with every man and woman, proclaiming to all the word of the Lord: And you are all brothers (Mt 23:8)”86.

[Attitude of service]

  1. The practice of charity is manifested in the attitude of service to others: “In the gesture of washing the feet of His disciples, Jesus reveals the depth of God’s love for man: in Him, God Himself places Himself at the service of men! At the same time He reveals the meaning of consecrated life, which is a life of self-giving love, of concrete and generous service. Following in the footsteps of the Son of Man, who came not to be served, but to serve (Mt 20:28), consecrated life, at least in the best periods of its long history, has been characterized by this ‘washing of feet,’ that is, by service, especially to the poorest and most needy”87. In these words of the Lord the brother finds the whole theological foundation of his activity as a servant88.
  2. Incarnate Wisdom Himself came to serve us. Hence it is not possible to dedicate our life to anything greater, nobler and more beautiful than to serve, that is, to love God in Himself and in the person of others.

Chapter 2: Work and community life

  1. Service to the brothers is shown in a singular way in work. Whatever office or occupation the religious may have must be a service, whether the most dazzling tasks or the most obscure; that does not matter. What is important is to make of each task an act of love for God and for neighbor for His sake, to serve Jesus in His members, with prompt and total availability for anything.
  2. For the concrete functioning of a community, the work of all its members is needed. Work is a divine law from the beginning of creation: fill the earth and subdue it (Gen 1:28). There is no man exempt from this duty. The religious, like any Christian, must work virtuously: with free will and a supernatural sense. “In the word of divine Revelation there is inscribed very deeply this fundamental truth, that man, created in the image of God, shares by his work in the activity of the Creator and, within the measure of his own capacities, in a certain sense continues to develop that activity and completes it, advancing ever further in the discovery of the resources and values contained in the whole of creation”89.
  3. After sin, work became a painful punishment: …cursed be the ground because of you: with toil you shall draw food from it all the days of your life (Gen 3:17). “Yet, in spite of… [this] toil—and perhaps, in a certain sense, because of it—work is a good thing for man… And it is not only a good thing in the sense of ‘useful’ or ‘something to enjoy,’ but a good thing for man, a good that expresses this dignity and increases it… because through work man not only transforms nature, adapting it to his own needs, but also achieves fulfillment as a human being and indeed, in a certain sense, ‘becomes more a human being’”90.
  4. But it is not only a good; it is also a duty. The “teaching of Christ about work, based on the example of His own life during the years in Nazareth, finds a particularly lively echo in the teaching of the Apostle Paul. He boasted of working at his trade (he was probably a tentmaker)91, and thanks to this he was able, even as an apostle, to earn his own bread92. With toil and labor we worked night and day, so as not to be a burden to any of you (2 Thess 3:8). Hence his instructions on the subject of work, which have the character of exhortation and command: Such persons… we command and exhort in the Lord Jesus Christ to work quietly and eat their own bread, he writes to the Thessalonians (2 Thess 3:12). In fact, noting that some among you are living in idleness, doing nothing (2 Thess 3:11), the Apostle, in the same context, does not hesitate to say: If anyone will not work, let him not eat (2 Thess 3:10). In another passage, on the contrary, he encourages: Whatever you do, do it from the heart, as obeying the Lord and not men, knowing that from the Lord you will receive the inheritance as your reward (Col 3:23-24)”93.
  5. It is possible to become holy through work, but it must be done virtuously, that is, in supernatural obedience to the will of God, which for the religious is manifested in the will of the Superior.
  6. Through work the religious brother brings great help to the community, especially if he knows how to find in work a source of joy. In work he can verify the progress of his abilities, the development of his personality and the perfecting of his physical and spiritual qualities. Joy, moreover, comes from service, according to the saying of our Lord quoted by St. Paul: There is more happiness in giving than in receiving (Acts 20:35).
  7. 79. The religious brother must give an example of diligence in work, but he must take care not to confuse work with activism. Diligence is an ordered, prudent and measured activity; activism is agitation and cannot be a source of sanctification. On the other hand, the fulfillment of one’s own office by virtue of obedience is equivalent to the fulfillment of God’s will for each one.

Chapter 3: Manual work and intellectual work

  1. Work can be intellectual or manual94. Both are work; they differ only by reason of the matter on which they are exercised: “since work in its subjective aspect is always a personal action, an actus personae, it necessarily follows that the whole person, body and spirit, participates in it, whether it is manual or intellectual work”95. Therefore, two temptations must be avoided:

– that those who devote themselves to intellectual work, clerics or brothers, look down on those who work manually;

– that those who work manually consider themselves the only ones who work.

  1. Work, especially manual work, has many advantages: it ensures subsistence, eliminates idleness, mortifies the body by restraining evil desires, and makes almsgiving possible96. The manual work carried out by the brother, while being complemented by intellectual work, is a necessary good for the community and meritorious for his spiritual life.
  2. Intellectual work is also useful and necessary for the community and a source of personal sanctification. The brothers too can carry out this type of work, joining in the drive to evangelize the culture.
  3. In the Institute the work of the coadjutor brother will be of great help to the community. The brother will allow the priest to take charge of what is specific to his ministry, while he devotes himself to some functions more in keeping with the lay state, such as, for example: finances, the sacristy, administration, care of the facilities, etc.

PART IV

APOSTOLATE OF THE COADJUTOR BROTHERS

Chapter 1: The apostolate of religious

  1. “Apostolic and charitable action… belongs to the very nature of religious life, since such action is a holy ministry and a work of charity proper to them, which the Church has entrusted to them and which they are to carry out in her name. For this reason, the whole religious life of their members must be imbued with an apostolic spirit, and all their apostolic activity must, in turn, be informed by a religious spirit”97. For this “it is necessary that their apostolic action proceed from intimate union with Him. In this way charity toward God and toward neighbor is itself fostered”98.
  2. The “apostolate of all religious consists first of all in the witness of their consecrated life, which they are to foster through prayer and penance”99. The duty of religious “to cooperate diligently in the building up and increase of the whole Mystical Body of Christ… they must pursue above all through prayer, works of penance and the example of their life”100.
  3. St. John Paul II, referring to religious of apostolic life, says that “it is a matter of fostering the integration of interiority and activity. In fact, their first duty is to be with Christ. A constant danger for those who labor for the Gospel is to become so involved in their own activity for the Lord that they forget the Lord of all activity”101.
  4. The very presence of religious in the world constitutes an apostolate that proves fundamental for the Church: “What would become of the world if it were not for religious?”102. “Without this concrete sign, the charity that animates the Church would risk growing cold, the saving paradox of the Gospel would risk losing its penetrating power, and the ‘salt’ of faith would risk dissolving in a secularized world”103. “The life of the Church and society itself need persons capable of giving themselves totally to God and to others for the love of God”104.

Chapter 2: The apostolate exercised by the coadjutor brothers

  1. Our Constitutions state in general terms the apostolate carried out by the brothers: “they will devote themselves to the service of the houses and to rendering spiritual services that do not require the sacramental grace of Sacred Orders”105. John Paul II affirms that for clerical institutes such as ours, in which “the sacred ministry is an integral part of the charism and determines its specific nature, purpose and spirit”106, the “presence of brothers represents a differentiated participation in the mission of the institute, with services rendered in collaboration with those who exercise the priestly ministry, whether within the community or in the apostolic works”107.

Article 1: Apostolate during the stage of initial formation

  1. For all religious in the stages of initial formation, the time and manner of apostolate are determined. The brothers will render the same spiritual services that the seminarians carry out and that do not require the sacrament of Orders: the permanent mission in the houses or parishes to which they are sent, popular missions, children’s camps, etc.
  2. When they have finished the novitiate, they may render service in whichever religious house is deemed most suitable. However, it must be kept in mind that until perpetual profession their initial formation continues; therefore, the local Superiors of those houses must take special care that adequate time and means for this fundamental formation are not lacking.

Article 2: Apostolate of the perpetually professed brothers

  1. For coadjutor brothers who have already made perpetual profession and live in houses of formation, the local Superior will determine the apostolate the brothers are to have.
  2. In the houses of charity they may devote themselves to the works of mercy with the sick and disabled, children and the elderly, knowing that the suffering members of the Church will always be the privileged ones, and that they are the suffering Christ Himself who asks for our charity: truly I say to you, whatever you did to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to Me (Mt 25:40). “The search for divine beauty moves consecrated persons to care for the divine image disfigured in the faces of so many brothers and sisters… Consecrated life thus shows, with the eloquence of deeds, that divine charity is the foundation and stimulus of freely given and active love”108. St. Vincent de Paul directed the Daughters of Charity “to give themselves to God in order to love our Lord and serve Him materially and spiritually in the person of the poor”109.
  3. Our Constitutions point out that, through the apostolate of charity, an indispensable work is carried out in relation to the evangelization of culture. “Otherwise, ‘the civilization of love’ will not be attained”110.
  4. It should be borne in mind that in many cases it will be fitting for the service the coadjutor brothers render in the houses of charity to alternate with periods of work and apostolate in other houses of the Institute, or also with times devoted to retreat and prayer in one of the houses of our contemplative branch. On this the Constitutions say: “The members of the Institute will also know how to interrupt their apostolic works with periods of retreat and spiritual refreshment, knowing that no one can procure the sanctification of others if he does not first possess the perfection he wishes to transmit”111.
  5. The lay brothers may likewise render apostolic services in parishes or in mission territories. For this they will take care to have adequate preparation for catechesis, of children as well as of young people and adults. They may help the priest in the ministry of caring for the sick. With due preparation, the brothers may be extraordinary ministers of the Eucharist and receive the lay ministries of Lector and Acolyte112. They may collaborate in the preparation of the liturgy, so that it has the splendor it deserves.
  6. Those who have a greater inclination for study or teaching may deepen their formation by obtaining teaching degrees or studying languages, in order to devote themselves to teaching113.
  7. In cases where the religious brothers take on an apostolate in the social communications media, they must have adequate preparation for it. Moreover, they must take into account the warning of the Constitutions in the section on the use of the media114.
  8. The Directory of Spirituality also urges the brothers when it asks our religious “not to shy away from missionary adventure”115.
  9. Finally, let us remember that the Constitutions list the coadjutor brothers as key pieces: “They—the sick and the elderly—together with the members of the contemplative branches, the religious brothers and those who devote themselves to the works of mercy, are the key pieces of the apostolic endeavor of our Institute”116.

PART V

FORMATION OF THE COADJUTOR BROTHER

  1. “We want to form the members of the Institute in a great human and Christian maturity so that they may attain the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ (Eph 4:13)”117. “Young people need to be spurred on toward the lofty ideals of the radical following of Christ and toward the profound demands of holiness, in view of a vocation that surpasses them and perhaps goes beyond the initial plan that moved them to enter a particular Institute. Formation, therefore, must have the characteristics of an initiation into the radical following of Christ”118.

Chapter 1: Need for the formation of the coadjutor brothers

  1. The coadjutor brothers need a formative path suited to their own vocation and mission. They begin the itinerary of formation from the postulancy and the novitiate; however, it is necessary that their initial formation continue until perpetual profession. “After first profession, the formation of all members must continue in each institute, so that they may live more fully the life proper to it and better fulfill its mission”119. Likewise, the Exhortation Vita consecrata “recommends… with regard to religious brothers, an explicitly formative period, extending until perpetual profession”120.
  2. The Constitutions establish specifically for the coadjutor brothers: “The brothers must make the Studentate, in which they will take some treatises of Philosophy and Theology, ‘attending to the needs of the Church and to the circumstances of men and of the times, as the end and character of the Institute require.’ The religious brothers will continue their formation until perpetual profession, in the houses to which they are assigned and under the direct authority of the local Superior, and attention will be given to their ongoing formation”121.
  3. Vita consecrata notes that the apostolate proper to religious institutes of brothers “requires an appropriate and integral formation: human, spiritual, theological, pastoral and professional”122. Therefore, once the novitiate is finished, the brothers will continue their formation in the houses to which they are assigned. However, it is fitting that they take advantage of some courses in the major Seminary.
  4. “We must be extremely generous in dedicating time and our best energies to formation. Consecrated persons are, in fact, one of the most precious goods of the Church. Without them, all formative and apostolic plans remain theory, useless wishes. Without forgetting that, in an accelerated age like ours, what is needed more than anything else is time, perseverance and patient waiting in order to reach the formative objectives. In circumstances where speed and superficiality prevail, we need serenity and depth, because in reality the person is forged very slowly”123.

Chapter 2: Dimensions of the formation of the brothers

  1. “In order to be complete, formation must embrace every area of Christian life and consecrated life. Provision must therefore be made for human, cultural, spiritual and pastoral preparation, taking the greatest care to facilitate the harmonious integration of the various aspects. Ample time must be reserved for initial formation, understood as an evolving process that passes through the various degrees of personal maturation—from the psychological and spiritual to the theological and pastoral. In the case of vocations to the priesthood, it coincides and harmonizes with a specific program of studies, as part of a longer formative itinerary”124.
  2. “In a time of profound transformations, formation must take care to root in the hearts of young consecrated persons the necessary human, spiritual and charismatic values, which make them fit to live a dynamic fidelity, in the wake of the spiritual and apostolic tradition of the institute”125. The best guide for all these aspects of formation is found in our Constitutions when they speak of the various dimensions that the formation of our religious must embrace: human, spiritual, intellectual and pastoral formation126. To what is said there we add some things that must be kept in mind in the formation of the religious brothers.

Article 1: Initial formation

  1. Following the suggestions given by the Exhortation Vita consecrata127, it will be fitting to establish a Regulation for the studies of the coadjutor brothers, which includes the stages of initial formation and of ongoing formation.
  2. Likewise it will be fitting that, both in the houses of formation and in the large communities in which coadjutor brothers are present, a priest be designated as responsible for their formation. In the period of initial formation, this is particularly necessary in order to guide the brothers in their studies and in the exercise of the activities proper to their religious life. It must be borne in mind that, in consecrated life, “the first years of full involvement in apostolic activity represent a phase that is critical in itself, marked by the passage from a guided and supervised life to a situation of full operational responsibility. It is important that young consecrated persons be encouraged and accompanied by a brother or sister who helps them to live to the full the youthfulness of their love and of their enthusiasm for Christ”128.
  3. 108. For the same reason it is fitting that in each Province there be a priest charged with seeing to it that the formation of the coadjutor brothers does not suffer harm.
  4. The coadjutor brothers also need to form their intelligence. A “certainty of the truth” is necessary, given only by a sound philosophy founded on the objective reality of things, since “the intellect… can reach what is”129. The brothers, to the extent they are able, must also have access to a philosophical and theological formation that allows them to face the challenges that “come from the values that dominate the globalized culture of our day”130. “The brothers must make the Studentate, in which they will take some treatises of Philosophy and Theology”131.
  5. For the study of sacred doctrine they will make use of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, and of other aids that will be indicated to them in due course for their theological formation. “This study must lead ‘to possessing a complete and unified vision of the truths revealed by God in Jesus Christ and of the Church’s experience of faith; hence the twofold requirement of knowing all the Christian truths and knowing them in an organic way’”132.

Article 2: Ongoing formation

  1. “Precisely because of its aim of transforming the whole person, the need for formation never ends. Indeed, consecrated persons must be given, until the end, the opportunity to grow in adherence to the charism and mission of their own Institute”133.
  2. “We consider it fundamental that formation be ongoing. For this, besides the ordinary means—conferences, retreats, courses, examinations, etc.—extraordinary means will be arranged as far as possible: for example, every ten years the month-long Spiritual Exercises, pilgrimage to the Holy Land and to the great shrines of Christendom, etc.”134.
  3. “It is very important that every consecrated person be formed in the freedom to learn throughout life, at every age and at every moment, in every environment and human context, from every person and every culture, so as to let himself be taught by whatever part of truth and beauty he finds around him. But above all, he must learn to let himself be formed by everyday life, by his own community and by his brothers and sisters, by the things of always, ordinary and extraordinary, by prayer and by apostolic weariness, in joy and in suffering, until the moment of death.»135
  4. “Ongoing formation, for institutes of apostolic life as well as for those of contemplative life, is an intrinsic requirement of religious consecration. The formative process, as has been said, is not limited to the initial phase, since, because of human limitations, the consecrated person can never assume that he has completed the gestation of that new man he experiences within himself, nor that he possesses in every circumstance of life the same sentiments as Christ. Initial formation, therefore, must be linked with ongoing formation, creating in the subject a readiness to let himself be formed every day of his life”136.
  5. Vita consecrata recommends “that each institute include, as part of its ratio institutionis, the definition of a plan of ongoing formation that is as precise and systematic as possible, whose primary objective is to accompany each consecrated person with a program that embraces his whole existence. No one can be exempt from applying himself to his own human and religious growth; just as no one can presume on himself and lead his life with self-sufficiency. No phase of life can be considered so secure and fervent as to exclude every opportunity of being assisted and thus of having greater assurance of perseverance in fidelity, nor is there any age at which the complete maturity of the person can be considered finished”137.

PART VI

THE COADJUTOR BROTHER

AND THE GOVERNMENT OF THE INSTITUTE

  1. As regards government, the religious brothers, like all the religious of the Institute, are subject through obedience to the local Superior. Nevertheless, in large communities, such as houses of formation, if it is deemed fitting, a cleric may be designated who, in dependence on the local Superior, acts as superior or prefect of the brothers, to whom they may turn, especially for the safeguarding of discipline.
  2. As for all the other members of the Institute, dialogue with the Superior is recommended to the coadjutor brothers138.
  3. As members of the community, the brothers also take part in the chapters. In addition, the perpetually professed brothers take part in the elective acts determined by the Constitutions.

Have faith in the Incarnation! 1

St. John Paul II

Just a few days ago we began Advent, a time in which we devote ourselves –as our Directory of Spirituality teaches us– “in a special way to prayer, to the practice of charity and piety, to denying oneself, to fulfilling better the duties of one’s state, etc.”2, as a preparation for the commemoration of the event that inaugurated the Christian era: the birth of Jesus.

It can be very illuminating during this time to note that in the first reading that we will read at the Christmas Eve (or Midnight) Mass, we will hear the Prophet Isaiah speak to us of a people that walked in darkness … [that bore a] heavy yoke, and whose shoulders were struck by a rod3. And he will also tell us that it was that people, with all its darkness and its pain, that on that first Christmas saw a great light, and its joy was made great4.

So it is: to a depressed, despairing and distrustful world, God came5. And He came to order men and the world according to God, to order not only their hearts and their souls but also their business affairs, their family life, their governments and everything human. This reminds us of that phrase of our Proper Law which we have read and heard so many times and which in this context takes on lofty relevance: “what is not assumed is not redeemed”6.

That is why our beloved St. John Paul II, quoting the Prologue of the Gospel of St. John, which we will read this Christmas, said: “The Word became flesh, and made his dwelling among us7. In saying became flesh, the evangelist wishes to allude to human nature, not only in its mortal condition, but also in its totality. Everything that is human, except sin, was assumed by the Son of God. The Incarnation is the fruit of an immense love, which moved God to want to share fully in our human condition”8.

So we can say that God solved the social, political, family and economic problems of the world not by promulgating a new economic system; He did not do it by showing us a mountain of papers with the results of His research, revealing new aspects of the problem; nor did He do it with wars. He saved the world from its evils by being born as a Child in the insignificant village of Bethlehem, assuming everything human.

If we look closely, that apparently trivial incident, so common and so ordinary, which tells how the owners of the inn turned away the Holy Family, simply telling them that there was no room for them in the inn, is the event that turned the order of the world upside down and the solution that gave it its peace. How many times have we also read that the Virgin Mother sought refuge in a manger and laid the newborn Child on the ground of this world. Well then, that Child, in taking that immense leap from the heavenly life down to the abyss of human existence, like another Samson shook the pillars of the world from their very root, pulled down the building that was already collapsing, and built the Temple of the living God where men will once again be able to sing, because they have found their God. That is the Good News that we are called to proclaim to the ends of the earth.

1. In Christ, human time was filled with eternity

But someone may ask: what does the birth of God as a Child have to do with the social, political and economic conditions of His time and of our time? What relationship can there be between the Child lying on the straw of a manger and Caesar on his golden throne? The answer is this: the birth of the Son of God in the flesh was the introduction of a new life into the historical world order; it was the proclamation to the world that the rebuilding of society has to do with its spiritual regeneration; that nations can be saved only when men are reborn to God as God was born that night for men. And this cannot leave us indifferent, we who are members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word, called as we have been to “launch ourselves boldly to restore all things in Christ”9.

In becoming man, God enters into the created order, becomes part of its history in an entirely new way, draws near to us and gives us a strength that comes from on high. Our Proper Law has, in this regard, a phrase of St. John Paul II that says: “God was never so close to man −and man was never so close to God− as precisely at that moment”10. Let us note, then, that “the fact that the Word of God became man brought about a fundamental change in the very condition of time. We can say that, in Christ, human time was filled with eternity. It is a transformation that affects the destiny of all humanity, since ‘the Son of God, by his incarnation, has united himself in some way with every man’11. [For] He came to offer everyone a share in His divine life. [And] The gift of this life entails a share in His eternity”12.

That is, “the Savior… willed to become the Son of Man so that we might become children of God”13. That is the true meaning of Christmas. Jesus became man to make us sharers in His divine life and then in His eternal glory. That is why Christ is born as a Child: to teach us that liberation from all the economic, social and political chaos can be achieved only by a birth. The birth of Christ in souls.

Since what is proper to us is “to restore, integrally, all things in Christ”14, it seems necessary to emphasize once again that the “Gospel must also penetrate, purify and transform the economic, social and political order”15.

At the time of Christ’s birth, humanity was weary; it was mentally and spiritually exhausted; for four thousand years it had been carrying out the experiment of humanism, and it found itself like a sick man who cannot cure himself. It was in a state like that of our world, which since the days of the Renaissance has tried to build a civilization upon the self-sufficiency of man without God.

How long it takes us men to recognize that humanity, left thus to itself, sinks and becomes little more than a beast. We have sufficient proof that people of an advanced culture can end up becoming a savage culture… (it is enough to watch the news on one of these evenings).

Without supernatural help, society goes from bad to worse until its deterioration becomes universal. It is the law of man without God: not evolution but regression; just as it is the law of the sunflower without the sun.

Humanity left to itself cannot even tie its own shoelaces. With all the knowledge that man has nowadays about chemistry, about genetics… we cannot make a human life in a laboratory, because we lack the life-giving principle which is the soul, which comes only from God. Life is not a push from below, but a gift from above. It is not the result of man’s necessary ascent, but of God’s descent. It is not “progress”; it is the fruit of the Incarnation.

Therefore, like that world into which Christ was born, today’s world also needs not a mixture of old retouched ideas, not a new economic system, not a new financial system: it needs a New Birth. It needs the irruption into its order of a new life and a new spirit, which only God can give. We cannot give ourselves that New Birth, in the same way that none of us can be born again naturally. If we are to be reborn to this new life, the principle of regeneration must come from heaven, and that is precisely the meaning of the Incarnation: the introduction into the world, at the level of human life, of the Life of God, who came not to judge the world but to save it. And that is why we say that the Incarnate Word solved our problems by being born as a Child, because the regeneration of society has to do with being born, being born to the life of grace and growing according to it. In this sense, the Incarnation is not something of the past. How can God belong to the past? The Incarnation is taking place now16.

For what God did to His individual human nature –which He took from the Blessed Virgin Mary– is what He wishes to do, to no lesser degree, to every human nature in the world; that is, to make us sharers in the divine life. The Incarnate Word, who from all eternity willed to be born in Bethlehem on that cold Christmas night, wants us and all the men and women who walk this world to be born into the eternity of our Heavenly Father, made new creatures, possessed by the new life of His grace and members of His Kingdom.

The same divine life that came into the world two thousand twenty years ago must once again permeate the course of the world. And unless it is reborn to that new life, the world will perish. That is why we affirm once again that today, as yesterday, “the correct understanding of the adorable mystery of the Incarnation of the Word is also the keystone for understanding and building the whole human temporal order, its culture and its civilization. To confess the authentic and integral human condition of Jesus, assumed by the eternal Word of God, makes it possible ‘to recover the dimension of the divine in every earthly reality’17”18. This is the truth that must drive us to mission.

We must never forget that we “confess the One Master and Lord, Our Jesus Christ19, and that in Him is contained a reference to all creatures. […] [Which means that He] is the point of union and meeting place of God and man; of the eschatological and of the incarnate; of sidereal distances and of the millimeter. Therefore, nothing escapes Christ: neither space travel, nor the world of electronics and computers, of the most current science or of the most perfected technology, nor recent discoveries, nor the family, work, culture, the economic-political sphere, etc.”20. Evangelizing in “the modern areopagi”21 is not simply a metaphor; it is our task to make it a reality.

In writing these paragraphs it seems inevitable to recall the ardent request that St. John Paul II made to us in the inaugural address of his pontificate: “Open, open wide still more, the doors to Christ! Open to His saving power the boundaries of States, economic and political systems, the vast fields of culture, of civilization and of development. Do not be afraid!”22.

Likewise, how timely and relevant it is to recall the fatherly exhortation he made in 1984 to some religious, which seems to be addressed especially to us here and now: “Have faith in the Incarnation! The nearness of the Christmas feasts moves me to highlight this fundamental program of your religious life […] To have faith in the Incarnation means first of all to believe firmly in Divine Providence. For God has concretely manifested His love for humanity by entering into it as Man and as Savior. St. John writes: in this is love, not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son as expiation for our sins23. Many events of present-day life shock and disturb us; the ‘Christian anthropology’ based on the concept of the ‘human person’ created by God, redeemed by Christ, enlightened by the Church, responsible for his acts for eternity, stands in opposition to the immanentist and historicist anthropology with no relation whatsoever to Revelation. […] But with all this, the commemoration of Christmas once again highlights, and continues to highlight, that the Word became flesh to enlighten humanity about its true destiny and about the presence of divine love in the vicissitudes of history. Humility is needed to accept and live this reality. To have faith in the Incarnation also means to love man, whoever he may be, as a creature of God. The very fact that God willed to become man clearly shows how much He loves, esteems and values him. […] And finally, to have faith in the Incarnation means to come to the aid of souls in order to save them. For it was for this that He was born in the grotto of Bethlehem and died on the cross”24.

That is why the call to subject everything human to our Lord is inescapable, and not to “leave anything untried so that the love of Christ may have supreme primacy in the Church and in society”25: since He is the only one who brings men into communion with God, ‘it is necessary that all of human culture be filled with the Gospel’26. There lies the most important and fundamental role that our little Religious Family is called to play.

“Christmas is the divine light that gives value and meaning to the life of persons and to the history of humanity,” said the Spiritual Father of the Institute. That is why spending our lives in multiplying Christmases must be the purpose of our priesthood. For it is so that God may be born anew in the hearts of so many men and so many women who still do not know Christ, or who perhaps, knowing Him, are not consistent with it, that the sacrament of Holy Orders has been conferred on us. Evangelization, the preaching of the Good News, belongs to each one of us personally, in spite of our littleness, the lack of means and adverse circumstances.

The watchword of this Christmas continues to be to contemplate our life with the eyes of God, full of trust and love. Jesus was born in the poverty of Bethlehem to embrace all of our humanity. Jesus comes back to us this year too, to renew the hidden marvel of the salvation offered to all men and to the whole man. His grace works silently in the intimacy of each soul, because salvation is essentially a dialogue of faith and love with Christ, adored in the mystery of the Incarnation. Let us accept this mystery as the true gift of Christmas and let us be, in turn, magnanimous distributors of this Gift, so that through our self-giving the ideals −the only ones that console− of a life spent in faith, in charity, in goodness, in holiness may triumph, today as always.

And this brings us to the next point:

2. Bringing the mystery of Christmas to the people

St. John of the Cross wrote that “the works of the Incarnation of the Word and the mysteries of faith; which, being greater works of God and containing in themselves a greater love than those of creatures, produce in the soul a greater effect of love”27. St. John Paul II, for his part, imbued no doubt with the doctrine of St. John of the Cross, did not hesitate to affirm that “in the Incarnation, more than in any other work, the glory of God is revealed”28.

Therefore, bringing to all peoples the light of the truth of God made Man, who has been given to us as “brother, companion and master, price and reward”29, is not only an excellent way of giving glory to God, but is the very purpose of evangelization and, certainly, one of the most effective pastoral practices, or rather, “the pastoral practice.” The Holy Father explains: “Man cannot live without love. He is called to love God and neighbor, but in order to love truly he must have the certainty that God loves him”30. And for this there is nothing better than the mystery of the newborn Son of God who asks for our love, “through which we spontaneously learn to enter into His feelings, into His thought and into His will: let us learn to live with Him and also to practice with Him the humility of renunciation, which is an essential part of love”31. That is the “greater effect of love” that we want to achieve.

What is more eloquent for souls “than the tenderness of God: God who looks at us with eyes full of affection, who accepts our misery, God in love with our littleness”32.

3. An altogether singular example

Before mentioning the different activities that our Proper Law proposes to us during the Christmas season, I would like to mention, as an altogether singular example for us, how St. John Paul II lived Christmas, for he lived it as a time of particular spiritual intensity. For we consider that his example, so human and at the same time so supernatural, can be replicated with a certain ease in our lives, in our communities, in our missions, given its simplicity. Let us see:

His devotion to the mystery of Christmas goes back to his childhood, and he describes it very succinctly but beautifully in a letter addressed to children: “When, many years ago, I was a child like you, I too lived the serene atmosphere of Christmas, and on seeing the star of Bethlehem shine I would run to the Nativity scene with my friends to remember what happened in Palestine 2000 years ago. We children expressed our joy above all with songs. How beautiful and moving are the carols which, in the tradition of each people, are sung around the Nativity scene! What deep feelings they contain and, above all, how much joy and tenderness they express toward the divine Child who came into the world on Christmas Eve! The days that follow the birth of Jesus are also days of celebration: thus, eight days later, we remember that, according to the tradition of the Old Testament, a name was given to the Child, calling Him Jesus”33.

Those who spent Christmas with the Holy Father testify34 how much he liked this time of year and how he longed for the coming of Christmas. They say that he always wanted to celebrate it in a family atmosphere, according to the Polish tradition; that is why he generally invited all those who shared his daily life with him, the sisters who worked in his house and the secretaries. He also invited some of his old friends from Krakow along with their families. On Christmas night, John Paul II would begin by lighting a candle and placing it in the window. They say that he began this tradition in 1981, when General Jaruzelski declared martial law in Poland; and the Pope wanted to symbolize with that candle his closeness to his persecuted fellow citizens. Once this was done, a passage from the Gospel was read, and only after that did the dinner begin, at which, of course, traditional Polish dishes were served.

After dinner, carols were sung. Those who lived with him testify that he liked singing very much, and during the Christmas season he did so every night from December 24 to January 6.

John Paul II also liked the Christmas tree, and very much so. It was he who began the tradition of setting up a monumental Nativity scene next to a giant Christmas tree in St. Peter’s Square. Not content with this, he also liked to decorate his apartment with the little trees from the Polish mountains of Zakopane that were sent to him, and in the hallway during the Christmas season there was always a Nativity scene, “because there is no Christmas without a Nativity scene,” he used to say. What is more, “in the Christmas period the stable with the manger occupies a central place in the Church”35, the Holy Father put in writing.

Now, under the Christmas tree there were no presents −following the Polish tradition− in which presents are given on December 6, the day of St. Nicholas. So on that day, “St. Nicholas came into the refectory, announced by bells. It was one of the sisters dressed up as the ‘Saint of presents.’ I don’t know if I should tell you this,” said one of his secretaries −now Archbishop Mieczyslaw Mokrzycki− and he continued: “To tell the truth, in my first year in the papal household I was surprised, because it seemed to me a children’s party and we were all quite grown up. But it really moved me a great deal to see how much the Pope loved us, and also because I understood that in those moments he was remembering his childhood in Wadowice”36. “At that point, ‘St. Nicholas’ had something to say to each person present there before handing out the presents. Little things: pastries, fruit, a scarf, a shirt, gloves. We joked and we laughed. And so it always was, every year, until the end, until December 2004”37.

This little testimony lets us glimpse, along with the simplicity of heart of the Holy Pope, the care and importance he gave to every detail of Christmas as a family, as if wanting to make others perceive −in a tangible way− God’s love for each one of them personally.

Moved surely by the same intention, it is no minor detail to recall the fact that in 1959, as a bishop, he celebrated Christmas in the open air in Nowa Huta, the so-called ‘model workers’ city’ on the outskirts of Krakow, the first city in the history of Poland deliberately built without a church.

Likewise, throughout the length and breadth of his whole pontificate, but especially at Christmas, St. John Paul II strove to make “the unheard-of tenderness of God, which is offered to all men and which is admirably manifested in the Child of Bethlehem”38 reach everyone. He wanted to infect everyone with the Christmas spirit. Thus, for example, when he visited New York in October 1995, before thousands of people in Central Park, the Pope who had loved Christmas since he was a child suddenly set aside the text he had prepared and began to speak about one of his favorite Polish carols, which he then began, spontaneously, to sing. The crowd applauded with delight, to which the Pope responded by saying: “And to think that you don’t even know Polish”39, and the people applauded even more vigorously. Then he said: “That is a hymn that moves us deeply by reminding us that Jesus, the Son of God, was born of Mary, born to sanctify us and to make us adopted sons and daughters of God. It is a hymn to the creative power of the Holy Spirit. It is a song to help us not to be afraid. If I speak of Christmas it is because in less than five years we will reach the end of the Second Millennium, two thousand years since the birth of Christ on that first Christmas night in Bethlehem. We must let the Holy Spirit prepare us for this important event, which is another significant stage of His passage through history and of our pilgrimage of faith”40.

4. Our way of celebrating Christmas

Similarly, and under the guidance of the Polish pope, each of the members of the Institute, imbued with faith in the mystery of the Incarnation and with great apostolic enthusiasm41 and priestly self-giving42, must not only personally live the Christmas season in all its spiritual depth and with festive joy43, but must also strive to transmit, spread and form others in the spirit proper to Christmas; even through the humblest expressions of popular sensibility, such as setting up a good Nativity scene, decorating a beautiful Christmas tree, promoting the participation of as many as possible in the ‘living Nativity,’ organizing pilgrimages to the Nativity scenes in the various churches or homes of the faithful, singing carols or holding Christmas concerts, etc. All these expressions of popular piety help people grasp intuitively44:

– the value of the ‘spirituality of gift,’ proper to Christmas: a child is born to us, a son is given to us45, a gift that is the expression of the infinite love of God, who so loved the world that He gave us His only Son46;

– the message of solidarity that the event of Christmas entails: solidarity above all with sinful man, for whom, in Jesus, God became man for us men and for our salvation; solidarity with the poor, because the Son of God, though He was rich, became poor to enrich us by His poverty47;

– the sacred value of life and the wonderful event that takes place in every woman’s giving birth, because through Mary’s giving birth the Word of life came to men and became visible48;

– the value of messianic joy and peace, the deep aspirations of men of all times: the Angels announce to the shepherds that the Savior of the world, the Prince of peace, has been born49, and express the wish for peace on earth to men whom God loves50;

– the atmosphere of simplicity and poverty, of humility and trust in God, that surrounds the events of the birth of the Child Jesus.

And so, in a simple way, all these practices of piety help to safeguard the memory of our Lord’s earthly coming, and keep the strong religious tradition linked to Christmas from becoming fertile ground for consumerism or for the infiltration of neo-paganism51.

Our Proper Law, for its part, though briefly, lists several activities which are not only expected to be carried out in our houses of formation or in our parishes, but which it wishes to see fostered and enriched as much as possible −whenever pastoral prudence allows− making room for apostolic creativity to give rise to other activities that awaken in souls the desired “holy familiarity with the Word made flesh”52.

  • The Directory of Parishes, for example, mentions that the Christmas Vigil should be solemnized53, and it may very well be preceded by a Christmas novena, since this is one of the high points of parish pastoral work54. Likewise, it stresses that this season should be made the most of, and with that in view it must be carefully prepared55
  • The Directory of Works of Mercy points out, in addition, that “it would be praiseworthy to foster the works of mercy among our laity. Such as the pious tradition of clothing a poor child on Christmas Day in memory of the Child of Bethlehem, or a whole family on the day of the Holy Family”56. Charity toward the poor who come looking for food should not be forgotten when preparing Christmas dinner57, and this not only in the missions but also in the houses of formation.
  • The Directory of Minor Seminaries says, in turn, that one of the hobbies it would be nice for the minor seminarians to learn is various techniques for building Nativity scenes58. Elsewhere, the creation of an Association of Christmas Nativity Scenes is also suggested, to take charge of promoting, spreading and awarding prizes to the public and family Nativity scenes made in a parish59.

On the other hand, the Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy issued by the Congregation for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments during the pontificate of John Paul II suggests things that may very well enrich the activities already being carried out, or that can begin to be put into practice, whether at the community or parish level as appropriate60; for example:

→ During Advent

The Directory points out the tradition of the Advent wreath, which is customary above all in Germanic countries and in North America, and has become a symbol of Advent in Christian homes. The Advent wreath consists of four candles on a wreath of green branches, which are lit progressively, Sunday after Sunday, until the solemnity of Christmas. It is a remembrance of the various stages of the history of salvation before Christ and a symbol of the prophetic light that gradually illumined the night of waiting, until the dawn of the Sun of justice61. It is good to make it, and to make it well, explaining the value of these traditions both to the faithful and to the religious of our communities.

Likewise, mention is made of Advent processions, which are a proclamation through the streets of the city of the coming birth of the Savior (the ‘clear star’ in some places in Italy), or else representations of the journey of Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem and their search for a welcoming place for the birth of Jesus (known as the ‘posadas’ in the Spanish and Latin American tradition). Or likewise the novenas held at dawn in some of our missions, such as in the Philippines (known as Simbang Gabi), Guyana, etc.

→ For Christmas Eve:

In the time between First Vespers of Christmas and the Midnight Eucharistic celebration, together with the tradition of Christmas carols, which are very powerful instruments for transmitting the Christmas message of joy and peace, popular piety proposes some of its expressions of prayer, differing from country to country, which it is fitting to value and, where necessary, to harmonize with the celebrations of the Liturgy. These may include, for example:

– “living Nativity scenes,” and the inauguration of the household Nativity scene, which can provide an occasion of prayer for the whole family: a prayer that includes the reading of the account of the birth of Jesus according to St. Luke, in which the typical Christmas songs resound and supplications and praises are raised, above all those of the children, the protagonists of this family gathering;

– the inauguration of the Christmas tree. This too lends itself to an act of family prayer similar to the previous one.

– Christmas dinner. The Christian family which every day, according to tradition, blesses the table and gives thanks to the Lord for the gift of food, will carry out this gesture with greater intensity and attention at Christmas dinner, in which the firmness and the joy of family bonds are manifested in all their strength.

The Church also wishes that all the faithful take part on the night of December 24, if possible, in the Office of Readings, as an immediate preparation for the celebration of the Midnight Eucharist. Where this is not done, it may be fitting to prepare a vigil with songs, readings and elements of popular piety, drawing inspiration from that office.

At Midnight Mass, which has great liturgical meaning and enjoys popular esteem, the following may be highlighted:

– at the beginning of Mass, the chanting of the proclamation of the birth of the Lord, with the formula of the Roman Martyrology;

– the prayer of the faithful, which should take on a truly universal character, even, where appropriate, with the use of several languages as a sign; and in the presentation of the gifts at the offertory there will always be a concrete remembrance of the poor;

– at the end of the celebration, the faithful may kiss the image of the Child Jesus, and it may be placed in the Nativity scene set up in the church or in some nearby place.

It is also important to give people catechetical instruction about the possibility of gaining plenary indulgences during the Urbi et orbi blessing imparted by the Pope, which must be accompanied by confession and communion on the part of whoever receives it with faith and devotion, even through television, radio or the internet.

I would like to add a word about Christmas songs, the carols, which should fill this whole season. It is beautiful for us to foster these songs, in the liturgy, in families, in parish groups, in Christmas processions, etc. May we religious be instruments for maintaining, enriching and increasing these beautiful traditions.

→ The Feast of the Holy Family

For the Feast of the Holy Family (Sunday within the octave of Christmas), our parishes should encourage the whole family to take part together in the Eucharist on that day. And it would be very meaningful for the family to entrust itself anew to the patronage of the Holy Family of Nazareth, for the blessing of children to take place, or for marriage promises to be renewed. In several of our jurisdictions, this day is also used for us religious to gather with the tertiaries to celebrate the Christmas season “as a family,” which has already become a beautiful tradition.

→ December 31

It is customary to have prolonged exposition of the Blessed Sacrament, which offers both religious and faithful an occasion for a time of prayer, preferably in silence; and the singing of the Te Deum, as a communal expression of praise and thanksgiving for the benefits obtained from God in the course of the year that is about to end.

In some places, above all in monastic communities, on the night of December 31 a prayer vigil takes place, which usually concludes with the celebration of the Eucharist. This vigil should be encouraged, and its celebration must be in harmony with the liturgical content of the Octave of Christmas, lived not only as a justified reaction to the carelessness and dissipation with which society lives the passage from one year to another, but as a vigilant offering to the Lord of the first fruits of the new year.

→ The Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God

In the West, January 1 is a day for exchanging greetings: it is the beginning of the civil year. The faithful are caught up in the festive atmosphere of the beginning of the year and exchange wishes of “Happy New Year” with everyone. However, it is good to know how to give this custom a Christian meaning, and make of it almost an expression of piety. The faithful know that “the new year” is under the lordship of Christ, and that is why, in exchanging greetings and good wishes, they place it, implicitly or explicitly, under the dominion of Christ, to whom belong the days and the eternal ages62.

Connected with this awareness is the rather widespread custom of singing on January 1 the hymn Veni, creator Spiritus, so that the Spirit of the Lord may direct the thoughts and actions of each and every one of the faithful and of Christian communities throughout the whole year. In addition, on that day, since 1967, the “World Day of Peace” has been celebrated.

In short, much more could be said, but what is important is that we all live Christmas as it should be lived: in a Christian way, as a family, and with festive joy. How much our world needs all this! It is our honorable office to permeate culture with the truth of Christmas: “The only-begotten Son of God, wishing that we too might be sharers in His divinity, assumed our human nature, so that, having become man, He might make men gods”63, that is, sharers by grace in the divine nature. And it is imperative to implore heaven that this divine life which makes souls fruitful may descend upon souls –as the dew descends each morning upon the field.

A holy Christmas! and a holy year!, which is the same as wishing you everything, everything good in the life here below that may serve, or at least not hinder, the gaining of everything good in the life beyond, which is the true life.

And since wishes, if they are not turned into works or prayers, are of no use, let us turn our wishes into supplications to the Incarnate Word and His Most Holy Mother for one another.

May the simplicity and tenderness of the Child in Bethlehem make its nest in your hearts and spread to your communities and, better still, to the whole world, thanks to you.

A very happy and holy Christmas!

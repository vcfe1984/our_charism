It now falls to us to consider the mystery so consoling for us Christians, which is the mystery of the Ascension of the Lord.

The Ascension is the fruit of His Passion

After the Resurrection, Our Lord remained forty days with His disciples; during them Jesus prepared the nascent Church and strengthened it so that it might know how to bear His absence.

At the end of those forty days, Christ led them all toward Bethany, a small village near Jerusalem; there the scene of the farewell would unfold, for there His Ascension into Heaven would take place. At that moment the event occurred: He raised those precious arms that bore the marks of the nails. That gesture would be the last memory of the Master that the apostles would keep. Those hands that now are raised so that afterwards, from heaven, they may descend toward the earth, bestowing unceasing blessings. Those hands that had been pierced by the nails of the cross—the moment had come for them to give their last blessing: While He was blessing them, He parted from them and was carried up into heaven1, says the Gospel of St. Luke.

The Ascension took place on the Mount of Olives, at whose foot Bethany lies. To get there, He led His apostles through Bethany, which meant that He had to pass through the very place where Christ had suffered, for they crossed the Mount of Olives; from there Christ had wept when He saw Jerusalem, and it was from there, from a mount situated above the garden of twisted olive trees stained by the Blood of the Passion, from there that Christ manifested His supreme power by rising in body and soul to the Father who is in heaven.

With this He taught us a great truth: that the Ascension and the glory of heaven were the fruit of that crucifixion. That in order to ascend to heaven, men must first face and bear the sufferings and crosses that present themselves to us on this earth, so as then to be able to enter thus into His glory. He had said, referring to Himself: It was necessary that the Messiah should suffer, so as thus to enter into His glory2.

It is remarkable, but if He had been crowned on earth instead of ascending to heaven, the thoughts men would have conceived about Him would have remained confined to the earth. But the Ascension would cause the minds and hearts of men to rise above earthly things. It is sometimes hard to believe that precisely He, the Man of sorrows, acquainted with anguish and suffering, should now be in glory. It is hard to believe that He who had not wanted to come down from a cross could now go up to heaven. It is hard to believe that He who in the manger of Bethlehem had no strength to lift His arms should now raise them and rise with them to His Father’s house. The Ascension now dispelled all these doubts by bringing His human nature into an intimate and eternal communion with God.

Men had mocked that human nature which He had assumed at His birth, when the soldiers blindfolded Him and asked Him to guess who was striking Him. They mocked Him as prophet; they mocked Him as king by putting a royal garment on Him and a reed for a scepter. Finally, they mocked Him as priest by challenging Him—He who was offering Himself as victim—to come down from the cross. But the vindication would be complete when He came in His justice, as judge of men, in the very human nature that He had taken from men. None of us who will be judged by God will be able to complain that God was ignorant of the trials to which we mortals are subjected. When He judges us, His very appearance will show that He has fought the same battles as men and suffered the same temptations as those who appear before the tribunal of divine justice.

Triumphant wounds

But there is something more important in the Ascension, and no less consoling, and it is what completes the phrase of the article of the Creed that falls to us today: He ascended into heaven, and is seated at the right hand of God the Father almighty. Another of the great reasons why Our Lord ascended to heaven in body and soul was so that He might plead, intercede before our Father with a human nature: He is true God, but He is also true man, and He can ask as a man, one with the rest of men. Now He can, by the mere fact of showing the wounds of His glory, not only as a trophy of victory but also as insignia of intercession. Before those triumphant wounds, through whose intercession all graces come to us.

Christ, from the moment of the Ascension, is seated at the right hand of the Father; from there He intercedes for us. That is why we Christians must never cease to consider this truth: Christ lives in heaven, and it would be great folly to live as if He no longer existed. Our religion did not end on a Good Friday; rather, it triumphed with the victory of the resurrection, and with the Ascension it continues on its way toward its end, until the last of the elect enters the kingdom of heaven. That is why He can give us, and in fact does give us, if we ask Him faithfully, everything we need; and He knows well what we lack: no one like Him experienced that this life was a valley of tears, and He suffered the vicissitudes of the passion; He was like us in all things but sin: Having, then, a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God, let us remain steadfast in the faith. For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who has been made like us in all things but sin3.

Christ ascended and there He waits for us, so that, following in His footsteps, we may arrive where He is, for, as He Himself said, I go up to prepare a place for you4, a place that awaits us and that none of us may leave empty. He had said in the Gospel of St. John: When I am lifted up from the earth I will draw all to myself5. Christ longs for us to reach that place; He watches over us and keeps us very much in mind. Sacred Scripture says: The eyes of God are fixed on His faithful6—fixed; that is, it is not that, being now in heaven with His mission finished, He is no longer interested in the things of earth; on the contrary, His priesthood is eternal and He wants to give us that glory which He won with so much effort on the Friday of the passion. If we live in accordance with this dogma of our faith, we will understand what a saint used to say: one thing is necessary, to reach heaven. All the sufferings and tribulations and troubles of this life die before one great truth: Christ has risen and is seated at the right hand of God7. That is why we must reach where He is; we may fail in many things, but in this we cannot fail. St. Alphonsus said: everything is little, not to say everything is nothing, so long as we gain paradise8. There all the problems of this life will come to an end. That is why, whatever may happen, when discouragement assails us, when sufferings and tribulations surround us, let us think of heaven, and let us think of Jesus Christ present in heaven; on Him our gaze must be fixed, because only there will we be happy, where, as the canon of the Mass says, God will wipe away the tears from our eyes, and there will be no more grief, nor pain, nor sadness, because we will arrive at the Father’s house, at the day without sunset, at the light that has no end, there where, as our Lord says in the Gospel, your sadness will be turned into joy9.

As we contemplate today, in this novena to St. Cajetan, the mystery of the Ascension of the Lord, may God grant us the enormous grace of always living with our gaze fixed on heaven, and may we never give up in the struggle to get there, so that there may be fulfilled in us what Christ asked of the Father before the Passion: Father, I pray for them, that where I am, they also may be10.

May the Most Blessed Virgin, who so many times after the Ascension must have fixed her eyes on the sky of Jerusalem, seeing her Son waiting for her with open arms, grant this grace to us all.

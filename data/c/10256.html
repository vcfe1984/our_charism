Good-night talk given by Fr. Carlos Miguel Buela on April 28, 1998, on the occasion of the feast of St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, at the “Marcelo Javier Morsella” Novitiate

It occurred to me to give a good-night talk on a special topic, as we generally do on the day of St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort. In a special way for me, because precisely in these days I was thinking about how God does things, how God has gone about forming one. It is not by chance that whatever happens happens, that we have whatever we have. And for me the most important thing we have is the Major Seminary. It is the “goose that lays the golden eggs” of the Congregation. That is why the topic I want to address in this good-night talk is that of priestly formation, because I personally think that the most important thing we have in our congregation, as I already said, is precisely the Major Seminary. To develop the topic, I will first recount some seminary experiences that I think will be useful, and then I will give some points of what I think is important to bear in mind in order to have a good seminary.

I. SEMINARY EXPERIENCES

In seminary X, I had a classmate who did not believe in virginity. I gave him Pius XII's encyclical “Sacra Virginitas,” and he laughed. I mention it to a spiritual director, and he answers me: –“That was a very weak encyclical that Pius XII wrote.” I thought: “They are of the same family,” as Castellani says in the story of the Quillotana. It is a most beautiful encyclical!

Among my classmates I had men who had been Marxist trade unionists, who were no longer trade unionists but were still Marxists; also a Freudian psychologist, a diplomat… All of these left. But it was a tremendous thing. Thank goodness that, by the grace of God, when I was a member of Catholic Action I had studied from a manual of apologetics by Naguemela, from Hilaire, from Olgiati, etc., which taught the central notions of the faith with great clarity. There were points of the faith that these classmates did not believe simply because they had not studied them. I remember that once –it was the time of the Council– a seminarian was running and shouting through the halls of the Seminary with the magazine “Criterio” in his hand: “…priests have to marry, priests have to marry!” The general atmosphere of the Seminary was hostile to celibacy. So much so that for a week, in prayer, I was asking God for light to see what I had to do. The proposal some were making was that it would be obligatory for priests to marry. I was aware that there was no power on earth that could oblige me to marry if I did not want to. But I asked myself: if they give permission to marry, what am I doing in the seminary? Because if I am thinking of marrying, I have to patch things up with the girlfriend I left, I have to start looking for work and housing, because to marry one has to have the means of support… So I said to myself: “God gave me the charism of giving myself to him totally, and I am going to give myself to him totally, with his grace.”

I remember that in my little room I had a window that looked out onto a terrace. From there one could see those who passed by, who were the kind who never study. One of those days one of them says to me: – “So? How are you doing?” –“Very well, happy…” – “Ha, ha! It'll pass. What's happening is that you're on your honeymoon now.”

In another seminary… From one day to the next thirty-five seminarians left. Thirty-five! In one theology class only six of us remained. Of those who left, many were admirable: virtuous, upright, pious, joyful…

Of the professors I had, the one I remember most (it stayed etched in my mind) was the one who denied the bodily resurrection of Our Lord Jesus Christ. Incredible! But there were also other kinds of professors. For example, one whose material for teaching his class fit on a little piece of paper the size of a tram ticket. Then he would say: “In class we are going to cover three points: point one, blah, blah, blah.” At the end of the class we would ask: –“And the second and third points?”; the answer was: –“I already covered them.” And so he taught the class badly, and who knew what he had covered. Another, if he taught Deo Uno, one barely got to learn that God was one, and if he taught Deo Trino, one never got to the trinity of persons. Twelve of those professors left the priesthood.

One December 8 we were in a neighborhood where we had gone to take part in a First Communion Mass. At one point, a Seminary professor was standing next to a priest who was Galician. The priest said to the First Communion children: –“Well, that business of loving God, that was before. Now you have to love your neighbor, because God is very far away. How are you going to say that you love God?” First, does not the first commandment of the law of God say that one must “love God above all things”? Second, God is not far away; he is more interior to us than we are to ourselves; he is in heaven, on earth, everywhere. And the professor, instead of correcting the priest, on the contrary, was laughing.

So one had to study the subjects twice: once to take the exam and again to see what the doctrine of the Church was.

Fr. Ángel Mancuso, when he was a seminarian, had to take an exam but did not want to study from the notes given by the professor of Deo Creante: “This is heresy; I'm not going to study from this.” So in the exam he presented what he understood to be the Catholic faith. And the professor failed him because he had not studied according to what had been taught in class. For the second exam he said to himself: “I am going to present what the professor said, but God knows that I don't believe any of this, so what I am going to say is simply to pass the exam.” He took the exam according to what the professor had taught in class, and the professor failed him as a heretic. In his simplicity, he had drawn the conclusions to which such principles led. Something the professor did not do!

Another seminary… The room of one of the seminarians: there was a picture of a finger writing on the sand: “I love you.” Next to the picture, a prayer of Teilhard de Chardin about the world. In the middle of the room, a stereo system with all the music of the moment.

… At that time the spiritual director used the “Medellín Documents” for spiritual direction. How can one teach Spirituality from the “Medellín Documents”? It is a mystery.

One seminarian put little labels on his flowerpots, took care of them, watered them, got up half an hour early to tend his little flowerpots… The seminarians, with their own keys, go out, come and go, come in, and all at whatever hour. The philosophy students with girlfriends. They are given Freud, Hegel, Rahner to study, as if they were something good.

In another seminary, the rectors came to remain in office for fifteen days, or a month, at most three months. With the aggravating factor that one of them, besides being rector of the Seminary, was pastor of the Cathedral, which is located kilometers away.

Sometimes, even in Seminaries that we suppose to be good, one can notice that they fall into the crassest, coarsest formalism. At bottom they fall into pastoral Jansenism, where what prevails is the supposed letter of the supposed law, rather than the spirit that should reign in the heart of a priest ready to give his life for others.

The main shortcomings occur, in my view, in different areas: disciplinary, doctrinal, intellectual, pastoral. Even charity disappears.

Discipline grows lax: getting up in the morning at a fixed time is questioned; the fact that everyone has to get up at the same time is a coercion, because if someone had stayed up at night praying (generally it was daydreaming), then in the morning he could get up later, etc. Therefore Mass and meditation, which was only half an hour, were optional. There was a certain piety, but properly speaking it was a pietism without content, without strength. Without forming men who eagerly seek the principle and foundation, who seek to reach God, who use the means in spite of their sins, their miseries. But no, they did not see that relationship with the heavenly Father.

In seminaries like that, the one who realizes it may well ask himself whether he is not the only one who has faith. When the case of the one who denied the bodily resurrection of Christ was put to the rector of the seminary, he answered: “What a thing! Some say that Christ did not rise bodily, but well, don't worry about it. There are many who hold that at most there are three miracles in Sacred Scripture.” The rector of the seminary! Those who ought to be specialists in priestly formation!

There was not much preaching; rather, there was little, and all very superficial. It was an insisting “on love, on love, affection, unity…” They preached unity and love, but among themselves they were all divided. “Pastoral, pastoral, pastoral.” Pastoral was nothing more than methods, without effective practical application: pastoral work was reduced entirely to method alone and it was a failure. Pastoral work consisted in seeking the latest novelty and in settling in at people's homes. They went for that and they failed.

I was able to work with modern methods –such as those of “discussion groups,” and Phillips 66– with much success. The point is to apply the good methods but giving them a religious sense; and not to fill them with contents of another kind, foreign to the Gospel.

From the intellectual point of view, a great, total void. Not a single topic could be discussed in depth. The “disputatio” was unknown, and the vast majority did not even know St. Thomas.

From the doctrinal point of view, they did not know the elementary things we had learned when we were aspirants of Catholic Action, such as the commandments of the law of God, the precepts of the Church, the works of mercy. They were not known, and they were not studied.

Finally, the most serious thing of all was that charity was not lived in concrete terms. And a great many other things.

In conclusion: all this bad priestly formation –together with the lack of creativity and of respect for individualities– inevitably leads to weariness, to a lack of joy, to a permanent questioning of what the priest is. It leads to a profound identity crisis, to holding superficially every topic under discussion, and even the truths and articles of the faith.

II. WHAT IS NEEDED FOR THERE TO BE A GOOD SEMINARY

The lesson of this, my particular and non-transferable experience, is that one must reflect in order to find a solution. By the grace of God, that is what we have tried to do, are doing, and are trying to do.

To have a good Seminary two fundamental things are required:

1. A homogeneous team of formators

What does homogeneous mean? That they have the same essential criteria regarding priestly formation, that they work united, that they know that the work of forming a seminary is a work that requires much sacrifice, that many times the fruits are not seen, that many times there are several pastoral failures.

2. The seminarians

This is because the Seminary is not made by the formators alone. Nor is it made by the seminarians alone. But if one has a very good team of formators but does not have seminarians who have “the right stuff,” who seriously want to seek holiness, who want to be formed (to the extent of each one's possibilities), one does not have a Seminary.

This year I am particularly happy because I believe we have a good team of formators in the Seminary. Perhaps as we have never had before. I believe that the reinforcements we have made, and the fact that there are more priests who can dedicate themselves full time to the Seminary, is very important. There is no action at a distance, nor is it written in souls with a pen alone, as Plato said.

Nor is it written in the soul of the seminarian with a pen alone. The seminarian has to see the priest –even with the mistakes we have, so as not to commit them–. There has to be closeness. And realize that these are the two essential things for having a good Seminary.

III. ELEMENTS NECESSARY FOR A GOOD FORMATION IN THE SEMINARY

Formation must not be a formation that is merely orthodox in doctrine –which is already a great deal. That is not enough. Formation in the seminary for the future priest must be integral. No empty spaces must be left. That is, the whole soul of the seminarian must be evangelized, because if we leave something unevangelized, then later that unevangelized something is going to turn against him, and that can be a cause of misfortune, of problems, of giving up, even from the human point of view. Hence the irreplaceable importance of the camps in Bariloche, to give one example.

Seminarians must be formed who practice sports, as should normally be done; who work –like the work of the cooks, which is an admirable thing.

Eutrapelia must be practiced and fostered; that is, they should be normal men who are clear about the true meaning of festivity, who are joyful. One of the things that has most struck me in seminaries is that they are all stressed out by study: “Well… it's because they study a lot.” As far as I know, none of them has distinguished himself in anything, none of them has published any book.

Another very important aspect is that of music. That must always be watched over, because when some seminarian begins to like bad music, it is a bad sign. It is a sign that spiritually he is declining, because evidently “the style is the man.” If there is a man who likes stupid music, not on the occasion of having fun, or in order to have fun, but who gets hooked on that music, it means that spiritually he is going about in foolishness. “The style is the man.” In a monastery there was a Christ made out of twine, all twisted –poor Christ–. When a brother was asked: “–And what is this? How can this be?” he answered: – “Well, but it's just a detail.” It is a detail, yes, but life is a sum of details.

Formation must be integral; men with their own engine must be formed. That was a great longing, and in part I feel very gratified because after years we have managed to have young men who think, who study philosophy in depth, seeking to have a metaphysical mind. This preparation is extremely important in order to be capable afterwards of knowing, seeking and finding solutions to the extremely complex problems that present themselves today. Solutions do not come out just like that, like an air plant. Solutions are years of thinking, of seeing, of deciding, of weighing pros and cons, of studying.

Deep intellectual formation in metaphysics must give the most complete knowledge possible of St. Thomas (this must surely be one of the greatest graces we have had in the seminary and that we continue to have). What has been done is not enough. The new generations must continue carrying out, as Pius XI said, an “ite ad Tomas” (go to St. Thomas). One must go and get to know not only the Summa Theologiae, but all the rest of his brilliant output, and the more and more, the better.

The sense of beauty must be formed; that is, young men must be formed whose stomachs turn viscerally when they are faced with something ugly, in bad taste. May there become incarnate in them what Alexander Solzhenitsyn repeats (although in reality it is a phrase of Fyodor Dostoevsky): “Beauty will save the world.”

Fidelity to the Magisterium of the Church: who could think of teaching something outside the Magisterium of the Church? Christ promised only to Peter, and in the person of Peter to his successors, “upon this rock I will build my Church” (Mt 16:18).

Spirituality must be robust, not mere sentimentality.

The life of discipline. Another of the graces I believe we have is that there is a climate of great joy, a climate that seems to me to be one of great interior freedom, but nevertheless there is seriousness, discipline.

Pastoral life. This means pastoral work in concrete terms; one must not do as those who talk and talk about pastoral work and remain in pure theories. Pastoral work must be a concrete accomplishment. That is why I believe it is also a grace that our men are taught what popular missions are, how to carry them out in concrete terms. A realistic and true pastoral work includes the Spiritual Exercises, vocational ministry, spiritual direction, sacred oratory, youth ministry, catechesis, concrete work with the poor. Regarding this last point, I want to recommend that you never abandon the poor. If you have the poor with you, then you will have God with you. Never abandon the poor or the sick, who are poor in health.

IV. CONCLUSION

I want to recall these things that I think are fundamental, because the test, the great test of priestly formation, you do not have now in the seminary. The test of priestly formation is outside. When it falls to you to do mission work in the Amazon jungle, there the true test will come. It is gratifying to see the maturity and the acquisition of supernatural criteria in newly ordained priests who are in mission territory. It is a very interesting thing to see that maturing that is reached later in the priesthood, because when one is a seminarian one does not know what it is to be a priest. One has the theory about what the priest is, but one does not know, because one does not experience what it is to be a priest. One knows what it is to be a priest when one is a priest, when one celebrates Mass, when one hears confessions, when one preaches, when one gives spiritual direction. Only then does one know what the priest is. Because before, you see the priest “from the outside.”

Therefore, with renewed enthusiasm, let us continue our task, asking in a special way of the Blessed Virgin, and on this day also of St. Louis Marie, the grace to form great priests: priests according to the Heart of Jesus, slaves of the Blessed Virgin, abounding in spirit, ready to set alight this world that is dying of cold because it is atheist, Marxist, secularist, to set it ablaze with the faith and with the charity of Christ.

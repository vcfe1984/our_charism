[Exordium] We are on the eve of the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God, which is, on the one hand, the greatest title a human being could ever come to possess, and, on the other, the most fitting one with which we can honor the Blessed Virgin Mary, our mother. And it is providential to celebrate this feast during these days of Christmas, when we see in our nativity scenes the Mother wholly bent over to contemplate in her Son-God the marvelous mystery of the Incarnation.

1. The law of the Incarnation is a law of suffering

Bishop Fulton Sheen liked to repeat that expression so typical of him: “all men are born to live, but Jesus, the Incarnate Word, is the only one who was born to die.” That was the principal business in which he was engaged from the day of his Birth. That was his goal. So much so that -as Fr. Faber beautifully says- “Calvary was not different from Bethlehem or Nazareth; it surpassed them in degree, but not in nature”1. That is why -Fr. Faber went on to say- the law of the Incarnation is a law of suffering. Our Lord was a man of sorrows from his Birth, and his Mother knew it… His Passion was not merely an event in his life but the end and the proper outcome befitting it2.

That is why that tender, fragile and defenseless Child whom we contemplate today in Mary’s arms is the same one who will later tell us: Do not fear those who kill the body… fear rather him who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna3.

The whole life of the Incarnate Word was wrapped in those words. Words that, at bottom, reveal to us the supreme struggle of every life and also of every day: the struggle to persevere in the good, to preserve our spiritual freedom and with it our dignity.

Today we come to the end of the year, with all the great graces we have received personally and as an Institute, and also with all the adversities and hardships that have befallen us, likewise personally and as an Institute. Why? Because that same law of the Incarnation “embraces us and surrounds us” –says Fr. Faber– until it conquers us absolutely or, if you prefer, in the words of St. John of the Cross: “until [we are] reduced to nothing”4, in such a way that we are not concerned about the esteem and good opinion of men, about little or much health and bodily strength, about the posts or offices that may be given to us or taken from us, about the prosperous or adverse events that may happen to us, about dying young or old5; which “will be the supreme humility, [and] the spiritual union between the soul and God will be accomplished, which is the greatest and highest state that can be reached in this life”6. A path on which one advances only with the staff of the cross.

That is why, in contemplating God made man7 in the arms of his Most Sweet Mother on this last day of the year, when one ponders the prosperous and adverse events one has gone through and prepares to face –with the grace of God– another new year, it is fitting to think that we too must conquer that glory which the Incarnate Word has won for us, and that very probably, in order to attain that supreme freedom with the glorious Christ, we may have to suffer great hardships on this earth. “True freedom consists –said Fulton Sheen– in preserving our soul, ours (keeping our soul, ours), even if we have to lose the body, if necessary, in order to preserve it.”

To each of us there frequently comes in life what Card. Newmann called “the supreme moment” of each day and of life; it is the moment of choosing between a temporal pleasure and eternal freedom. Choosing between following the “law of whim that guides many ignorant men who pride themselves on being shrewd” (as Don Quixote says) and in that way finding oneself ‘free’ of threats, or standing firm in one’s principles and convictions even to giving one’s life out of fidelity to the truth and to what we believe.

2. The sufferings of this life

Let us notice that from the cradle the Incarnate Word subjected his tenderness and fragility to the harshness and discomforts of the manger; his infinite Wisdom and Majesty was surrounded by rough, poor men, held to be of no account; he surrendered his Omnipotence to the needs and hardships of a life made human; being Lord of the world, he had to flee before the malicious plans of Herod, who was seeking him to kill him. And so it was throughout his whole life, up to the supreme moment of his Passion and of Calvary, when the Incarnate Word submitted his Majesty to the supremacy of his enemies; he subjected his hands and his feet to the nails; he submitted his Body to the tomb; he let his good Name be subjugated to the mockery of his enemies; he let his Blood be poured out, making it captive to the lance; he allowed his comfort to be subjected to the plans of pain of his enemies; and his Life he handed over like a servant at their feet. But there is one thing he did not hand over, and that was his Soul; his Soul he kept free for himself.

That he did not surrender, because he knew that if he preserved his freedom, he could recover everything he had already handed over into their hands.

His enemies knew this, and up to the last moment they tried to make him surrender even his spirit by challenging his power: Come down from the cross and we will believe8. And the Incarnate Word did not do it. He refused to do the human thing in order to do the divine: and he remained on the cross. And by acting in that way, he preserved his soul for himself. One thing was untouchable in his life, and in reality in each of our lives: the soul. Our soul is the impregnable fortress of our character. And as long as we keep it ours, no one can snatch it from us, even if they snatch our life.

“Freedom –teaches Fulton Sheen– is not the right to do whatever we feel like, but the right to do what we ought in order to attain the highest and noblest end of our nature, which is nothing other than the enjoyment of eternal happiness.”

St. John of the Cross, when he saw his religious discouraged or weary of the struggle, would tell them: “Come now, sons, on to eternal life”9, for what is ours is “to go by the level road of the law of God and of the Church, and to live only in dark and true faith, and certain hope and entire charity, and to await our goods there, living here as pilgrims, poor, exiles, orphans, dry, without a road and without anything, awaiting everything there”10.

It is always very healthy for our souls to keep in mind what our true destiny is, the purpose of our life, the goal we pursue. With that in mind, we can tell whether or not we are advancing. Now, if because of adverse circumstances, because of the momentary pressure of the powerful, if because of seeking ourselves, preferring a more comfortable and pleasant existence, we constantly change our goal and give up the post God has entrusted to us, it would be nonsense to say that we are advancing, because we are going after the imperfections of our will.

The Incarnate Word, our supreme Model, never lost sight of his goal, and precisely because he did not, he sacrificed everything else so as to be free and able to reach it. It is as happens on journeys: one gets rid of unnecessary things in order to travel more freely, more quickly. What is more: our Lord left everything, even his life. That is why we said at the beginning: the Incarnate Word was born to die. And so he did.

From the sacrifice of his life in order to keep his spirit free, we must learn not to be intimidated by the sufferings, the trials and the great disappointments of this life. The temptation is always there that, forgetting our ideal, we concentrate not on saving our soul but on obtaining a ‘peaceful and comfortable life here on earth.’

Our Lord could have had all the angels and the whole created universe attend to his needs; he could simply have halted the course of the malicious plans of his persecutors… but that would have meant that the ideal of one day being seated at the right hand of his Father in his glory was secondary to immediate and temporal pleasure here on earth. And then the purpose of his life would have been less important than a moment of his life; then the freedom of his soul would have been secondary to the healing of his hands and his feet; then the noblest and most elevated part of himself –his soul– would have been the slave of the lowest and most corruptible part. And that is what we must avoid by every means. Because “what profit is there, and what is it worth before God, that which is not love of God? And this love is not perfect unless it is strong and discerning in purging joy from all things, placing it only in doing the will of God”11.

God in his infinite mercy grants us another year precisely to save our soul and attain the glorious freedom of the children of God. The Crucifixion ended, but Christ remains. How many sorrows –trivial or quite profound– we have gone through in this year that ends today! But here we still are, by the grace of God, and perhaps even stronger than before. That is why we must never descend from the supreme ideal of our lives, which means the imitation of the Incarnate Word and the salvation of our souls.

The temptation will always be strong, and temporal advantages will seem to us ever grander, ever more irresistible: we must be strong and remain masters of our soul. Without retreating a single step in our endeavor.

Fulton Sheen asks: “Must wood submit to the fire so that we can draw with charcoal? Is death the condition of life? Is disciplined study the way to knowledge? Are long hours of tedious practice the road to performing good music? Must we lose our life in order to save it for eternity? Yes. The answer is yes.”

Because the sufferings of this life are nothing compared with the eternal joys that await us12.

Often, in the face of adversity, we find ourselves asking: why? Why does what happens to us happen? Or: how long? How is it possible? And things of that sort. And I have not found a more satisfying answer than the one St. John of the Cross puts in the mouth of God the Father in the Ascent of the Mount, where he says: “Fix your eyes on Him alone, because in Him I have spoken and revealed everything to you, and you will find in Him even more than what you ask and desire”13. And “if you wanted me to answer you with some word of consolation, look at my Son, subject to me and bound by my love, and afflicted, and you will see how many he answers you. […] Look at Him also made man and you will find in that more than you think, because in Christ dwells bodily all the fullness of the divinity”14. And “He has been given to us as brother, companion and master, ransom and reward”15, with all the treasure of graces that each of these titles of Christ means for us. Have you stopped to think about that?

We have been chosen as God’s trusted men, and He himself has placed us at the head of this, his Family. To bargain away his trust, his friendship, for an existence free of tensions, to assent to falsehoods merely to be free of pressures, would be to do him a great offense. God deliver us from such ‘freedoms.’

[Peroratio] Let us begin this new year contemplating God made man in the arms of his Mother, who is also our Mother. And let us give thanks to the Virgin, Queen and Mother of our Institute, for all the blessings (including the crosses) she has granted us during this year. And to Her, the Mother of God who keeps nothing for herself, let us entrust ourselves and the whole Family in this year that is now beginning.

May Our Lady bless and protect you all.

A.M.D.G

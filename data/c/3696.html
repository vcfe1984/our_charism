St. Paul Monastery – Tuscania, June 10, 2017

[Exordium] Dear Mothers and Sisters: First of all, I want to tell you that I am very grateful for the invitation you have extended to me to come and visit you and share this “good afternoon” talk here with you. It is sincerely a joy for me, since a monastery is -as St. John of the Cross said- “a land very well suited for serving God”1, and I am sure that here He is served in abundance.

Today, as we celebrate the great solemnity of the Most Holy Trinity, a mystery to which “we must have a ‘deep and deep-rooted devotion,’ since it is the active principle of the Incarnation”2, I would like –if you will allow me– to reflect with you on the most singular call that each one of us has received to live “a transfigured existence”3, through which –as we say in our formula of profession– we want “to be a concrete imprint that the Trinity leaves in history”4.

1. The primacy of the spiritual

Our spirituality undoubtedly has an “intensely Christological and Trinitarian” character5. If you notice, already from the first paragraph of our Constitutions the Trinity is mentioned. What is more, in the very introduction and in what refers specifically to our Spirituality, we read that from this devotion to the One and Triune God there derives something that gives our spirituality a special stamp, namely (verbatim quote): “the primacy of the spiritual in all our thinking, feeling and acting, since God is the one who works in us both to will and to work according to his will (cf. Phil 2:13), and because the teaching of the Incarnate Word is most clear: Seek first the Kingdom and its justice, and all these things will be given you besides (Mt 6:33)”6.

And this endeavor –which must be firm and resolute– to configure our existences to that of Christ, imitating all the attitudes this entails, we must live in our Religious Family “with a particular style”7 –as the Directory of Consecrated Life says–, which implies on our part a “serious, not sentimental, spirituality”8 (which, as you know, is one of the non-negotiable elements attached to our charism). That is, it is a distinctive and most noble characteristic of our spirituality “to transcend the merely sensible, and to be ready to pass through the dark nights”9 in order to attain this divine transformation.

This non-negotiable element attached to our charism, that of a “serious spirituality,” is non-negotiable precisely because it is what sets us apart against the world, and although it has often generated and still generates rejection, it is what has allowed us to present a living Christianity and has been a source of abundant spiritual fruits and of vocations (and also of numerous crosses and persecutions, since it is in frank opposition to a sentimental or, even more, an empty spirituality). And, therefore, everything that does not correspond to it, everything that tries to diminish its decisive force or draws us away from it, must be set aside as something foreign to our charism.

To put it positively: it is because of this distinctive and most singular note of our charism that, for example, we practice the Spiritual Exercises and that we are formed –as our Constitutions wisely and clearly indicate– “with the doctrine of the great masters of the spiritual life, especially: St. Augustine, St. Thomas Aquinas, St. John of the Cross, St. Teresa of Jesus, St. Ignatius of Loyola, St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, St. Thérèse of the Child Jesus, and of all the saints of all times whom the Church proposes as exemplars so that we may imitate their virtues”10. That is, always seeking to be formed in the school of the great mystics and doctors of the Church.

Within this framework, today I would like to highlight the figure of St. John of the Cross (and I have chosen him because, as you know, this year marks the 475th anniversary of his birth), since his doctrine is explicitly quoted nine times in our Constitutions and Directory of Spirituality, and as many more times in the rest of our Proper Law11. From this we have more than enough elements to infer that the doctrine of this Holy Doctor of the Church is a most certain guide for finding and uniting oneself to the Triune God –which is not a sensible reality but a purely spiritual one, and requires a great faith which is purified in the tribulations or nights of the soul–.

We can sum up his doctrine on this point in a well-known quotation of St. John of the Cross, in which he says that “to enter into this divine union, everything that lives in the soul must die, little and much, small and great, and the soul must remain without craving for all of it and as detached as if it did not exist for the soul nor the soul for it”12. Without this, he clarifies elsewhere, there cannot be the “true and total transformation of the soul in the three Persons of the Most Holy Trinity”13, and therefore we could not speak of the “transfigured existence” we mentioned at the beginning.

Such a transfiguration is certainly a grace of God, but like all graces, it must be implored with prayer and sacrifice, and one must likewise dispose oneself to receive it, “keeping oneself in solitude for God”14, says St. John of the Cross. Therefore, it will not be given unless we have intimacy and familiarity with God, which is acquired, exercised and nourished principally in prayer, in contact with Christ in the soul.

It is very important for everyone (for those who have been in religious life for a short time as well as for those of us who have already been in it for some years) to read and reread the doctrine of St. John of the Cross and to become steeped in his teachings in such a way that they come, as it were, to govern our life of prayer; to illuminate our nights; to serve us as a criterion for interpreting God’s action in our souls (and even in the souls of others, should it fall to us by office or by charity to do so); that his teachings may help us to moderate our spirits in consolations; even serve as an incentive in the practice of charity in community life –hence that “where there is no love, put love, and you will draw out love”15– and in everything “help us to work at stripping ourselves of all that is not God for love of God”16.

You who by vocation “dedicate yourselves to God alone in solitude and silence”17 must –and here I quote words of the Holy Doctor taken from our Proper Law–: “Live as if there were in this world nothing but God and [your soul], so that your heart may not be detained by any human thing”18. But rather leave everything, even “your skin and all the rest for Christ”19. Because what is greater and nobler than God?

It is precisely from this doctrine of St. John of the Cross that the wise and most practical teaching contained in number 68 of our Constitutions derives, where it deals with the vow of poverty, which invites us not to be concerned: “with the esteem and good opinion of men, with health and bodily strength, with the posts or offices that may be given to us or taken away from us, with the prosperous or adverse events that may befall us, with dying young or old.” But rather to have our center, our “nest”20, in God the Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

An author says, commenting on the drawing of the Mount of Perfection made by the Saint: “If we foolishly entertain ourselves with the goods of earth, which is what usually happens; or foolishly entertain ourselves with the goods of Heaven, we are wasting our time. We do not arrive where we ought to arrive, ‘because when you dwell upon something, you cease to reach the all.’ This fits perfectly with what Jesus wants of us, as we have seen in the two standards: supreme spiritual poverty, enduring reproaches and contempt, in order to arrive at supreme humility. For the one who truly seeks this: ‘Nothing wearies him from above and nothing oppresses him from below, because he is at the center of his humility.’”

He goes on to say: “If we were capable of forming religious women who truly love the Cross, the world could change. But as long as we do not have souls who really follow Christ crucified and want to be crucified with Christ, we will do things, often external things, which may have some value. But we will not do the most important of what we have to do, which is to attain holiness and spread the holiness of Christ throughout the whole world.”

2. Four spiritual counsels

For this, St. John of the Cross gives four spiritual counsels (or pieces of advice)21, which we can summarize in four words:

a. Resignation: “living in the monastery as if no other person lived there. That is, without meddling either in word or in thought in the affairs of others; and even if the world should collapse, not wanting to notice it or interfere in it, in order to keep the tranquility of the soul.” This is what we mean when we say “avoid worrying uselessly about things that are none of my concern.” Avoiding frivolous thoughts, thoughts of the past, judging one’s neighbor, etc., which only serve to interrupt the interior silence that must reign in the soul. Notice, then, how important it is to keep the peace of the soul and the vigilance we must have in this regard, because this is a sign that one is struggling to eliminate contrary loves and it is a sign of self-possession22.

b. Mortification: knowing “that we have come to the convent for no other reason than to be worked upon and exercised in virtue.” In this regard an author says that one must avoid “seeking heaven without putting the things of earth in order, always going about seeking consolations, seeking to be understood, or in prayer seeking to feel good. Claiming securities that cannot be had here; trying to understand mysteries that exceed our capacity.”

c. The exercise of virtue: “for which it is fitting to have constancy in carrying out the things of your Religious Order and of obedience, without any human respect, but solely for God. And for this never set your eyes on liking or disliking, but do it solely for God.” This is what St. Ignatius calls “holy indifference.” That is why you should never fail to do anything because you dislike it, but do it because it is the Will of God and you only want to please Him.

d. Finally, solitude: by which “it is fitting for you to consider all the things of the world as finished.” By which, the Saint himself clarifies, “it is not meant that for this reason one should fail to do the office one has, or any other that obedience may command, with all the solicitude possible and that may be necessary, but that one should do it in such a way that no fault clings to one in it, because neither God nor obedience wants this.”

And after these four counsels, the Holy Doctor concludes by saying: “Try to be continual in prayer… and go about always desiring God and with your heart set on Him, for which it is required not to leave the soul to any thought that is not directed to God, and in forgetfulness of all the things that are and happen in this miserable and brief life. In no way, says St. John of the Cross, seek to know anything except only how you will better serve God and better keep the things of your Institute.”

And since it could not be lacking in a “serious and not sentimental spirituality” and in this disposing oneself to attain a “transfigured existence,” devotion to Most Holy Mary, the tender and filial love we owe her, occupies a preponderant place and must be renewed daily –as St. John Paul II said– according to one’s own traditions, through a “spiritual union with the Virgin Mary, going through with her the mysteries of the Son, particularly with the praying of the Holy Rosary”23.

Dear Sisters: as St. John of the Cross wrote in one of his letters: Rejoice and trust in God!24 “On this road one must always keep walking in order to arrive, which means always removing desires, not sustaining them. And if they are not completely removed, one does not completely arrive… and so the soul will not be transformed in God”25.

“And this transformation must not be held to be impossible,” because nothing is impossible for God, and “because it is precisely so that we might reach this transformation that God created us in his image and likeness”26.

May Our Lady bless you.

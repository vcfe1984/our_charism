Homily preached by Fr. Carlos Miguel Buela to the students of the “Isabel la Católica” school on the occasion of the feast of their co-patroness St. Edith Stein, on August 9, 1999

On this very special day on which we celebrate this great woman who was St. Edith Stein, I want to leave you a lesson in a beautiful, artistic way, with a beautiful fable that Fr. Leonardo Castellani gives in his famous book Camperas. It is a fable that illustrates an enormous truth, a truth that the saint we celebrate today illuminated with her life. The fable is called “The Mire,” and it is about purity.

A boy is talking with his dad:

“ – Dad, can I go to the marsh?
-No.
-Why?
-Because I said no.
-I'm not going to drown. Don't you know the saying: How deep could the marsh be, if a cat crossed it at a gallop? It doesn't even come up to my knee.”

Then the dad begins to tell this boy the story of an Englishman, the manager of the “Los Tilos” sugar mill, who one day went into the marsh, broke the wing of a white heron, and went in to look for it. A white heron was worth 200 pesos back then. And this Englishman thought he knew all the secrets. The secrets of the woods, perhaps, “but the secrets of the marsh, the secrets of the mire, nobody knows to the bottom. They have no bottom. The farmhand he took with him was also an outsider. And they said: ‘The water comes up to our knees at most.’” (That is the first lie of the marsh. It will go no further; the water comes up to the knee.)

“The wounded heron went flapping its wings further and further in, further and further in. How wide is the marsh? Who can know? In the dry season it may be half a league or perhaps one. But in the rainy season the whole lowland floods. And when, on top of that, the Amores River overflows, who can know the leagues of water and mud that stretch beneath the green and lying mantle of water hyacinth that covers it? It all fills with rushes and bulrushes, so that it looks like a field of oats. A lovely field. In the peace of the quiet afternoon, the sun varnishes it and the wind gently ripples it. Up above, everything is beauty and charm. The white and purple flowers. The pink flamingos, which also look like great living flowers. The ducks, the gray herons, the jabirus. A red-breasted bird that rises chirping and flies in joyful circles. A little blue puddle here and there, where the sky is painted. And beneath all that beauty, the mud, stinking, who knows how many meters of mud. That is what vice is like. That is what a vice is like, one that you do not know yet.

But the Englishman was wearing high boots, and the heron was close by, tempting his greed. How lovely the little white heron, delicate and graceful! The Englishman, who was stubborn, set his heart on it. And on and on they go, at times in two spans of mud, and at times over almost dry ground, which reassured them. That is how it is: that is the diabolical lie of the swamp. That is how it also goes…” with vice. (It is the lie: yes, one sinks a little at times, but then one gets out. One goes to confession, it seems one is safe. But one remains in the occasion of sin.)

“-Did they catch it, Dad, the little heron?
-I don't know. What does that matter? Suddenly they came to a patch of reeds and there they stepped on firm ground and looked around. The farmhand said:
-Let's go back, boss.
And the Englishman said:
-What is that group of trees over there in front of us? Isn't it the bed of the Amores?
-It seems to me it must be, said the other.
-We have to cross the marsh and get over there, said the Englishman. It's close.

When the Englishman said ‘We have to,’ there was no turning him around. It's close! Haven't you seen what happens on the pampa, a little hut or some trees that seem to be very close, and one walks and walks and never gets there? That is the other lie of the swamp. (It is the lie of thinking that one will soon get out. It is another lie of the swamp.) Happiness is just over there, and one looks and longs, and runs and runs, and never, never gets there. And their legs sank deeper and deeper, and the mud was thinner and stickier.

-Let's go back, boss.

But the Englishman cursed and kept going. The trees were right there. Try to always step on top of the bulrushes. Careful, splash… A hidden pool, no need to be frightened, just a soaking… although even the cartridges in the cartridge belt have gotten wet, damn it. Now a detour; there is a dead cow over there, and an unbearable stench… Let's go back, boss.

Go back, yes. The boss's face was somber and bathed in sweat: but was going back still possible? (There comes a point, when one is in the swamp, when it seems that one is closer to getting out than to going back.) Night was rushing down upon them, and it was better to make a superhuman effort and reach, even if half-dead, the banks over there, which were now much closer than the ones back here. The decision was desperate, but no other could be thought of anymore, if indeed those heads, upon which Terror had already cast its trembling and treacherous shadows, were now in any state to think.

And indeed, the dreadful thing happened. They fell into a mud pit and sank up to their hips, and night fell upon them. The moon with its immense mantle of shimmering silver, and the stars that gaze at themselves in the waters as in a mirror of steel, watched impassively the flailing, the plunging, the falling sideways and face-down in the mud, the snapping of the vines they clung to, the despair of those who feel the ground give way inch by inch, the agony of living bodies swallowed by the slimy and fatal mouth of the lagoon. And they heard cries of horror and desperate curses.

-Kill me, boss. Do you have a cartridge left? Shoot me, please.

Then the cries ceased.

The marsh is evil and treacherous and an enemy of the human race. No one can understand the agony of that night. Suddenly, in the midst of the funereal splendor of the full moon, a voice abruptly began to sing: it was the farmhand Benito. He had gone mad. And then the diabolical marsh began to sing too. It sang perversely, with its thousands of crickets, of toads, of frogs, of whispering rushes, of moaning waters, with the voice of the thousands of suckers of mud that swallow. Glug, glu, glu, said the marsh. Haven't you seen mad Benito, the poor old man, how he howls every night of the full moon, feeling inside his brain the horrible song of triumph of the marsh? He says he heard it sing, that it said Glu, glu, that it laughed. And it is true that he heard it sing.

-How did he get out, Dad?

-He got out on his own. Nobody knows how he got out. From the swamp, if one does not get out on one's own –and it is a miracle of God–, no one else can get him out; you can't go on horseback or on foot, you can't go by boat…

-And the Englishman?

-And there we were, searching for them in the woods! We could never have imagined that they were in the marsh, after so many warnings… Until we heard the shot of the Englishman's Martini rifle, which had a powerful voice, it never occurred to us that…

-Dad, but what became of the Englishman?

-Look, do you see that rusty rifle in the corner? Once, three or four years later, there was a great flood of the Amores. Floating islands of water hyacinth were coming down the river, full of snakes, rushes and rubbish. On one of them (I found it) came that rifle, and beside it a skull split by a bullet. The rest of the Englishman, even his bones, the swamp had swallowed.

-Dad! –said the Gurí, turning his eyes away and shuddering all over–. How horrible! Why did you keep it?

-To show it to my children and tell them: all those who go deliberately into the swamp of lust have always said: I'll only go as far as there. The mud doesn't come up any higher than my knee.” (That is the great lie of the swamp.)

That is how it is with you. How many good girls! How many good young women I have known in my life! “Only as far as there.” First, not giving importance to knowingly entertaining bad thoughts, to watching bad television programs. Then, sometimes pushed by their own mothers, having a little boyfriend. The caresses begin, the kisses, the bad dances. Then they ask her for the proof of love. She becomes pregnant. Sometimes they force her to have an abortion. It is like the swamp. And it does not all happen at once, but little by little. And at some point they find themselves stepping on firm ground because they went to confession, and then they think that, well, now they are all right. But they remain in the swamp. And the swamp swallows everything.

That is why you should ask St. Edith Stein, your patroness, on this her feast day, for the grace to know how to defend that beautiful virtue, the most beautiful virtue of purity. And know how to defend it “now,” from now on and always! Above all with the difficulties that abound in this world in which we have to live, that beautiful –most beautiful!– virtue. Do not let yourselves be deceived by the flowers on top of the swamp. Yes, the pretty things… Ah, what a cute little nose he has… Oh, light-colored eyes! What a handsome boy! But the mud below… Don't be foolish. Think of your children. Think that you must know how to form honest families. Think that you must be young women with a sense of your own dignity. That you must not let yourselves be taken as objects, that you are persons and not things, and respect yourselves. Beware of the swamp. Once one goes in, only one can get oneself out, and only by a miracle. Because there is no help that avails, neither from your mother, nor from a priest, nor from the Sisters, nor from anyone. When one goes in, one thinks one will get out easily, and many times one never gets out again.

We also ask this grace of the Blessed Virgin.

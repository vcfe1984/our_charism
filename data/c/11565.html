Points preached during a spiritual exercise corresponding to numbers [164-168] of the book of the Spiritual Exercises

According to paragraphs [164-168] of the book of the Spiritual Exercises

[Introduction]

In order to gather and concentrate into a single beam all the rays of light that have shone from the Principle and Foundation up to now, St. Ignatius presents us with a luminous beacon to guide and orient us when the time comes to make the election. The beacon is none other than the consideration he calls the “three kinds of humility” [164-168], which is what we are now going to consider. The saint wants this consideration to be made “before entering upon the elections,” “considering it at intervals throughout the whole day, and likewise making the colloquies” [164].

The purpose of this contemplation is none other than “to seek and find the divine will in the disposition of one's life”1. This is a meditation that aims not only at the intellect or the will but at both, so as to fix firmly in the soul what the message of the standard of Jesus Christ is: the cross.

Unlike the previous contemplations, no fixed hour is indicated, nor are all the preludes that are ordinary in prayer prescribed. Nevertheless, we must keep the preparatory prayer, which is always the same:

[46] Prayer. The preparatory prayer is to ask grace of God our Lord, that all my intentions, actions and operations may be ordered purely to the service and praise of His Divine Majesty.

And we must keep the first prelude:

[150] 1st prelude. The first prelude is the history. This we will find between numbers [165] and [167] of the book of the Exercises.

1. Three kinds of humility

St. Ignatius presents us with three kinds of humility. All three are good, but each represents different kinds of dispositions (that is, how much I let God work in my soul), and therefore some bear more fruits of holiness than others. Now, it is important to understand that these fruits we are speaking of are not exterior things, but are principally interior. It is not a matter here of saying: well, I am a nun; if I become superior general I bore much fruit; or, I am a local superior; if I become provincial superior I bore much fruit; or, if I was a subject all my life I bore no fruit. Nor is it a matter of thinking that because one spent one's whole life as a subject one is super-humble, and that if one had to serve as superior one is not. The fruits of faith are not measured that way; they are measured by the degree of holiness. So it is a matter here not only of realizing what my interior dispositions are, but of determining myself to improve in order to order my life according to the divine will. Placing our intellect and will at the service of God with great courage and generosity, as we said at the beginning of these exercises; with “a great and very determined determination”2 to want nothing else but what is of greater service to God; taking care that “the eye of our intention”3 be simple, be upright, that there be no passion that might divert my election. That I may be capable of sacrificing whatever must be sacrificed, of renouncing whatever must be renounced for the glory of God, seeking in all things to do the will of God.

[165] 1st humility. The first kind of humility is necessary for eternal salvation, namely, that I so lower and so humble myself, as much as is possible for me, that in all things I obey the law of God our Lord, in such a way that even if they made me lord of all created things in this world, or for the sake of my own temporal life, I would not deliberate about breaking a commandment, whether divine or human, that binds me under mortal sin.

This kind of humility, St. Ignatius says, is necessary for eternal salvation. It is characteristic of those who keep away from evil by every means. That is, not only from mortal sin, but from venial sin, and who fight against their imperfections. So to speak, they are the ones who strive to diminish the voluntariness and the number of daily imperfections, of the small infidelities to the will of God into which we fall day after day (give examples). That is, they are souls who are concerned with correcting, or with effectively combating, their own defects. They are the ones who, with the will of the third class of men, have decided to walk the way of perfection.

Notice that St. Ignatius says that they are those who obey the law of God in everything, in such a way that even if they made them lords of all created things in this world and even if they threatened them with losing their bodily life, such persons have made the “formal decision not to make pacts, not to compromise, not to capitulate, not to negotiate, not to concede, nor to make deals with the spirit of the world”4. They are those who, as long as they are allowed, are determined to do every good with fervor, to fulfill their office completely; even when they are criticized, even when their intentions are misinterpreted, they want to do good, to act according to the will of God, because otherwise, they understand, they would fail God.

[166] 2nd humility. The 2nd is more perfect humility than the first, namely, if I find myself at such a point that I do not want or incline more to having riches than poverty, to wanting honor than dishonor, to desiring a long life than a short one, provided it be equally for the service of God our Lord and the salvation of my soul; and, with this, that not for all creation, nor even if they were to take my life, would I deliberate about committing a venial sin.

These are souls who, besides having left sin and correcting their defects, seek to practice generously the virtues of self-annihilation, namely: “humility, justice, sacrifice, poverty, suffering, obedience, merciful love…, in a word, to take up the cross”5.

Such persons have conscientiously applied themselves to embracing spiritual poverty; they have truly determined to submit themselves to the divine will, and “they live as if there were nothing in this world but God and it (the soul)”6. In such a way that neither “for all creation nor even if they were to take its life” would they consent to the slightest infidelity or offense against God.

Our Constitutions describe them, saying that they are those who have “uprightness and severity, so as to act solely seeking to please God, together with kindness and meekness, so that all may perceive that he acts moved by charity; likewise, there shine in them magnanimity and fortitude, to begin great works in the service of God and to persevere to the end in carrying them out, bearing the weaknesses of many, without faltering before flattery or threats and remaining above the ups and downs of fortune or failure, having the soul ready to receive death, if necessary, for the good of the Institute in the service of Jesus Christ”7.

[167] 3rd humility. The 3rd is most perfect humility, namely, when, including the first and second, and the praise and glory of the Divine Majesty being equal, in order to imitate and be more actually like Christ our Lord, I want and choose poverty with Christ poor rather than riches, reproaches with Christ filled with them rather than honors, and I desire more to be esteemed as worthless and a fool for Christ, who was first held to be such, than to be thought wise or prudent in this world.

This degree of humility is most perfect. In the words of St. John of the Cross it is “the highest humility”8, which is precisely “to be reduced to nothing”9, which he identifies with “freedom of spirit”10.

This is the nun who preaches the gospel without many words; who, when she speaks of suffering, knows what it is to suffer; who, when she speaks of renunciation, knows what it is to renounce; who, when she speaks of martyrdom, knows what martyrdom is; and when she speaks of the love of God −blessed is she− knows what love is. This degree of humility makes a person coherent between what she preaches and what she does. These are the ones who truly fulfill that exhortation of St. John Paul II which our Proper Law does not hesitate to make its own and which says: “Demonstrate by the depth of your convictions and by the coherence of your conduct that Jesus Christ is our contemporary”11.

You see, then, that it is not a matter of having only deep convictions or of only carrying out exterior works; both things go together and are a demonstration of the faith and charity that animate them. Those who possess this third degree of humility are the ones who dispose themselves for what is most perfect, because they have removed from themselves “completely whatever is repugnant to and not in conformity with the divine will”12. But not only that; they even want and choose poverty, reproaches, and even becoming “fools for Christ”13. Those who have this third degree of humility “will be led here and there, they will be laughed at and held to be dull, backward and even feeble-minded; nevertheless, they will know how to bless those who curse them and will laugh along with those who laugh at them and mock them like children who do not understand, and even when they are beaten, persecuted and martyred, they will give thanks to God who found them worthy”14. That is, they are not content with the holiness attained, with doing good, but have desires for greater holiness, for heroic holiness. They always seek what is most perfect; they are the ones who truly make “offerings of greater worth.” They are “God's unconditionals,” unconditional in their self-giving, in sacrificing whatever it may be, in serving God in whatever it may be and as God wills.

An Irish Jesuit priest wrote during his spiritual exercises something that seems to me can very well be applied to women religious too. It goes like this: “God wants me not to be content with the life of a mediocre religious; He wants me to wage incessant war on myself, on my passions, inclinations, bad habits, to subdue and break my will, to mortify it in all things. For this I must strive to cut away every comfort from my life, to choose what is hard, to go against my natural inclination and to renounce the life of self-pleasing I have led until now. The motive for doing so is the immense, deep and true love of the Heart of Jesus for me. Will I be able to do this for five, ten, twenty years? Will I be able to lead such a long crucified life? Jesus asks me only to do it for this day, which passes so quickly, and with it the memory of the small suffering and mortification endured. Once it has passed, it is over. But the reward is eternal.” This and no other is the humility asked of us; it is the humility proper to the true missionary of the Religious Family of the Incarnate Word; because it is solid, because it remains unchangeable before the ups and downs of fortune or failure; because it seeks to do the will of God in everything, whether in the most adverse circumstances or in the most favorable.

2. Third kind of humility

This virtue of humility, while it is essential for every Christian, is of capital importance for women religious. I would say that it is inherent to the condition of a religious. To the point that one cannot be a religious and not be humble. Because women religious have consecrated their lives to serving God and neighbor. If they do not serve, then there is a great contradiction between what they believe they are and what they are in reality. Being haughty, seeking to stand out, not serving others (and of course for this they look for a thousand pretexts), promoting oneself among others by boasting of EVERYTHING one does –to the point that they seem never to have read that phrase of the gospel that says: do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing–, wanting to have the last word in everything, never consulting, “commanding imperiously”15, as St. John of the Cross says (meaning with pride, with high-handedness), and thousands more examples we could cite, take us away from, or at least put in great danger, the humility that must characterize us and that must characterize you especially.

A Carmelite, Ana de Jesús Lobera, who was a directee of St. John of the Cross, bears witness to the saint's humility. It is a very short example, but it can serve to illustrate what we have been saying. The Carmelite nun says: “I also remember that once, when he was present, I began to praise him before some persons of rank, and I began to say that he had been prior or subprior of a certain convent, and he ‘brought up, with greater salt [with greater emphasis], that in that very place he had been the cook. As if fleeing from vainglory and humbling himself’”16. Do you see where this is going? You must not think too highly of yourself. “Those who have responsibilities in the community must flee from the temptation of considering themselves ‘great’ and of making their authority felt”17. “The humble one is the one who hides in his own nothingness and knows how to abandon himself to God”18: that is how the saint categorically defines the humble person. Something to bear in mind.

On the other hand, notice that the expression “third kind of humility” appears explicitly in three of the four books written for you, and the word “humility” appears eighty times in those same works. If we take into account our Proper Law, in the Constitutions and the Directory of Spirituality alone the words humility/humble appear some thirty-five times. What do you think? Is this topic of humility important or not?

“The third degree of humility is to set out really to attain perfection, that is, to embrace the Cross: ‘Nothing, nothing, nothing, nothing, nothing. And on the Mount nothing,’ in order to reach the Supreme Good, which is God”19. Notice that Father says to set out really, not in a utopian way, not when I make my perpetual vows, not when I celebrate 50 years of perpetual vows, but here and now: really; in spite of my crises, in spite of my spiritual dramas, in spite of whatever… It is a determining of oneself here and now, in these exercises, not in next year's, not when I get a better spiritual director, or when I am sent to another assignment. But here and now, as I am, whatever the state of my soul, to set out. And the first step is simply to be humble. To know and acknowledge our misery. And to be ready to renounce those things, those ways, those places that make me feel so comfortable; those people who make me feel loved, valued, who consult me; those tastes, those offices that give me so much security; those defects to which I cling so much and which so often hold me back, take me away, or distract me from the summit I must reach. It also means continuing to do good, even though others envy me, criticize me, whether I feel like it or not; without becoming sad because others push me a little to one side, without engaging in the thankless task of keeping count of how much I do and how much others do… to embrace the cross, to know how to abandon oneself to God.

Now, I want it to be well understood that the self-denial to which St. John of the Cross invites us in his works, and Christ above all in his gospel, is not a denying oneself for the sake of denying oneself; it does not consist in programming a life of mortification –as if it were a matter of drawing up a fixture list of the penance you are going to do each day– but of opting totally (radically) for Jesus Christ. Is that clear?

I suppose that by now you will have realized this, but I mention it anyway: climbing that summit, reaching the highest humility, is a matter of time and effort, as is acquiring any virtue or habit. The concrete way to proceed –the Saint advises– is to accept the nothingness of one's own being before God20, that is, “not only holding one's own things as nothing, but with very little satisfaction in oneself”21. From there springs the sincere desire to be taught and guided by others22. And he also says that it is a sure sign of true humility to accept one's own limitations and imperfections. Humble souls, “in the imperfections into which they see themselves fall, bear with themselves in humility, and with gentleness of spirit and loving fear of God” hope in Him23. They are those who hold others to be better and do not think themselves ‘something’ because of the things they do or are entrusted to do, or because of the academic achievements they have had, because, since they are humble, they know how much God deserves and how little is all that they do for Him. Humble souls do not think themselves anyone's teachers, because they never think they get anything right; so they ask for advice, they ask for help, they let themselves be taught, they consult… Nor do they go around noticing whether others do or do not do things, as if keeping count of what the other sister does and what she herself does. The humble sister does not despair over mistreatment either, over the lack of esteem of the other sisters or of the superiors; her world does not come crashing down if she suffers some injustice, if she receives some insult…

Now the one who is not humble, says the saint of Fontiveros, would like “to teach everything, and even when it seems they are being taught something, they themselves take the words out of the teacher's mouth as though they already knew it”24; I do not know whether you have ever come across someone like that. “Also some of these, the saint goes on to say, make little of their faults, and at other times become overly sad at seeing themselves fall into them, thinking that they should already be saints, and they become angry with themselves with impatience, which is another imperfection.” “Sometimes too, when their spiritual masters do not approve of their spirit and way of proceeding (because they want their things to be esteemed and praised), they judge that they do not understand them, or that they are not spiritual, since they neither approve of it nor go along with it. And so they then desire and try to deal with someone else who suits their taste.” “In general they are enemies of praising others and friends of being praised, and sometimes they even seek it.” The Saint treats all this in the first book of the Dark Night. Hence St. John of the Cross says that “whoever trusts in himself is worse than the devil”25.

True humility is intimately linked to self-knowledge and has its concrete expression in spiritual nakedness, which leads straight to the exercise of charity26.

In this regard, Fr. Segundo Llorente, a Jesuit, missionary in Alaska, in some talks he gave to Carmelite nuns about this “setting out really” (or concrete practice of spiritual nakedness), told them that “when you do not know what to confess, be sure that you fail in the following”; there are four things, pay attention:

1st. «Practically contenting oneself with mediocrity in the spiritual life», that is, not aiming higher, not aspiring to more, not wanting to distinguish myself more in the service of God, not loving God with all the strength I ought, not supernaturalizing everything I do as I should… and not doing all that amounts to contenting oneself with mediocrity in the spiritual life, all right?

2nd. «Interior irreverences in dealing with God». Everyone falls there, don't they? Because if we really realized, fully realized, whom we are dealing with in Holy Mass or in communion and those things… well, we would be, like… trembling with emotion, in a state of trembling tenderness before God… and instead, we take it for granted and go yawning to communion… Well, not yawning, but we go carelessly, thinking about So-and-so or What's-his-name, or thinking about who knows what, instead of thinking what we ought to think; so that is what is called interior irreverence; outwardly we are very prim and proper, very pretty indeed… inwardly we are off wool-gathering in the hills, aren't we? Now you have two accusations.

3rd. «Failing in interior silence». Do you know what it is to fail in interior silence? We fail in interior silence when we have frivolous thoughts, silly thoughts, vain thoughts, thoughts about the past, thoughts of when we went to school, of that time we fell into the river, of that time we went out dancing…, and through lack of interior silence God our Lord does not speak to us as He would like to speak to us, because He finds us busy… He wants to speak to us, but we are busy. What are we busy with? Well, with those thoughts.”

Silence is not a minor topic; our Proper Law gives it great importance. And here comes the interesting part, pay attention:

4th. «Worrying uselessly about things that are none of my concern». Because you are not dead yet… Are you dead already? You haven't died yet, have you?… To die, 1st, you must want it, you have to want it… 2nd, you go to the church… and you begin to die to everything. You die:

  • to your homeland, because if they send you to Alaska, or send you to Tanzania or to Canada, you are ready to leave Italy, aren't you? Well then, you die to your homeland. And to those who have already been a long time in this or another country we can also say: ‘you are ready to leave your mission country, aren't you?’
  • you die to your language. [Several here died to their own language long ago] If they send you abroad you have to learn another language. Are you ready to learn another language? Japanese or Arabic or English or something like that? Are you ready to sacrifice Spanish?… Each one of these deaths is a formidable blow to the head of selfishness, eh?…
  • you die to your family, which means that you must be completely detached. You must love your family very much, eh?, but you must leave it, all right? Some are terrified of going to a remote mission place because they will be far from their family. What kind of missionary is that?
  • then you die to your own will. Why, that is very easy, says Fr. Llorente, and in a couple of lines he puts into practice the doctrine of St. John of the Cross on humility that we have just mentioned. He says: “Look, all you have to do is the following: never do anything because you like it, never fail to do anything because you dislike it. Do it because that is the will of God the Father; everything you do, you do to please God, and with that you no longer have a will of your own. Well, what things does God want?… He wants all the things the Rule commands; God wants that; the virtues, the practice of the virtues, God wants that; silence, union with God. What is it that God wants?… Well, the soul develops a kind of radar to see, even if there is a very thick fog at an enormous distance, even if it is the darkest night, with which it sees the heavens coming from afar; it already sees coming from a thousand kilometers away what offends God and what pleases God… eh? You do things to please God. Everything you do, you do because God wants it; you go to bed because God wants it, you eat because God wants it. You do everything for the glory of God, and when death comes to you, you say: I am dying because God wants it… I live because God wants me to live… and if you fall ill, you say: because God wants it, like that… And when you supernaturalize everything, when you do everything, since you have done it for the glory of God, you have died to your own will… It is not impossible, is it? Well then, get to work…
  • then you die to companionship. You are here with the sisters God brings you, not with those you would have wanted to have. But there are times when you get a superior who is favorable to you, or a sister who is truly like your sister, or a chaplain or spiritual director or parish priest who is truly a father to you or with whom you work very well; now, if God takes you away from those people, well then, die to the consolation of good company too.
  • you die to spiritual consolations. You cling to the consolations God gives you. To the tears He gives you, cling tightly to them, and if God takes them away from you, if God wants you to go through those purifications, nights of sense and night of the spirit, do not complain, eh? And say to the Lord: Lord, if you reprove me, you are loving me… The more arid and dry I am, the more you love me. That way, with that, the aridity is easier to get through… When the devil persuades you that you are already damned, that these are the trials of the purification of the spirit, say to the Lord: here Satan does nothing but tell me that I am damned, but I do not believe him… You say it to Him, eh? And so with that you are now stripped of everything and dead to everything…
  • Ah, of course! There is reputation; you must die to reputation. It makes no difference to you whether you are abbess or cook, does it? One office is the same to you as another, isn't it?… Are you not yet dead to offices?… reputation, occupations, companions, your own will, your language, your homeland, your family and spiritual consolations… to all that you must die. And when you have died to this, then you are dead. And then afterwards you get up dead and start walking dead. Is that clear?… Until after three or four days you rise again… and once you rise again… die again! Do you know how many times you have to die and rise again? Seventy times seven!… And if death catches you dying and rising, dying and rising, you are like the Lord, who was on the way to Calvary, which is death, and there: Come, blessed of my Father… And that's it…

St. John of the Cross says –Fr. Llorente goes on– that God draws such souls with such force, and the souls draw on God with such force, that the flesh can no longer hold the soul and the soul goes out, and that is dying of love… And those souls no longer pass through purgatory, because they are already purified by the love of God. And they do not pass through judgment, because they are already judged beforehand and approved. Well, that gave me a lot of light… When I find myself in some difficulty or some setback comes along, or a snub or something, I say: Well, what does all that matter to me, if I am going to die of love?… What difference does it make to me… whether I drown, or die in the hospital, or die in bed, whether it snows, whether it is cold, whether I miss the train or don't miss it; what difference does it make to me, if I am to die of love? What difference does it make to me! Perhaps it may help you”27.

[Peroration]

That is why now, when you go to the meditation, let each one think about what it is she must die to in order to be more humble. And make the decision, or renew the decision, to live according to the third class of men and according to the third kind of humility. Ask God and Our Lady for great desires for reproaches and contempt in order to imitate Jesus more! Say with Blessed Mary of Jesus Crucified:

“If I do not taste your sufferings, O my Jesus,

I live sad and sorrowful.

Wound me, then, for I have already given you my soul.

And if you do me this good, my God,

I will see clearly that you love me well”28.

[Colloquies]

In this case St. Ignatius recommends with singular emphasis in note [168]:

[168] Note. Thus, for one who desires to attain this third humility, it is very profitable to make the three colloquies of the classes of men already mentioned, asking that our Lord be pleased to choose him for this third, greater and better humility, in order more to imitate and serve Him, if it be equal or greater service and praise to His Divine Majesty.

These are the ones found in [147] of the book of the Exercises.

[Examen of the meditation]

We must not omit making the examen of the meditation for the space of fifteen minutes, as the holy Jesuit recommends.

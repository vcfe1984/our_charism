“Ad maiora natus sum”

St. Stanislaus Kostka

Pope St. John Paul I, speaking from the balcony of St. Peter’s Basilica on the day after his election, recalled, among other things, that in the conclave on August 26, when it was already becoming clear that he himself was to be the one elected, the cardinals beside him whispered in his ear: Courage! Very likely this was the word he needed at that moment, and it remained engraved on his heart, since he recalled it right away the following day.

So we too, like any Christian, need to hear this word of encouragement from time to time, above all when situations come upon us that require a “robust will”1.

Naturally, it is not only the soldier who exposes himself to the risk of losing his life in a war to defend his homeland who is brave. It is well known that in time of peace, too, we need fortitude. Thus did St. Charles Borromeo, for example, who during the plague in Milan continued to exercise his pastoral ministry among the inhabitants of that city with great courage. Or St. John of the Cross, whom, when he was imprisoned in Toledo, they wanted to ‘buy’ by offering him a good library, a good cell, a priorate and even a golden cross. But he answered categorically: “He who seeks Christ stripped bare has no need of golden jewels”2. In the same way, how many times throughout history and today have we seen countless religious, priests and missionaries who, so as not to ‘sell themselves’ –even when they were promised advantageous ways out–, so as not to renounce their principles, so as not to let their freedom be trampled, in other words, so as not to be tributaries3, have known how to stand firm, with great courage and great fortitude, even in spite of the many threats they suffered and the consequences that such a decision held in store for them. That is why it is not only on battlefields that we must look for brave men.

Each one of us, as members of this dear Institute, but even more by the simple fact of being Christians, has received the noble office of “confessing the faith of Christ with courage and fortitude”4. But this office, common to every Christian, takes on special connotations and even greater relevance in the case of religious. Why? Because our condition as religious requires a radical and total self-giving, because ours “must be a courageous and clear witness”5 to the message of Christ, which, if authentic, must necessarily contradict the world, and “misunderstandings, rejections, persecutions”6 are bound to come upon us… and, therefore, all of this requires greater courage.

At this point, then, we would like to recall the example of St. Stanislaus Kostka, Patron of youth, whose tomb is in the church of Sant’Andrea al Quirinale in Rome. There this Saint, by nature very sensitive and frail, and yet very courageous, ended his life at 18 years of age. Fortitude led him, who came from a noble family, to choose to be poor following the example of Christ, and to place himself exclusively at His service. And although he faced great opposition in his surroundings, with great love and great firmness at the same time he succeeded in carrying out his resolve, summed up in the motto “Ad maiora natus sum” (I was born for greater things). He arrived at the Jesuit novitiate after making the journey from Vienna to Rome on foot, fleeing from those who were following him and wanted to dissuade him from his purpose by force7.

We too “were born for greater things,” and we must know how to overcome the fear that comes upon us in situations of threat, of silent but ill-intentioned persecution, when we expose ourselves to the risk of unpleasant consequences, of suffering even greater insults, and even in the face of the possible loss of material goods or of going to prison. We were born for greater things!

1. A letter of St. John of Ávila

This is the very thing St. John of Ávila sought to persuade the religious preacher Fr. Alonso de Vergara, O.P., of in a letter8 that he wrote to him consoling him for a persecution that had arisen against him. And this letter seems to us so full of value, because of the wise doctrine it contains, and so fitting for those of us who have been called to serve the Truth9, that we shall quote some parts of it and offer some commentary, in the hope that the reflective reading of these pages may impress even more deeply on our hearts that the resounding idea continues to be: to sacrifice oneself!10, because we were born for greater things!

A short time before, Fr. Alonso de Vergara had written a missive to the Doctor of Ávila, but in a tone quite opposite to that of the letter to which we are going to refer. In that first letter –to which St. John of Ávila had not replied– the Dominican preacher was consoled, and in this one that we are now going to consider, he is extremely desolate. That is why the first lines the Saint writes to him say: “To anyone who wished to know what man is when God helps him and lavishes gifts on him, I would show a letter that your grace sent me some days ago; and to anyone who wished to know the weakness of man when he goes on his own, I would show this one that you have now sent me.” The same happens to us, and that is why, as we have been taught, we must apply to ourselves the rules of discernment of St. Ignatius that we have so often preached to others: “7th rule: let one who is in desolation consider how the Lord has left him in trial in his natural powers, so that he may resist the various agitations and temptations of the enemy; for he can do so with the divine help, which always remains to him, even though he does not clearly feel it; because the Lord has withdrawn from him his great fervor, abundant love and intense grace, while leaving him sufficient grace for eternal salvation”11. And that other one which says: “8th rule: let one who is in desolation strive to remain in patience, which is contrary to the vexations that come upon him, and let him think that he will soon be consoled, taking diligent measures against such desolation”12.

“Oh, God help me […]! What a great abyss of miseries man is, and how few things knock him down, and how quickly he changes, like thin ash before a great wind! […] Your reverence asks me whether I think you are alive or whether I count you among the dead, since I do not write to you. I answer that I do not forget you; but I was keeping my letter for this time, because at the other time it was not needed.” How great a truth the Saint has spoken here: what a great abyss of miseries man is, and how few things knock him down, and how quickly he changes, like thin ash before a great wind! We must accept that we are weak, that we are “nothing but sin,” as St. Catherine of Siena used to say, which requires humility on our part. How many religious are cast down by an ill-sounding word that has been said to them, by an apostolic failure –even having taken every precaution for the good outcome of the undertaking–, by a permission that has been denied them, by the many undeserved criticisms they receive from the very souls they shepherd, by the lack of support –on the part of superiors, of peers, of subjects–, etc. In short, all we can do is confess: how few things knock us down! That is why it is good to keep in mind the wise warning that our Proper Law gives us, this time through the pen of St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort: “Be very careful! Do not go believing –like proud and conceited devout people– that your crosses are great, that they are proof of your fidelity and a testimony of a singular love of God for you. This deceit of spiritual pride is very subtle and ingenious, but full of poison. Think rather that your pride and delicacy lead you to regard straws as beams, pinpricks as wounds, mice as elephants, a little word carried off by the wind –in reality a mere nothing– as an atrocious insult and a cruel abandonment… that delighted turning over and over of your own ills, that Luciferian belief that you are of great worth, etc.”13.

The Saint then continues with the answer he was reserving for this moment, and begins by pointing out to him that he is not alone, invoking for this purpose the example of St. Anthony: “St. Anthony complained to our Lord because during the battle he did not see our Lord, and [He] answered him that He was there. But He was watching how he fought, in order to make him reign14.” And immediately afterward he makes him see how trials, difficulties, the things that humble us, ‘are part of the program,’ are in some way inherent to our condition as Christians and are like the insignia of the religious and priestly state, and therefore one must not be frightened by them; that is why he makes him reflect thus: “Did your reverence think you would never have to walk alone, without a baby-walker and without another’s hand leading you by yours? And how, father, were you to learn to walk? Was it all to be eating children’s food, pap and milk? And how were you to become a perfect man? Oh, my father, if it were not that I see your reverence distressed, how gladly, hearing you complain and tremble, I would laugh, like one who hears a child crying and trembling because they have frightened him with a straw lion!” Does not the same thing happen to us? We see the name of the Institute outraged in a headline, we clearly realize that obstacles are placed in our way at every step, we are publicly wronged –always, of course, under the guise of good– and the temptation may come to us to be frightened, to want to flee, to be cast down or to lose heart. Do we not realize? The Master of Ávila tells us: “if your reverence, by the strength of God, has died to what worldly people adore, and this before their very eyes, are you astonished that they want to stone you? They adore honor, one’s own judgment, one’s own spirit, duplicity, lukewarmness, self-love, self-reliance15 and other similar idols they have made, which Moses –which spiritually means ‘the law of God’– calls abominable, senseless and most hideous things; but your reverence, who by the great mercy of our Lord lives as with your own lord, [that is,] with our God, and have truly adored not the vain idols of the world, which can give no good or salvation at all, but Him who with very great reason deserves to be adored. What wonder is it that there should be strife where there is such diversity of opinions and ends? But this strife is stirred up by the children of strife and suffered by the children of peace; the former biting like dogs, and the latter suffering and praying and loving like lambs; but, with the favor of God, the lambs will conquer the dogs, and even the wolves, for that is why God sent them, as lambs among wolves”16.

Why, then, are we amazed if we suffer setbacks because we are not “tributaries”17, if we do not mix the human with the divine –which is a kind of mixture with which no Christian understanding should clothe itself18–, if we treasure and defend the Constitutions and the sound traditions that form our own patrimony19, which is exactly what the Church herself has sanctioned as being ‘according to God and the Gospel.’ Why are we surprised that we are attacked if, when we have taken a firm position regarding the truth, those who prefer to ride the waves of popularity rather than dare to refute the triumphant error of the moment for fear of making enemies shout against us and look on us with scorn? Why are we surprised if, being poor and undertaking our humble works trusting only in Divine Providence, the rich and powerful look on us with suspicion? “What wonder is it that there should be strife where there is such diversity of opinions and ends?” It is for us, as St. John of Ávila says, to be children of peace, to suffer, pray and love like lambs, because they are the ones who will conquer.

The Saint then recalls the example of the people of the city of Gibeon, who had made a pact with Joshua, and how “five kings allied themselves to fight against them20; because it seemed to them a great loss to lose so great and royal a city, and that this favor and people should be added to Joshua, their enemy. And so have the demons and worldly people done with your reverence, seeing you give yourself to Jesus Christ. […] They already smell the strength God has given you to wound hearts with the word of God, and they weep a double lament for what they lose and Jesus Christ gains. Hence the contradiction in everything and from everyone; hence the combat of the five who join together as one, and with one voice say what they say and do what they do.” We have heard the same from those who lament and are even appalled that we have vocations, and many vocations by the grace of God; so they begin to say that there is no selection, that we brainwash them, that we recruit them with tricks; they look on scandalized at the fact that our religious, even with the bare minimum of means, undertake great works or launch out to do missionary work in places where no one wants to go, and so, “all magnanimity seems to them pride, all heroism seems to them extremism, all generosity seems to them exaggeration”21, and they obstruct everything with false arguments. Hence the contradiction in everything; hence the combat of those who join together and with one voice say what they say and do what they do.

But this too is ‘part of the program,’ and St. John of Ávila points it out to us with great realism: “Is your reverence perhaps the first to be afflicted because he went over to Christ? Or will you be the first to be abandoned among those who suffer for Christ? Do you not see, my father, that the cause for which we are persecuted is not ours, but God’s? Do you not see that His honor is at stake in it? Tell me: why did you formerly have so many at peace with you and now so many opponents? Without any doubt the cause has been that your reverence has shown yourself to be on the side of Jesus Christ. For what king would there be who would not take it as a very great insult that, merely because someone had offered himself to him as a servant, and he had received him, there should be someone who despised and persecuted him? Is it not, perhaps, to despise and dishonor the king to persecute one who wishes to serve him, only because he came to live with him? Does this not concern the king? Is it not his affair? It is, certainly. And that is why David said: Awake, Lord, and defend Your cause. Look, Lord, and remember the affronts that this insolent and ignorant people does to You every day22. It is God’s cause, and they are God’s dishonors, those that are done to the servants of God; just as it is God’s honor and His cause when we do good to His little ones23 and honor them.” In a few words: we signed up for this, and therefore persecutions –whatever they may be, and from whomever they may come– should not seem extraordinary to us. Cervantes already said it: “if they bark, Sancho, it is a sign that we are riding on.” On the other hand, we are not the only ones who suffer, nor those who suffer most, nor the first nor the last who will suffer for the cause of Christ. That is why, instead of grumbling and becoming downcast, we must learn to see in this the great favor God is granting us: that of imitating Him better24 and of giving Him great glory whenever “what is said against us is false”25. But at the same time, we must “take great care not to turn our ills over and over”26. God Himself will defend His cause.

St. John of Ávila exhorts the preacher, cast down by intense and continual persecutions, not to fear, reminding him of what the Levite said “comforting the people of Judah, who were going up to war”: Thus says the Lord to you: Do not fear or be dismayed before this so great multitude; for the battle is not yours but God’s27. “And, therefore, –the Master of Ávila continues– it will not be you who fight, but only wait with confidence, and you will see the help of the Lord upon you28.” St. John of the Cross said something similar in one of his letters: “But, because it is fitting that we should not lack the cross, as our Beloved did not, even unto the death of love, He orders our passions in the love of what we most love, so that we may make greater sacrifices and be of greater worth. But all is brief, for all lasts only until the knife is raised, and then Isaac remains alive, with the promise of a multiplied offspring29”30. And therefore, at times it is a matter of knowing how to wait patiently for God to do His work, without anguish in trying to solve a situation that is out of our hands, that is, that does not depend on us, without wearing ourselves out and being overwhelmed in defending ourselves.

On this point, there is a text of St. Thomas Aquinas, in his Commentary on the Gospel of St. Matthew, in which he asks why Jesus remained silent before the High Priest. Perhaps it is a text that many of you know, but we consider it fitting to point it out again as a criterion for knowing how to conduct ourselves in similar situations. The Angelic Doctor answers: “He did so for three reasons. First, to teach us to be cautious; for He knew that whatever He said, they would use it to slander Him; and in such cases, before those who lay snares for us, we must be silent, as is said in Ps 39[38]:2: I said to myself: ‘I will guard my ways, so as not to sin with my tongue; I will put a bridle on my mouth while the wicked man is before me’. Another reason: that it was not the moment to teach, but to endure patiently, and thus is fulfilled what Is 53:7 says: he was oppressed and he humbled himself and did not open his mouth. Like a lamb led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that is mute before its shearers, he too did not open his mouth. The third reason: to teach us constancy when someone unjustly accuses us of something, as is said in Is 51:7-8: lend me your ear, you who know what is just, people who are mindful of my law. Do not fear the insults of men, and do not be dismayed by their outrages; for the moth will eat them like a garment, and the worm will eat them like wool. But my justice will be forever, and my salvation for generation upon generation”31.

Now, it is true that many times those who persecute us, or assail us with their outrages, sometimes seem not to realize the harm they do, and even think they are acting according to God. Pay attention to the answer that St. John of Ávila, with a wise pen, has left imprinted in this letter we have been quoting: “And if those who persecute think they do not offend God in it, what does this take away from my confidence, since the servants of God are expressly warned that they will be persecuted by people who believe they are rendering a great service32 in persecuting them? They suffer for God and because they drew near to God; and the persecution is against God. If the persecutors think otherwise, perhaps they lessen their guilt somewhat, but not our crown; and if they, deceived, think they are serving God, we, undeceived, know that we are serving God.”

Fr. Alonso de Vergara then complains that a certain person “carries and brings back” distorted information to a third party, which apparently brought him no small number of crosses. St. John of Ávila, a theologian of great depth and a true humanist, answers not as a spectator detached from the scene, but as one who knows the pain that slander causes, since he himself went to prison in 1531 because of a sermon of his that was misunderstood: “What do you care, father, about the opinions of blind men, since it is certified that the doctrine you preach is from God, and that the manner in which you preach it is from God, as appears from the fruit; and that the spirit you have received is from God, since it has made you keep the law of God and has freed you from the law of sin…? Let your reverence see that Wisdom admonishes you not to think little of the knowledge our Lord has given you33, nor to let yourself be carried away or overcome, having that knowledge; rather, dare with it to despise the vain idols; and find yourself so rich with the hidden treasure God has shown you that you do not consider it a loss to lose all you had in order to obtain it. Do not esteem God so little that you would wish to give little for Him, since God esteemed you so much that He did not wish to give less than Himself for you. You were loved on a cross; love on a cross. You cost Christ dearly, and He who won you gave birth to you with groans; do not wish to offer the Lord sacrifices that cost you no toil and pain in your sensuality.” Do you realize? If we did not preach the last things, if we were not Thomists, if we did not denounce progressivism, if instead of elevating cultures with the values of the Gospel we offered only technical solutions, if we fed ourselves and not the sheep; if instead of preaching the Truth we negotiated with it, trimmed it, avoided it, do you think they would attack us? No. We would be doing exactly what the world wants us to do. But we do not do it, and, by the grace of God –in spite of our misery–, our apostolates bear fruit: it is enough to see the numerous vocations, many of them coming from countries that lament the crisis of the local Church, the 101 priests that the Institute has given to the Church in these last 6 years, all that which is “certified to be from God” and which is what the world and its henchmen attack and persecute and obstruct and want to put an end to once and for all. That is why, aware that we carry this treasure in earthen vessels34, we must not let ourselves be overcome, we must not cease to proclaim that the Word became flesh35, even when the world laughs at us and throws stones at us and curses us36. We must not let ourselves be swayed by what others say about us in trying to make us give up our purpose. What does it matter! We were born for greater things!

“What greater honor, my father, than to suffer for Christ! True glory […]. Blessed is he for whom the reason he is mistreated is that he serves God and does not wish to give in to the vain entreaties and opinions of men. This is a business of love, and love is a manner or kind of war, and cowards are not admitted here, whom God commanded to be dismissed from among the men of war37.” For as our Proper Law says: “love that is not born of the Cross of Christ is weak”38.

But it is also true that when one “sees himself denigrated among his own”39, when those who ought to defend us are precisely those who insult us, when those who ought to listen to us are those who turn a deaf ear to our appeals, when it is those in whom we hoped to find protection who reject us and cast us out, who is there, however virtuous he may be, who does not complain at least interiorly? But notice what this giant of good spirit points out: “Why do you complain, father, of the words and esteem of men and the judgments of the blind? See that your witness is in heaven, watching you to see how you do40, who is to be the judge of proof and, upon proof, approves your doctrine and work. Well, if He vouches for you, what does it matter if all the rest condemn you?41. It matters little and is nothing that men without God condemn us in this life42. Let your reverence count it as nothing that such base and miserable judgments condemn you. What heed is to be paid to people who will grow old like everyday clothes43 and will be eaten by moths44?” Do we not meditate every year on the Principle and Foundation, where we read: “that we should not, for our part, want honor more than dishonor45? “Why tremble or faint over such small things, when our great Lord suffered such grave things for love of us and is to give us such abundant rewards here and there for our labors.” How can we not recall at this point that comforting phrase that our Proper Law places on the lips of our Most Sweet Savior: “I, your defender: why do you fear adversaries?”46.

We have been called to share in the labors of our Lord Jesus Christ; we say that “we want to imitate Him until we can truly say to others: Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ47, it is no longer I who live, it is Christ who lives in me48”49. Now, have we fought to the point of shedding blood50? The truth is that we are not yet companions and equals in labors with the Apostle, who said: I die every day51. “Can your reverence count persecutions, affronts, stonings, scourgings, prisons that you have undergone for Jesus Christ with St. Paul?52. Why are you so delicate a soldier, and why do you show yourself so weak, fighting on the side of Jesus Christ53 and having as your captain this Lord, to whom His Father gave a person and a countenance firmer than diamonds and flint54, so that no affronts, no insults, no blows could make Him turn back from what He had begun? Come, then, sir, let us leave this heaviness and weakness that holds us in its grip, and let us run with patience to the war that is set before us, always looking to Jesus Christ to take courage, who, just as He is the author of our faith, will carry it to its end and perfect it in us.”

Note that the Master of Ávila does not deny how arduous the struggle is, but he also warns us that as long as we persevere patiently in the struggle that God Himself prophesied to us as real –you will weep and lament55; in the world you will suffer tribulation56– no less real is the reward He promised us. “He commands you to carry the cross, He promises you an eternal reward; and if it is a hard word to suffer temptations with Him, it is most sweet to sit at His table with Him in His kingdom57. Oh father, and why should we go and sit at that table of the persecuted, the dishonored, the saints, the tempted and those put to the sword58, having ourselves suffered nothing? […] Let us bring something to glory in; let us bring some feat of love for our true Lover, so that our love may not be of word alone. Let us trample this viper of tribulation and press on, preparing ourselves for greater things”; because we were born for greater things!

The Saint then goes on to advise: “Offer, father, your life and honor into the hands of the Crucified, and make a gift of it to Him, for He will keep it safe, as He has done for others: I know whom I have believed, says St. Paul59. And it did not go badly for him. What is suffered is little and momentary60; and to whomever it seems great, it is because he is small in love and has false weights. Let him grow and he will eat, for this is food for the grown61. And even if His help is delayed, He will come and calm the sea.”

“Our Lord has wished to test you, not to abandon you; the mother has hidden behind the curtain and is listening to the child cry, who cannot bear to be without her; but she will come out, for her heart will not let her bear it, and she will take the child in her arms and give him milk, and he will be so happy that he will forget his past troubles, as if they had never happened. And many of those who now persecute you will follow you, according to the promise of our Lord Jesus Christ: Those who murmured against you will come to you62.” But at the same time Master John of Ávila warns that if the persecuted one were to turn back because of the persecution he suffers, he will be gravely accused on the day of judgment, especially by the persecutors, who will say to him: “‘If we persecuted you, we did not have knowledge; but you, who had it, would have done well not to abandon it; for if we had known what you knew, we would not have abandoned it because of the persecution of those who did not know it. You harmed yourself and us, because, if you had persevered in virtue, we would have come to the knowledge of it.’ And that is why, my father, –St. John of Ávila goes on to say– you must take strength in the Lord, and believe most certainly [that], if you persevere, et per Christum abundat tribulatio tua, ita per Ipsum abundabit consolatio tua63, and that the Lord will repay you with the gain of souls for what you lose in those other things in the eyes of worldly people.”

Persecution suffered over a long time, and the wearing down –humanly speaking– of physical strength through missionary labor, can take a toll on the missionary spirit. That is why, in the last paragraphs of the letter, in a tone that is always very human and compassionate, St. John of Ávila recommends to Fr. Alonso de Vergara some things that we too can put into practice if God ever grants us the favor of being able to suffer something for Him.

First recommendation: “a trip to Escalaceli or some similar place, where you might have time for yourself alone for a few days.” Our Proper Law, for its part, also has the same suggestion: “particularly when they see that a missionary has slackened in his spirit or shows signs of a certain weariness. A few months in another community or carrying out other activities, under the guidance and supervision of a qualified Superior, can restore to the missionary the spirit and strength that have perhaps been spent in apostolic battles”64.

Second suggestion: “and if this is not enough, I would like you to have the company of Father Fray Luis.” Our Directory also recommends that the missionary, during that time of prolonged rest, have the opportunity, whenever possible, to turn more easily to the company of good friends65.

St. John of Ávila’s third practical recommendation: the reading of Sacred Scripture. “To you
–the Lord Himself says– it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God, but to the others in parables66. Who are these ‘you’? To you, my disciples, who do not live gladly in this world and despise it, afflicted for my sake, made the refuse of this world67. […] And without this, reading is of no profit at all. It seems to me that, by reading St. John and St. Paul and Isaiah, they will soon come to know the Scripture […] I do not know what more to tell you, father, except that you should read these; and when you do not understand them, consult some holy interpreter on them, and especially read St. Augustine, Against the Pelagians and against others of that sect; and set a crucifix before you and understand Him in everything, because He is everything and all of them preach Him. Pray and meditate and study.” In this sense our Proper Law says that the time of rest “is not a time in which ‘nothing is done,’ but one in which the missionary can recover his physical energies, take up again his intellectual work, perhaps somewhat neglected, give witness of his missionary life, etc.”68

The Master of Ávila ends his letter to the persecuted preacher by telling him: “And do not weary yourself in standing up for yourself or in giving many excuses for your innocence, because the Lord says: You shall keep silent, and the Lord will fight for you69.”

2. Great confidence

St. John of Ávila was, according to Pope Benedict XVI, a promoter of “a spirituality of trust”70. Our Proper Law does the same throughout all its documents, exhorting us to have “total confidence”71 in the Incarnate Word.

In times of greater trial, and if we do not want to offend Christ, it is very fitting to cling to His goodness and trust in His infinite mercy. We would be very blind if, after so very many benefits that God has granted us, individually speaking and at the level of the Institute, we did not love the Incarnate Word with complete surrender, and our weakness would be very great if, after so many mercies as we have experienced, we did not trust in Him.

All the goods that God has been pleased to grant us are means to stir us to love Him more and, at the same time, a call to strive to trust more; because if God has given us all the goods He has given us in the past, has called us to follow Him more closely and has put us –so to speak– on this course, He will also grant us to finish it, because “God will take care of His estate, for it belongs to no other owner”72 and the Owner “will not abandon those who give themselves to Him with such confidence”73.

Now that we are beginning Lent, let us take the opportunity to devote ourselves to meditating unhurriedly and deeply on the Passion of our Lord, for that is where the infinite goodness of the Father is manifested, who “handed Him over for all of us; [and] from which there must spring up in us a limitless confidence in the overflowing generosity of the Father: How will He not give us all things with Him?74”75.

Christ suffered and died out of love for us! That is why let us say with the Master of Ávila: “let every doubt, then, go far away, every faintness of heart and every distrust; for as great as is the power of His Passion, so great are our merits, since it is ours, for He gave it to us. There I presume and trust, and there I mock my enemies; there I make my petitions to the Father, offering Him the Son; from there I pay what I owe, and have more than enough left over. And although my sorrows are many, there I find greater remedy and cause for joy than I find in myself cause for sadness”76.

We do God a great wrong if we do not trust fully in Him. St. John of Ávila would say: “we are worse than brutes!” Do we perhaps not believe that He will give us everything, and even more than we ask, after all He has already given us? “Shall we not believe that You will defend those whom You drew out of hell? Will You not feed those whom You took as children? Will You not teach the way to those whom, being astray, You set upon it? Will You not give what they ask for Your service to those to whom You gave many things when they were outside Your service, and, when they were offending, You defended them, and when they were fleeing from You, You followed them and brought them to Yourself, and cleansed them and gave them Your spirit, and filled their souls with joys, giving them the kiss of peace? And why all this? Surely so that they may believe that, since through Christ You reconciled them to Yourself when they were enemies, You will all the better keep them through Christ now that they are friends”77.

We must stamp on our hearts an immense confidence in God, even when we do not feel the sweetness of consolations. “Do we feel weak? Let us hope in God, and we will be strong. Do we not know what we must do? Let us trust in God, and light will be given to us, as Isaiah says: If anyone has walked in darkness and has no light, let him hope in the name of the Lord and lean upon his God78; and elsewhere it is written: Those who trust in the Lord will understand the truth79. Are we in tribulations? Let us hope in God, and we will be delivered, as God says through David: He hoped in me and I will deliver him80. In these words we must note that God asks no other merit in order to deliver us than to hope”81.

“Oh souls created for these grandeurs and called to them! What are you doing? With what are you amusing yourselves? Your aspirations are lowliness and your possessions miseries. Oh miserable blindness of the eyes of your soul, for you are blind to so much light and deaf to such great voices, not seeing that, while you seek grandeur and glory, you remain miserable and lowly, made ignorant and unworthy of such great goods!”82. We were born for greater things!

* * * * *

We need strong men, brave men! Let us persevere steadfastly in the struggle. Let us strive to keep our hearts in peace, without letting any event of this world disquiet us, since everything is to come to an end83. If we place all our love in Christ, there we will find peace even in the midst of the cruelest affronts, since He is the foundation of everything: all things were created through Him and for Him… all things hold together in Him84. For this reason our Proper Law affirms that “peace must be sought within, not outside, filling ourselves with useless worries and filling our hearts with resentment against everything around us, because the peace of our soul does not depend on the disappearance of exterior obstacles, but of attachment to sin”85. And if at times we feel the file of abandonment more harshly, we must remember that Christ is watching how we fight in order one day to make us reign.

May the Virgin Mother, who persevered immovable at the foot of the Cross, obtain for us the grace to persevere bravely and in peace, even though the storms grow fiercer, because we know in Whom we have trusted. May the company of our ever-loving and solicitous Mother fill us with great courage and always remind us that the sufferings of the present time are nothing compared with the glory that is to be revealed in us86.

Come, then! We were born for greater things!

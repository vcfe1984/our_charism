To contemplate and to bear witness

Directory of Consecrated Life, 234

It must be affirmed that the crisis in which religious life finds itself in our days has as its principal and preponderant cause the deviation from, the lack of, or the outright abandonment of an authentic and profound life of prayer.

The Holy Father himself already said it: “We are facing a ‘hemorrhage’ that weakens consecrated life and the very life of the Church”1. If this happens, something is not right in the souls of those religious, and it is very clear that this deeply affects the relationship of these souls with God.

And while it is true that there are many factors that contribute to this painful situation—such as, to give some examples, the ineptitude and “unscrupulous” irresponsibility of many in positions of authority, together with the lack of formation and discernment of religious leaders who lead their communities to embrace prayer practices conducive to an inadequate and at times even syncretistic spirituality—and so we hear of priests who practice tai chi2, religious sisters who take yoga courses, and some in the hierarchy of the Church who put courses of Catholic catechesis on a par with Zen courses—; nevertheless, the fundamental, deep and principal problem of religious life in our time is the lack of contemplative life, in the sense of a life of authentic prayer of union with Our Lord. It is sad to note, for example, how the names of the great mystics of Catholic spirituality, such as St. John of the Cross, St. Teresa of Jesus, St. Edith Stein or St. Thomas Aquinas (to name only some of the principal ones), are unknown in many of the houses of formation of numerous religious congregations; they are not studied, they are not explored in depth, much less assimilated.

Seeing today so many religious (and religious communities) wandering disoriented after various techniques of prayer, of doctrine and of empty spiritualities under a “supposed updating,” there comes to mind the cry of St. Teresa of Jesus when, faced with the situation of some religious of her time, she said: “To turn away from Christ… I cannot bear it”3.

How timely this cry proves to be when we see so many Christians, and even more, so many religious and priests who remain prisoners of an empty, intimist, immanent spiritualism, incapable of a free openness to the transcendent God, and who propose ‘contemplation’ as a vague immersion in the divinity4 or as “thinking of nothing”5 or of trivialities, with the grave danger of folding back on oneself or on the horizontal society in which one works. Thus, forgetting what is essential, they turn away from the Incarnate Word, running the risk of conceiving those practices as autonomous methods of ‘redemption’6; to such a point that we could say that this is equivalent to not praying. The absurdities observed in many of the practices embraced by some churchmen of our day go so far at times that they seem to border more on paganism than on genuine prayer. It is not surprising, therefore, that these practices have led us to see that a good number of these religious communities are in “free fall,” and some even see their disappearance close at hand. It is a sort of “process of self-destruction”: what is essential has been forgotten, the counsels of the founders have gone unheeded, the saints and mystics who forged the sublime history of Catholic spirituality have been neglected and have been replaced by foolishness. When not that, the Cross of Christ has been cut down, thus choosing the opposite paths, or those that lead away from God.

Therefore, it stands out clearly that if there is anything the Church needs in our days (days marked by confusion), it is an authentic life of contemplative prayer—above all in her consecrated souls—which, precisely because it is such, is transforming. That is, a prayer that consists in a personal encounter with Him who is the only way to lead us to the Father. A prayer that is born of faith and that increases faith. This is the principal problem, perhaps the most fundamental one and the root of the immense majority of the grave problems we face today. Only a serious, responsible and authentic life of prayer can form the leaders and pastors the Church needs, since true prayer gives the discernment, the light and the strength to put the will of God into practice.

Already in 1982 St. John Paul II warned and spoke out “against certain techniques of prayer that are not inspired by the Gospel and that practically tend to do without Christ, in favor of a mental emptiness which has no meaning within Christianity. Every technique of prayer is valid insofar as it is inspired by Christ and leads to Christ, the way, the truth and the life”7.

For this reason, since “the true greatness of the Christian and religious vocation [is] union with God”8, we members of the Institute are not only called but also impelled “to seek the city to come… perfect union with Christ, that is, holiness”9. Hence we are very rightly told that “the contemplation of divine things and assiduous union with God in prayer must be the first and principal duty of all religious”10. This search for Christ in the contemplation of His mysteries contains “an ardent desire to know Him and love Him in prayer, to practice heroic virtues so as to become more like Him, who has done all things well11; and a heartfelt love for the souls for whom Christ shed His blood”12, from which it follows that “Christian contemplative prayer constantly refers back to love of neighbor, to action and to suffering, and, precisely in this way, brings one closer to God”13.

Thanks to a serious and radically Christocentric spirituality, all of us members of the Institute have been instructed to live the spiritual life “as relationship and communion with God”14, and we have been formed in the school of the great masters of the spiritual life of all times, something we should never cease to keep in mind (everyone will remember how, from the first steps in the novitiate and in the seminary, the names of St. John of the Cross, St. Thomas Aquinas, St. Teresa of Jesus, St. Ignatius of Loyola and St. Louis Marie—to mention a few—were already part of our daily life). Moreover, if we look closely, everything in our Proper Law is nothing but provisions and directives for putting into practice what these great mystical saints teach.

Hence the danger of going astray by following those impersonal or self-centered techniques of prayer is perhaps less threatening for us (which does not mean, obviously, that we should neglect vigilance). But abandoning prayer, or the lack of recollection—which leaves our consecrated life, as it were, unprotected—does not cease to be a temptation and a real trial for the soul, thus producing a weakening of the original fervor, leading us to superficiality and mediocrity in life, which in turn brings with it a lack of discernment, with the disastrous effects that all this produces, in ourselves and in others.

For all these reasons, we must be very aware that the following of Christ in our beloved Institute entails the demand for conversion and holiness of a transfigured existence, the demand to contemplate and to bear witness to the transfigured face of Christ15. Therefore, we must emphasize the irreplaceable importance16 of an authentic life of prayer for our consecrated life, as well as the preponderance it has when it comes to discerning the signs of the times and effectively carrying out the noble task of the evangelization of culture to which we have been called.

The crisis of the Church in which we are living is, at its depths, a crisis of authentic prayer.

1. To contemplate

May these lines and these considerations, then, be an incentive that leads all of us to be men of prayer—even more, masters of prayer, as our priestly mission asks of us17; always in line with the great mystics of the Catholic tradition; may they always be the ones who set our “north.”

Several times throughout the length and breadth of our Proper Law we are exhorted to contemplate the mysteries of the life of our Lord, among which the mystery of the Incarnation18—for everyone, but particularly for us—is of capital importance. This contemplation must be carried out—as our Proper Law points out—“with patient and fervent perseverance so that… the very face of Christ may be imprinted”19 on our souls. “This was the exhortation of Jesus Christ Himself to His disciples: Pray always without losing heart20, and that of St. Paul: pray constantly21”22. To which another Directory adds, in a fatherly way: “let him live in the presence of God and let his whole existence be a living in a state of petition and of immersion in God”23.

This contemplation, obviously, must not be understood as reserved to the contemplative members or to the mystics, since all of us members of the Institute have been called to a “holy familiarity with the Word made flesh”24, so that that “equality of friendship”25 may be brought about in our souls. That is why St. John of the Cross, a great knower of souls, wisely warns us: “And it must not be held impossible that the soul should be capable of something so lofty as to breathe in God as God breathes in her, in a participated manner […]. For… so that she might attain to this, He created her in His image and likeness26”27.

Consequently, the state of perfect contemplation and union that we say we want to attain is not merely a theory. It is life!28. And like all life, it is nourished, grows and expands through divine grace in prayer. That is why one of our documents says, very realistically and rightly, that “holiness is attainable because it is, above all, the work of God”29. In this sense it seems important to us to point out that it is God who grants the favor of placing [the soul] in the state of contemplation30, and that is why the Mystic of Fontiveros says that “pure contemplation consists in receiving”31, for it is there that God’s action in the soul becomes perceptible even without the mediation of discursive reasoning or of the natural activity of the powers of the soul. Hence the Mystical Doctor says that “the powers rest and do not work actively, but passively, receiving what God works in them”32, that the powers “are acted upon”33, that “the soul (is) occupied”34. That is why the experience of the one praying “that he is doing nothing”35 and “that he is wasting time”36 is not uncommon. We must be aware that these trials are spared no one who takes his spiritual life seriously, and to accept them with patience is to let God decide the manner in which He wants to make us sharers in His love37. Let us realize that it is precisely in those periods marked by aridity that one must strive more firmly to persevere in prayer, because it is precisely then that prayer constitutes an expression of our fidelity to God, in whose presence we prefer to remain even though we are not ‘rewarded’ by any subjective consolation38. To walk along these dark yet sure paths is, then, to walk along the way of the one authentic contemplation.

It is then that “God begins… as they say, to wean the soul and to place it in the state of contemplation… and [the powers] pass their exercise over to the spirit, God working it in them thus”39. St. Teresa of Jesus, for her part, says that God plays the first role as a friend, who speaks “without the noise of words”40. And along the same lines St. John of the Cross affirms that “God is the principal agent”41 or, simply, “God alone is the agent”42, or “the supernatural craftsman”43, and then distinguishes, as it were, two moments or stages: first by suffering, afterwards in sweetness of love44.

This is how St. John Paul II, the great follower of St. John of the Cross in our times, understood contemplation; he stressed that “Christ crosses the path of prayer from one end to the other, from the first steps to the summit of perfect communion with God. Christ is the door through which the soul accedes to the true mystical state45. Christ introduces it into the Trinitarian mystery46. His presence in the unfolding of this ‘friendly intercourse’ which is prayer is obligatory and necessary: He brings it about and generates it. And He is also its object. He is the ‘living book’47, the Word of the Father. Man learns to remain in deep silence when Christ teaches him interiorly ‘without the noise of words’48; he empties himself within ‘looking upon the Crucified’49. Contemplation is not a search for hidden subjective potentialities by means of refined techniques of interior purification, but rather an opening of oneself in humility to Christ and to His Mystical Body, which is the Church”50. So that our prayer may be a generous walking in the footsteps of Him who loved us so much.

Likewise, let it be well understood that “divine contemplation is not simply philosophical speculation; rather, in accordance with the Gospel message, it corresponds to an intimate union with Christ, considered as the ‘Bridegroom’ of the soul and as the ‘Bridegroom’ of the Church”51. Hence when one prays well, when one “lives and acts in the presence of God and with confidence in Him”52. When in prayer one experiences the living God and gives oneself to Him, then one can understand more deeply, with the help of grace, the Church in which Christ continues His presence. We must be convinced that in our prayers, with the help of the Holy Spirit, the whole Church prays, from which it follows that any supernatural contemplation, impelled by faith and charity, whether in the celebration of the liturgy, or in that interior savoring of the word of God, or in silent adoration, is communion with Jesus Christ and is at the same time “help given to my sweet Jesus”53 in the Church, as the holy virgin and teacher, Teresa of Jesus, affirmed.

To underline the necessity and importance of the contemplation to which a serious spirituality leads “means, therefore, to subordinate every interest, every activity to the conquest of friendship with the Lord, from which arises the commitment to fraternal charity, which tends to lead men to the same spiritual experience. Therefore, the end of love of neighbor is to make known to our brothers, by word and by action, the goodness of the Lord—His wonders, His grace, His truth, His gifts—which has been experienced beforehand and at the same time in contemplation”54. Note that we emphasize at the same time in order to corroborate what our Proper Law points out when it says that “the missionary must be a ‘contemplative in action’”55. That is why the great Spanish Doctor of the Church considered the life of prayer as the supreme manifestation of the theological life of the faithful who, believing in divine love, strip themselves of all things in order to obtain completely His presence full of love.

I would like to insist: the experience of God—which is like a new name for contemplation56—is communion with God, in which the soul opens itself entirely to His action and tastes wisdom, infused by the Holy Spirit, while the mind and heart adhere to the Incarnate Word as the “door” through which one goes to the Father and through which the Father grants familiar intercourse. It is faith, united to charity and hope, that produces that intimate and savory knowledge which we call the experience of God, Christian contemplation. That is why it is something that goes beyond theological or philosophical reflection.

For this reason, St. John Paul II once affirmed that “God denies no one the water of contemplation,” and, quoting St. Teresa, he added: “‘rather He calls us publicly, aloud. But since He is so good, He does not force us; rather He gives drink in many ways to those who wish to follow Him, so that no one may go away disconsolate or die of thirst’57”58. In fact, the great Pope wrote later, “many simple and devoted souls receive it from God, through the Spirit”59. And in this sense we can quote St. John of the Cross, who, in dedicating the Spiritual Canticle to Ana de Jesús, tells her: “Although Your Reverence lacks the exercise of scholastic theology by which divine truths are understood, you do not lack that of mystical theology, which is known through love, in which they are not only known but at the same time savored”60. We can therefore argue that the experience of God is a gift united to fidelity to authentic prayer. For God, who is always faithful, seeing souls prepared, desires nothing else than to fill them with gifts61.

Now, as we said earlier, among all the mysteries of the life of Christ, that of the Incarnation is as it were the center of the mysteries of the Christian life and is “central in our spirituality”62. “He is the ‘Way’ to go to the Father, and no one goes to the Father except through Him”63, affirms our Proper Law. To which the Spanish Mystical Doctor adds with her usual solidity: “Through this door we must enter if we want the sovereign Majesty to show us great secrets. So let one desire no other way, even if one is at the summit of contemplation. By this way one goes securely. This Lord of ours is the one through whom all good things come to us”64.

For this reason, in a very fatherly but firm way, our Proper Law encourages us to “the contemplation of the mystery of the Incarnation [which] nourishes love for the Most Holy Trinity: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who brought about the Incarnation; and enkindles in us an ardent love for the Word who ‘for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven’”65, because, as St. John of the Cross says, “the works of the Incarnation of the Word and the mysteries of the faith… being the greater works of God and those that enclose a greater love in themselves, produce in the soul a greater effect of love”66.

Although no good spiritual person denies that the whole life and work of the “Son of God made man”67 have been ordered to our salvation, nevertheless, His Passion, His voluntary death and the cross represent the culmination of Christ’s saving work. That is why “only Jesus Christ, the definitive Word of the Father, can reveal to men the mystery of suffering and illuminate with the radiance of His glorious cross the darkest nights of the Christian”68. Consequently, the great mystics of the Church, and especially St. John of the Cross, approach the contemplation of the cross as the key to the path toward union with God in and through Christ. He always insists on this point. There is no other way of living union and love with Him. “The Incarnate Word Himself teaches us to love the cross: deny yourself, take up your cross each day and follow me69”70. We must be very clear about this. That is why in the following paragraph our Proper Law encourages us to the contemplation of Christ Crucified, saying: “We must not want to know anything except Jesus Christ and Jesus Christ crucified71”72. So that when crosses come upon us we may know how to unite ourselves supernaturally to our Redeemer and, as St. Paul said, glory in our cross.

One author states that “the death of Christ was efficacious in itself, not because of the sufferings in themselves, but insofar as it was the expression of His obedient and loving surrender to the will of the Father, and that is what makes our own crosses valuable as we follow Him. […] For the Saint of Fontiveros the cross is valuable insofar as it is a surrender of the person and of one’s being, of the will, of love and of life. He does not put pain and blood in the foreground”73. In this sense the Directory of Spirituality teaches us that “all the co-redemptive efficacy of our sufferings depends on their union with the Cross, and in the measure and degree of that union. […] If we do not learn to be victims with the Victim, all our sufferings are useless”74. To move away from the spirituality of the cross is to choose the path opposite to the one that leads to union. Whoever accepts Jesus Christ accepts that the way is the way of the cross, and there is no other.

For all these reasons, let us be convinced of this truth: the cross belongs “in a particular way to the essence of the religious vocation”75. It is as if on the day of our profession Jesus Christ had said to us: “Arise! I await you on Calvary”76. It is the cross, which comes upon us day by day in different forms, that “enkindles in hearts the fire of divine love; what is more, it maintains and increases it, just as wood does a fire. And thus it is proven in the clearest way that one loves God. Because it is the very proof that God made use of to manifest His love to us. And the one God asks of us to manifest ours to Him”77. Therefore, “the Cross is God’s most beautiful gift”78. Thus did Jesus make it known to St. Gemma, the virgin of Lucca: “See, my daughter, the greatest gift I can grant to a soul loved by me is the gift of suffering”79. Embracing the cross with patience and humility helps us to strip ourselves of the old man and to clothe ourselves with the purity, the pleasure and the will of our Divine Savior, and to bear limpid witness to the transfigured face of Christ.

We must always remember that “the true apostolate consists in participating in the work of Christ’s salvation, something that cannot be accomplished without an intense spirit of prayer and sacrifice […]. This spirit of prayer, and particularly the sacrifice of oneself after the example of Christ and united to Him, is most efficacious for cooperation in the redemption of the human race”80. In reality, we believe we are not mistaken in saying that, although the Church needs missionary religious to evangelize, she nevertheless has as much need, and more, of religious who seek, cultivate and bear witness to the presence and intimacy of God, with the intention of obtaining the sanctification of all men.

When the missionary, moved by the impulse of divine love, bears witness to a close friendship with God—cultivated in prayer and sustained in the trials of daily life—to the point of being fused into one single love, that is, when he submits himself totally, without reservations, to the Will of God, then that friendship becomes an apostolic leaven, it is a cause of joy for the Church and for others; it is like a most powerful voice that penetrates the divine heart and redounds to the good of all81.

For this reason, the contemplation of which we have been speaking and union with our Lord, though always necessary, become an irrepressible longing in the soul above all in moments of trial. When tribulations become more grievous, in those dark moments of existence, when the night of disorientation and suffering seems most closed in, there arises in the soul that need for a prolonged colloquy of hope, that the most sweet presence of the Lord may not be interrupted. How many times in our missionary life do we experience failure, lack of support, aridity, indifference, bewilderment, misunderstanding, ingratitude even on the part of those who have been helped! Aware that our help comes from the Lord82, that He alone is our support and help, it is important not only to have an authentic spirit of prayer but to be persevering in it, because prayer serves to restore us when weariness comes upon us and to remind us of the beauty and loftiness of the goal we are called to reach. Only in Christ, the Good Shepherd, are the principal strength and true rest to be found83, and only with His help can one joyfully face the burden of the day and the heat84. Likewise, when darkness and difficulties grow harsher for our Institute, it is important to pray—as a community and individually—in order to join forces for a common defense and a mutual strengthening.

It happens not infrequently in our life as missionaries that we fall “into the trap of measuring the value of our apostolic efforts by the criteria of efficiency, functionality and external success that govern the world of business. Certainly, these things are important. A great responsibility has been entrusted to us, and precisely for that reason the People of God expect a response from us. But the true value of our apostolate is measured by the value it has in the eyes of God […]. The cross shows us a different way of measuring success: it falls to us to sow, and God sees the fruits of our labors. If at times it should seem to us that our efforts and works are crumbling and bear no fruit, we must remember that we are followers of Jesus Christ, whose life, humanly speaking, ended in failure: in the failure of the cross”85, and we must know how to unite ‘our failures and frustrations’ to that of Christ. What we are saying about apostolic efforts also applies to those in our Institute who exercise the sacred office of governing and who sacrifice themselves tirelessly and silently, “bearing the weaknesses of many”86, whom our Proper Law especially reminds that “they must not dedicate themselves exclusively to the active life, since they must excel in the contemplative life. That is why St. Gregory says: let the superior be the first in action and let him give himself more than anyone to contemplation”87, so as to remain standing “above the ups and downs of fortune or failure”88.

As a corollary of what we have been saying, the fact stands out that, our Institute being fully committed to missionary activity, silence and interior recollection are two bulwarks for authentic prayer. That is why our Proper Law stresses that “silence is a need of the soul and a means for achieving union with God and, therefore, it must lead to the summit of prayer”89. In order to remain zealous in our ministry, in order to keep ourselves from falling into the aberrations of empty spiritualities, we need to welcome divine inspirations within ourselves, and this is only possible if we are capable of spending some time beside the Divine Master. Being with Jesus is an indispensable condition for all authentic and credible evangelization. Moreover, we could say that missionary endeavor is preceded and prepared by moments of contemplation; otherwise, everything becomes a beating of the air.

“Contemplation in this world,” said St. John Paul II, “allows us to have a foretaste of paradise; but no one can be allowed in this life, not even contemplatives, to rest content with contemplation alone. It is necessary, without doubt, to keep our eyes fixed on the transcendent goal, in order to know how to give the exact orientation to our work, so that we may not labor in vain nor lose the right path—in short, for spiritual discernment. Contemplative experience, when it is authentic, that is, when it is based on the sacramental life, on the word of God and on a rigorous moral commitment, allows us to know truly what the will of God is and what we must do to attain eternal life, for eternal life consists precisely, as the apostle St. John teaches us, in the contemplation of the only true God, and of him whom He sent, Jesus Christ”90.

And this brings us to the second point we wish to address.

2. Discernment of the signs of the times

Our Constitutions point out that “the priest must make his ministry humanly as credible and acceptable as possible”91. “That is why the Second Vatican Council teaches that all clerics, especially priests, deacons and catechists who by office are dedicated to the ministry of the word, must read and study Scripture assiduously so as not to become ‘empty preachers of the word, who do not hear it inwardly’92”93. Then it says immediately afterwards: “because ‘… the priest is the man of God, the one who belongs to God and makes people think of God…’ […] therefore, ‘… it is necessary… that the priest be formed in a deep intimacy with God’”94.

This demands of all of us—as essentially missionary religious95—the acceptance of living in a state of permanent conversion96. The true missionary is the one who accepts to commit himself resolutely to the paths of holiness97. Therefore, if the missionary is not a contemplative, he cannot proclaim Christ credibly. For “the missionary is a witness of the experience of God and must be able to say with the Apostles: What we have contemplated… concerning the Word of life…, we proclaim to you”98.

And here is the core of the message we wish to address to all the members of the Institute and to the generations that are to come after: we have been called to “seek God first of all and God alone”99, for which it is necessary to join contemplation (through which we are united to God) to apostolic love100. If there were not a perfect balance between our life with God and the activities carried out in the service of our neighbor, not only would the work of evangelization in which we are engaged be compromised, but also our own personal condition as evangelized. The life of prayer is the soul of our work for the Kingdom: liturgical prayer, centered on the Eucharist, received and lived with that purity of conscience which recourse to the sacrament of Confession demands; the Liturgy of the Hours, which sets the rhythm of continual adoration in spirit and in truth; with the ‘beloved’ presence of the Blessed Virgin Mary, model of the Missionary and the Contemplative. There will always be opportunity to sow the good seed; but it can only be fruitful if it is wrapped in habits of prayer and contemplation, of study of Sacred Scripture according to the authentic Magisterium of the Church, and confirmed by a life of sacrifice.

At this point, it seems important to us to stress that countless times our Proper Law mentions—explicitly and implicitly—that the apostolate is a supernatural reality whose fruitfulness depends “on union with God and with the Church”101. Therefore, “it is not, then, simply a matter of a science or a technique, but of having the charity and sentiments of Christ, who went about doing good102 even to giving his life for the sheep103”104. And this seems to us no minor reality when it comes to evangelization. What is proper to us is not to offer technical solutions to the various problems of the realities we face, but the truth about Christ, about His Church and about man himself105, to be applied to those concrete situations.

For this reason, since the proclamation of the Gospel is an essential part of our mission, we must bear in mind that it takes place in very concrete circumstances which include, and must take into account, all the events of our age: the difficult social, moral, economic and political problems, the problems of modern culture in the various countries, all the sufferings of men, their doubts, their anxieties, the rapidity of changes and the acceleration of history to the point that it is scarcely possible to keep up with it106, the new ways of perceiving values, Christophobia and persecution, etc. All this demands a spirit that is at the same time close to God and closer to men, a spirit attentive to the voice of the Spirit who speaks in the depths of consciences as well as through the signs of the times, in order to resolve to overcome difficulties in a Gospel key. “The gaze we direct at the realities of the contemporary world, which we hope will always be full of the compassion and mercy that our Lord Jesus Christ has taught us, is not limited to perceiving errors and dangers. Certainly, the negative and problematic aspects cannot be ignored either, but we must immediately try to seek paths of hope and point out perspectives of intense commitment with a view to the integral promotion of the person, to his liberation and to the fullness of his happiness”107, said St. John Paul II. But for this “evangelical discernment, supernatural prudence”108 are necessary. And it is filial and constant prayer, prompt and docile listening to the Word of God and of the Church, and recourse to wise and charitable spiritual direction that will allow us a Christian evaluation of those historical phenomena that it falls to us to live through, for only in Christ, savored interiorly, are found the ultimate and decisive keys for reading the present human condition and for choosing priorities109.

This discernment, sought in prayer, will help us not to confuse the Regnum Dei with the Regnum hominis, which has caused and is causing so much havoc among many religious and even in entire congregations in our days. The Gospel is definitive and does not pass away. Its criteria are forever. One cannot make rereadings of the Gospel according to the times, as some do, conforming oneself to everything the world asks. On the contrary, it is necessary to read the signs of the times and the problems of today’s world in the unfailing light of the Gospel110.

In this sense we must always keep very much in mind that “to love and serve the men of today certainly means working for the development and progress of society and for the attainment of more just and dignified human conditions; but it means not deceiving anyone about the authentic meaning of the earthly pilgrimage, whose ultimate goal transcends time and cannot be attained without the exercise of an enlightened detachment from material goods and without the practice of charity”111.

Not in vain does our Proper Law explain that when St. Paul writes to Timothy: I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus… proclaim the word, insist in season and out of season, reprove, exhort… be watchful… fulfill your ministry to perfection112, that importuning “does not refer to the absence of ‘discreet charity,’ which discerns, but to not heeding natural prudence, to not conforming oneself to the world and to what for the world is ‘opportune’ or convenient. For the world’s ‘fashion,’ for the ideologies or currents of the moment, the Word of God must be ‘inopportune’; it must importune and contradict the world”113.

“The missionary must be very attentive to the ‘signs of the times’ in order to enlighten souls at the time and moment they need it, attentive to the moments of grace, to the dispositions of men, nations and peoples, to the human vicissitudes that Providence disposes or permits, always ordered to the preaching of the Gospel and the salvation of the elect, to the need to enlighten with those points of Sacred Doctrine that are most urgent, fitting and necessary, those most contradicted by the fashion of the world. The missionary must therefore be up to date with regard to information about temporal reality and about the contemporary Magisterium, especially that of the Pope, and his pronouncements on current problems”114. That is why we would fall short, or our action would be unjust, if we did not also mention, together with the importance of the life of prayer, that of ongoing formation, in its twofold dimension of conversion and continual cultural updating, in order to achieve a fuller and more coherent fidelity to one’s own vocation115.

Today as much as yesterday we must bear in mind that what is proper to us is “to begin great works in the service of God and to persevere to the end in carrying them out”116, just as so many men and women of prayer did who accomplished great works in the Church. Let us think, for example, of St. Padre Pio, who spent so many years without ever leaving Pietrelcina and founded the beautiful work of the Home for the Relief of Suffering; let us think of St. Teresa of Calcutta, who in the most arduous spiritual dryness went on smiling and founding houses to care for the poorest of the poor; let us think of St. John of the Cross himself, who with only 28 years of religious life, and while being a contemplative, was prodigal in works for his Order, formed and continues to form legions of Christians, and today is venerated as a Doctor of the Church; let us think of St. John Paul II, a mystic who spent long hours in prayer, to which he knew how to unite a tireless pastoral ministry… the list is endless. All of them, through their “assiduous contact with the Word of God, obtained the light needed for the personal and communal discernment that served them to seek the ways of the Lord and to discover the signs of the times. They thus acquired a kind of supernatural instinct which made it possible for them, instead of bending to the mentality of the world, to renew their own minds, so as to be able to discern the will of God, what is good, what is pleasing to Him, what is perfect117”118. They are our guides, not yoga, the new-age, or any other deformation of the absolute that has been presented, or that people wish to present or impose in our days, or that will be presented to us in the future.

Similarly we, sent into a changing world, in which injustices and unheard-of sufferings persist and grow worse, where disorientation and perplexity become gigantic, and scandals are the order of the day, must be bearers of light and of Christian hope. It is true that “the challenge that contemporary culture poses to faith is precisely this: to abandon the easy inclination to paint dark and negative scenarios, in order to trace possible paths, not illusory ones, of redemption, liberation and hope”119. But we must neither lose heart nor be mistaken in what we propose. Moreover, we count on the maternal and most wise help of our Mother in Heaven and Spouse of the Holy Spirit, from whom we can obtain that ‘higher’ counsel which is discernment and wisdom in decisions, and which above all helps to identify the growing spiritual needs of our age, with a vision of social and human reality in the light of the Gospel, and consequently gives the courage to give those needs and those visions the appropriate responses, which is nothing other than ‘to bite into reality.’

All the present problems must not make us lose sight of the fact that it is the Holy Spirit who “with admirable providence guides the course of the ages and renews the face of the earth”120. And it is the same Holy Spirit who must guide our discernment, precisely because the Spirit must be the hidden strength of all our missionary labor, leading us to the depths of contemplation, from which must spring our “witness to Him who came not to be served but to serve and to give His life for many121, and who gives His life freely122”123.

*****

“Ours is a world of speed, in which the intensity of motion takes the place of the lack of purpose; where noise is summoned to drown out the whispers of conscience; where talking, talking, talking gives the impression that we are doing something when in reality we are not; where activity kills the self-knowledge that is gained through contemplation…”124, said Venerable Fulton Sheen.

That is why we must understand that “without contact with the Lord, there is no convincing and persevering evangelization”125 and that, while apostolic works are very necessary, prayers are more so.

We have the challenge and the immense grace of taking part in the glorious adventure of evangelizing the culture and, therefore, of sharing in the sufferings of Christ in the midst of a world without God. We must, nevertheless, immerse ourselves in the life of this world without God, without trying to disguise impiety with a varnish of religion or with a nominalist label of ‘evangelization,’ but in order to make the cultures subject to Christ, by Him and for Him. This is achieved, counting on the grace of God, by sacrificing oneself silently and hiddenly, in abandonment to the holy will of God, in serene fidelity even in the face of the decline of one’s strength and of one’s own influence, that is, by uniting oneself to the Cross of our Lord, which presupposes a great spirit of prayer.

“There is no love for Christ if this love does not impel one to give oneself to the Church, and there is no filial obedience toward the Church if this obedience is not translated into works carried out with fervor, to which prayer lends its strength and solidity”126. Let us always consider ourselves beginners and never cease to aspire to a holier and more perfect life127.

May the sublime example of the Mother of God spur us on to be men of prayer, capable of transforming every encounter with other men into an invitation to communion with God. Let us go with resolute spirit to the mission, preserving that unity of life which keeps us faithful to contemplation, in such a way that it may be said of us what St. Teresa said of those “who, the further along they are in this prayer… the more they attend to the needs of their neighbors, especially of souls, for it seems they would give many lives to rescue one from mortal sin”128.

In times of tension and trial such as those through which our Institute is walking, let us decisively choose the radical path of the following of Christ, the building up of the Church with living stones of holiness, and let us raise ever higher the standard of the Incarnation of the Word.

Conference at the Monastery “Blessed Maria Gabriella of Unity”

The Lord reigns forever! (Ex 15:18)

Unity, a resplendent mark of the true Church, is the summit of Christ’s priestly prayer at the Last Supper; it is his last testament of love, the watchword he left us before his passion: Ut omnes unum sint. That was the supreme aspiration of the Most Sacred Heart of Jesus, and hence the cardinal importance of the cause of Christian unity.

We, members of the Religious Family of the Incarnate Word, who “want to love and serve, and to make Jesus Christ loved and served […] both in his physical Body, which is the Eucharist, and in his Mystical Body, which is the Church”1, cannot excuse ourselves from “embracing the holy cause of ecumenism”2. The cause of the unity of the Church is a cause that is our own, which begins with the solid union of each one of us with the Incarnate Word and among ourselves through mutual love3, as our Proper Law commands us. That is why we are exhorted to “strive to attain, through prayer, penance, study, dialogue and collaboration, the full unity of the Church, ‘according to the mind and heart of our Savior Jesus Christ’4”5.

And if this concerns all of us as Christians and members of the Institute, all the more does it concern you, since you have as your specific mission and special intention to pray for Christian unity. The cause of the unity of the Church ought to give –or, in fact, already gives– a special hue to everything that is done in this monastery. In the words of Fr. Buela, this particular intention must “also color the intellectual and apostolic work” carried out here in this community. This gives your life an ecclesial dimension that makes it transcend these walls, as St. John Paul II rightly said: “Enclosure does not isolate… from the communion of the Mystical Body. Rather, it places cloistered nuns in the very heart of the Church”6.

Note that to consecrate oneself to God, especially in the contemplative vocation, is also a desire to consecrate oneself to the Church; therefore, the immolation of each one of you for Jesus also implies completing what is lacking in the passion of Christ for his body, which is the Church; the contemplation of God in Christ during prayer becomes a loving contemplation of his Church. So all of this, taken together, gives the contemplative life an apostolic ardor, which affords it the possibility of making the seed of the good news fruitful wherever God pleases. That is to say, by your works –even if these are hidden sacrifices– and your prayers you offer an irreplaceable and most valuable contribution to all the evangelizing activity of our Religious Family on behalf of the Church, and indeed to the missionary work of the whole Church. You are, as St. Clare of Assisi said, “co-workers of God himself and a support of the weak and wavering members of his ineffable Body”7.

So in this talk I would like, even if briefly, to develop the theme of the cause of the unity of the Church in three points, as if they were three steps that consequently serve or lead –through the merits and grace of God– to the unity of the Church:

  1. Our union with God.
  2. Our union with our brothers and sisters, particularly with those of our community and of our Religious Family.
  3. Our service to the unity of the Church.

1. Union with God

To treat the first point, that of our union with God as the first and most important step in the cause of unity, I would like to cite a marvelous example of zeal for the Church and for her unity, given us precisely by a contemplative soul: St. Teresa of Jesus.

She knew perfectly the martyrdom of the Body of Christ, divided and profaned, which she herself describes succinctly in her book “The Way of Perfection”: “At this time,” writes the saint, “news reached me of the harm done in France and the havoc these Lutherans had wrought, and how much this wretched sect was growing. It grieved me greatly, and as if I could do something or were something, I wept before the Lord and begged him to remedy so great an evil. It seemed to me that I would have given a thousand lives to remedy a single soul of the many that were being lost there. And as I saw that I was a woman and wretched and unable to be of any use in what I would have wished in the service of the Lord, and all my longing was, and still is, that since he has so many enemies and so few friends, these few should be good ones, I resolved to do the very little that was in me, which is to follow the evangelical counsels with all the perfection I could”8. And with this eagerness she did everything, as she herself says, to “please the Lord in some small way,” and she spent her days praying –she herself and all her nuns– for those who are “defenders of the Church, and preachers and learned men who defend her.” For she understood that the love of God must impel us to work generously for the Church.

These are her words: “Love does not consist in the greatest delight, but in the greatest determination to desire to please God in everything, and to strive, as far as we can, not to offend him, and to beg him that the honor and glory of his Son and the increase of the Catholic Church may always go forward”9. That is why in the “Book of Her Life,” after speaking of those who truly serve the Church, she exclaims: “Happy the lives that end in this!”10. And although she grieved and her heart broke at seeing the division of the one Body of Christ, her spirit expanded before the new missionary horizons she saw opening up in America11. The divisions, the difficulties, the scarcity she noticed did not daunt her in her desire to go through the world serving the Church; on the contrary! her longing to found more monasteries grew ever greater.

For St. Teresa did not remain only in abstract contemplation. For her, to contemplate Christ was to contemplate with the same gaze the Church, which, existing in time, expresses by her life the actions and the mystery of Christ. And if the Church was suffering divisions, schisms and outrages, she had to do “the very little that was in me,” as the Saint said, so as to be able to “please the Lord in some small way.” And she also wished her daughters to do the same, that is, to sacrifice themselves generously so that the Lord might “protect his Church,” putting all their concerns into this. Listen to what she told her nuns: “When your prayers and desires and disciplines and fasts are not employed in what I have said (on behalf of the Church and the sacred hierarchy), consider that you are not doing or fulfilling the end for which the Lord brought you together here”12.

Our Proper Law too –with fatherly wisdom– encourages us to do the same: “In the apostolate carried out by consecrated persons –which in your specific case is that of prayer for Christian unity– their spousal love for Christ becomes almost organically a love for the Church as the Body of Christ. Through this apostolate you put your love for the Church into practice”13. This is so because love for Christ cannot fail to be love for the Church; or, put another way: love for Christ the Head includes love for his body, the Church, with which he identifies himself mystically14.

Now, this apostolate, so worthy and so special, requires –like all the works of God– a total, constant self-giving, without reservations. That is why the Directory of Consecrated Life goes on to say, “the religious who consecrates himself to God in a particular way, seeking union with God through the practice of perfect charity, must do so through Christ, with Christ and in Christ. He is called to unite himself to God in Christ and to imitate more closely and represent perpetually in the Church ‘that form of life which the Son of God embraced when he came into this world to do the will of the Father, and which he proposed to the disciples who followed him’15”16. This is the very little that is asked of us, the end for which the Lord brought us together. And that is why we are exhorted to offer “not only our prayers and supplications, but our very immolation, so as to contribute ‘powerfully to the good of the Church’17”18. This Christocentric stamp, so marked in our spirituality and in our apostolate of evangelizing the culture19, must stand out in a particular and principal way in your prayer for Christian unity. Without this union with God, one is cut off from one’s source, loses one’s substance and cannot attain one’s end20.

Contemplating the mystery of the Church, which in those times was ‘suffering,’ St. Teresa felt the tearing apart of her unity and the betrayal of many Christians; she regarded the relaxation of morals as a rejection, contempt and profanation of love. For divine friendship was being betrayed. Those who did not accept the Church nor live with her, those who did not follow her Magisterium, were rejecting Christ, scorning his love.

That is why she constantly exhorted her nuns to pray for the Church, “trusting that the Lord commands us to ask,”21 she told them, so “that so very great an evil may not go forward, nor such outrages as are committed in the places where this Most Holy Sacrament was, among these Lutherans, the churches destroyed, so many priests lost, the sacraments taken away. […] Let not this ship of the Church always be in such a tempest; and save us, my Lord, for we are perishing”22. For she was fully convinced –and it is important that we be so too– that in one who prays in the Holy Spirit, that is, in accordance with the Will of God, the whole Church prays. Mind you, our Constitutions express the same when they say that whoever prays must do so “with the conviction that ‘all who perform this function… while they praise God, stand before his throne in the name of Mother Church’”23.

Therefore, all authentic supernatural contemplation, which springs from faith and love, whether in the liturgy or in listening to the Word of God, whether in praise of the Lord or in silent adoration, is a glorification of God the Father and a communion with Christ. In other words, prayer is that ‘help to the sweet Jesus of my soul,’ as St. Teresa called it, made a reality in the Church”24.

That is why the interior life of contemplative souls, by virtue of monastic profession, is not only a private exercise of spirituality, praiseworthy as that certainly is, but is an ecclesial mission and service for the benefit of all the members of the Church and, truth be told, of all humanity25.

Prayer is what keeps us in contact with Christ, and if we are in contact with Christ, we cannot fail to be concerned for the Church. Rather, thanks to this particular union with God through prayer, we have an insight, so to speak, a keener perception –if you will– of the needs of the Church, of her urgencies, of her sorrows… the mystery of the Church as “sacrament of salvation”26 deepens within us, as it were, and the longing of the Incarnate Word becomes all the more challenging: Ut omnes unum sint.

Well known to all is the Saint’s phrase when she so forcefully exhorted the sisters to throw themselves into prayer as a most excellent means of working for the unity of the Church: “O my sisters in Christ! help me to beg this of the Lord, for it is for this that he brought you together here; this is your calling, this must be your business, these must be your desires, for this your tears, these your petitions; not, my sisters, for the business of the world. […] The world is on fire; they want to sentence Christ again, so to speak, since they raise a thousand false witnesses against him; they want to raze his Church to the ground; and are we to waste time on things which perhaps, if God granted them (speaking of the intentions for which people asked them to pray), would mean one soul less in heaven? No, my sisters, this is no time to treat with God about matters of little importance”27.

St. John Paul II also highlighted prayer as the means of perceiving –firsthand– the sacramentality of the Church, the torrent of her grace, the outrages she suffers, and the great need to console Jesus. He said that “when someone prays, lives by prayer, and through it has experience of the living God and gives himself to him, he also opens himself to a more intimate experience of the Church, in which Christ is mysteriously present with his grace; he understands the urgency of an unconditional fidelity to the Bride of Christ and feels in his inmost being the desire to work for the Church, even to the point of giving his life for her. When prayer, inflamed by the love of God, shows itself as a close friendship with him, it tends toward the communion or union of love in which the creature gives his will totally to the Creator; then friendship becomes an apostolic leaven, a cause of joy for the good of the Church and of men, a powerful cry that reaches the divine heart and redounds to the benefit of the whole Church28.

From which we see that this cause of the unity of the Church begins with our applying ourselves to tend toward full union with the Incarnate Word, to adhere to him with mind and heart, and it implies the faithful and persevering practice of the evangelical counsels. For, as St. Teresa herself said, “the way of perfection chosen in this life is not only for one’s own benefit, but also for that of many souls”29.

“There can be no unity among brothers if there is not a profound union –of life, of thought, of soul, of purposes, of imitation– with Christ Jesus; even more, if there is no intimate pursuit of interior life in union with the Trinity itself”30.

This is the message of St. Teresa, proclaimed with the authority of one who has experienced it in her life: the conviction that there is no love for Christ that does not become a generous self-giving to the Church, and that there is no true filial affection for the Church unless it translates into apostolic ardor and labor, nourished and strengthened by prayer.

We will do very little for the work of the unity of the Church if we are not united to Christ, if we do not strive to obtain from God the grace of close intimacy with the Incarnate Word, if God’s own love for his Christ is not deeply rooted in us. If genuine union with God is lacking, the struggle for the cause of Christian unity remains mere words and the occasional external act.

Therefore: the more united to God you are through prayer, the more zeal for the establishment of the Kingdom of God and the salvation of souls31, and therefore the greater the contribution to the cause of the unity of the Church. That is to do precisely what our Constitutions beautifully say in describing our vocation: to be chalices that pour out their superabundance upon others32.

And this now leads us to speak of the second point of this talk:

2. Union with our brothers and sisters

“Division ‘openly contradicts the will of Christ, is a scandal to the world, and damages that most holy cause, the preaching of the Gospel to every creature’”33. These words of our beloved John Paul the Great, taken from his most beautiful Encyclical Letter Ut Unum Sint, allow us to glimpse by contrast the imperative need for our unity.

“It is increasingly urgent that those who collaborate in ecumenical activity give united witness”34, our Proper Law says explicitly.

You, within the monastic community, are called to give the world the luminous example of fraternal communion. So that “through fraternal communion rooted and grounded in charity, you may be an example of universal reconciliation in Christ”35.

The privileged experience of contemplation to which you are called as contemplatives presupposes of itself, if it is to be authentic, a high degree of unity of spirit, and also brings as its fruit an elevated form of mutual charity. In fact, the very structure of community life in the monastery, which implies a daily and unceasing relationship among you, necessarily requires –and you will know this better than I– a very demanding exercise of all the social virtues proper to Christian life in common; otherwise community life would be very difficult and perhaps unbearable36.

So important is this that the Directory of Contemplative Life devotes several pages –indeed, an entire section– to speaking of the community virtues in the monastic life of the Institute. And although we will not now go into that in depth, I would like to mention a phrase of St. Benedict, quoted in number 17 of the Directory, which seems to me in some way to sum up all that is said there: “Let them be the first to show honor to one another. Let them bear with the greatest patience one another’s weaknesses, whether of body or of character; let them vie with one another in obedience; let no one seek what he judges useful for himself, but rather what is useful for others; let them practice fraternal charity chastely; let them fear God with love; let them love their Abbot[ess] with sincere and humble affection, and prefer absolutely nothing to Christ, and may he bring us to Eternal Life.” So far St. Benedict.

The reason for this is that “if we are not one, as the Father is one in Christ and Christ is one in the Father, the world will not believe: the concrete proof of the mystery of redemption escapes it, the mystery by which the Lord has made of scattered humanity one single family, one single organism, one single body, one single heart”37. If in the monastery –we might say– you are not as one, the world will not believe, and prayer for Christian unity remains mere words… You as a monastic community have the noble and marvelous responsibility of showing the world –a world in which voices of conflict often resound, between parents and children, between subjects and rulers, between Christians and non-Christians– that it is possible to live in unity.

How will you do it? With the charity of Christ. St. Luigi Orione challenged his own, saying to them: “How shall we show them Christ? With charity. How shall we make Christ loved? With charity. How shall we save our brothers and the nations? With charity; with the charity that becomes a holocaust, but that overcomes everything; with the charity that unites and restores all things in Christ”38.

That is why it is of capital importance that we strive to preserve, to increase, to solidify the unity among ourselves, that is, among the members of the community, with the other monasteries, and among all the members of the Religious Family, through the bonds of charity. Ours is not to function like parts assembled in a machine; ours is to conduct ourselves with an esprit de corps39, like the Church herself, like a family.

So precious is this unity that Blessed Paolo Manna asked his religious: “let us sacrifice everything in order to maintain unity and concord; let us sacrifice especially our self-love, our points of view and our comforts”40, and everything that might hinder, crack or diminish the cohesion that must exist among us.

Much of this theme has already been developed in my last Circular Letter, which you may have already read. Nevertheless, I would like to highlight two elements that make for this unity and that become particularly relevant especially at this time.

a. The first of them is, as the Directory of Fraternal Life points out: “reference to one’s founder and to the charism lived and communicated by him and afterwards guarded, deepened and developed throughout the whole life of the Institute. This is definitely a fundamental element for the unity of the community”41.

And it seems important to me to stress this element to you, because you, as contemplative members of the Institute, are asked not only to be at the forefront of the apostolic endeavor of our Institute but also to be “guardians of its spirit”42.

For that reference to the founder, to his evangelical intentions, to the charism, to the very patrimony of our beloved Congregation, gives us “a clear vision of our own identity, around which it is easier to create unity and communion [and it brings with it a noble sense of belonging that makes us proud]. This in turn allows us to adapt creatively to new situations, which offers us positive prospects for the future of the Institute”43.

Consider this: such must this unity be that the spirit of the Founder, the criteria of judgment, the interests of our Religious Family must show themselves in each one of us as our own, as truly ours, as part of our identity. The spirit of Fr. Buela, the charism he received, must pass whole to each one of us, without dilution, without diminishment, but rather with all its strength, with all its freshness, with all its potential. And although that is a fundamental task of the superiors, each one of you as well –indeed, each one of the members of our Religious Family– must have a great, practical and effective interest in the good of the Institute as a whole; that is, all should feel united by one and the same esprit de corps so as to favor –or rather, to give priority to– in the best possible way and whenever the occasion presents itself, our proper apostolates, the projects of the Institute, vocations, etc. All this, of course, requires no small personal sacrifices… but all that is part of the program!

We cannot skimp on efforts, hold back our goodwill or our people, or time or any other means when it is a matter of the good of the Family, when it is a matter of saving souls and of establishing the Kingdom of Christ. Ours is to work with a spirit of mutual cooperation, which is the opposite of a parochial spirit and has nothing to do with wanting to impose oneself over others, nor with the spirit of opposition that sometimes moves some religious. The spirit of our beloved Congregation is all zeal, all generosity, all magnanimity in self-giving, as the Incarnate Word himself did and commanded us to do. And it falls to all of us –without exception– to live it, to treasure it and to pass it on.

I do not know whether you know the case of Blessed Marie-Anne Blondin44 (1809-1890); she is a Canadian blessed. She founded the religious congregation of the Sisters of St. Anne “for the education of poor country children, in mixed schools.” She was the foundress and the first superior. The thing is that, as the community grew so much, the bishop decided to move the mother house to another place. In the absence of Mother Marie-Anne, the chaplain began to interfere abusively in community life. For example: in the Foundress’s absence, he changed the price of the pupils’ board. And if he went away, the sisters had to wait for him to come back in order to go to confession, and things of that sort. This obviously led to a struggle between Mother Marie-Anne and the chaplain. The solution the bishop gave was to order Mother Marie-Anne to resign her office as superior. Then he himself called elections and demanded of the Mother “that she not accept the mandate of Superior even if the sisters wished to re-elect her.” They sent her to another convent as directress, but shortly afterward the nuns had her called back to the Mother House on the pretext of “bad administration” and with the episcopal order to “take measures so that she does no harm to anyone.” Then they assigned her the work of laundering and ironing in the basement of the convent. From that new removal until her death, she was kept out of all administrative responsibilities. No one knew that she was the foundress. But look at Divine Providence: since all the novices were sent to work in the laundry, she had the opportunity to deal with all the sisters who were entering the congregation, and there she formed them, passed on the charism to them, and gave them an example of heroic charity and humility. She offered herself to God “to expiate the evil committed in her Community; and every day she asked St. Anne, on behalf of her spiritual daughters, for the virtues necessary for Christian educators.” They say that once she was folding clothes in the laundry together with a novice, and the nuns’ clothes at that time bore a number according to the order in which they had entered. So the novice asked her: ‘And what is your number?’ And she answered: ‘Number one.’ Only then did they realize that she was the foundress. Astonished, the novice asked her why she was working in the laundry, to which she replied: “The deeper a tree sinks its roots into the soil, the more chance it has to grow and bear fruit.”

They stole the correspondence she had had with the bishop and afterwards would not let her read the replies to the new letters she sent him; they forbade the sisters to call her Mother, and things of that kind. Just to finish the story, even though we stray a little from the subject, I will tell you that on her deathbed, and “for the edification of the sisters,” she had the chaplain called and, convinced that “there is more happiness in forgiving than in taking revenge,” she forgave him in front of them all. Before dying she said to her sisters: “May the Eucharist and abandonment to the Will of God be your heaven on earth.”

I tell you this example simply to show you that, when it comes to passing on the charism, the spirit of the congregation, and to feeling the interests of the Institute keenly and as one’s own, it does not matter what office one holds in the convent, it does not matter how hostile the environment where one finds oneself, one’s good or bad reputation among the sisters, one’s years of religious life, or whether one is from Argentina or from China. What matters and what counts is to be faithful to the spirit received and embodied in the Constitutions and alive in the sound traditions of our Family; what matters is to live it day by day, with conviction, with authenticity, without duplicity, with courage, without expecting reward in this life, and only for the love of God. Because that is to be faithful to one’s vocation.

Blessed Paolo Manna told his missionaries: “We must greatly prize this spirit of the Institute: […] and not lower and belittle ourselves in cliques or any particularism, which might obscure, even in the slightest, the brilliant, wholly evangelical ideal of the missionary life, as the Institute understands and carries it out, and which, together with our own personal sanctification, constitutes our whole purpose”45. The same holds for us.

Therefore, none of us should behave as a closed and solitary whole, like a nomad who is sufficient unto himself; rather, we must learn to act as the members of a whole46, as a true family, as our Proper Law asks of us. Put another way: we must love our vocation, which is nothing other than loving our Institute, where God has called us to follow him and serve him; to love the community as our own family; to embrace the ideals, the projects, the challenges of our beloved Institute as our own! That too is to love the Church, since we are fulfilling what God has called us to be and to do in his Church.

Notice that when one meets someone who loves his vocation, one is before a strong, autonomous person, secure in his identity, who does not need various supports or compensations; and one notices that it is precisely this that strengthens the bond of union with the other members who share the same call, that makes it so strong and so deeply rooted47. For such a person it does not matter what those outside say about the congregation, nor those inside either; all of that ultimately serves to make him love the Congregation more, defend it, embrace the ideal more closely… I am not going to start telling you about it now, but I have seen this clearly in all my visits to the provinces, truly…; it is very noticeable, and one must acknowledge that it is a grace from God and from Our Lady.

So, steeped in the spirit of our Institute, most faithful to it, regarding it as a title of honor to belong to this beloved Religious Family, let us give it great consolations by loving, practicing and most faithfully passing on the whole most beautiful patrimony that has been bequeathed to us; that is our treasure, so incredibly rich and full of wisdom.

I told you so in the Circular Letter: “let us give our beloved Institute religious solidly founded on Christ, united like granite and as if ‘fixed’ in the charism of the Institute, and we will be like a city on a hill48 that needs no defensive walls, since its own perfectly united members are its fortifications”49.

Look, so united must we be that if, per impossibile, persecution were to break out, “our Congregation should fall as one and die a martyr,”50 as St. Luigi Orione said.

b. The second element –even more outstanding than the previous one– is precisely Christ himself, present in the Most Blessed Sacrament of the Altar. The Directory of Spirituality says it clearly: “the deepest foundation of our unity as a religious family we will always find in the Eucharist, which perpetuates the sacrifice of the Cross”51. And another of the Directories beautifully says that yes, “shared tradition, common works, rational structures, pooled resources, common constitutions and esprit de corps are all elements that can help to build and strengthen unity; but the foundation of unity is communion in Christ established by the one founding charism”52.

It is in the Eucharist, celebrated and adored, “that the communion of spirits is built, the premise for all growth in fraternity”53. This is a theological reality, not a strategic convenience. The Eucharist is the center of our life54, the axis that sustains everything; “it is the root of the invisible common union”55 (you know very well that there is an invisible ecclesial communion –of each man with God and with the other men who share in the divine nature, in the same faith, in the same Spirit– and another visible one –which is our communion with the Church here on earth through doctrine, the sacraments, etc.). Well then, thanks to the Eucharist, wherever we may be, we know ourselves united to one another in the Incarnate Word.

Listen to what Fr. Buela says in his characteristic and masterly style: “The Eucharist is the source and creative force of communion among the members of the Church precisely because it unites each one of them with Christ himself: “really partaking of the Body of the Lord in the breaking of the eucharistic bread, we are taken up into communion with him and with one another: ‘Because the bread is one, we, though many, are one body, for we all partake of the one bread’”56. That is why the Pauline expression the Church is the Body of Christ means that the Eucharist, in which the Lord gives us his Body and transforms us into one single Body57, is the place where the Church permanently expresses herself in her most essential form: present everywhere and yet only one, just as Christ is one”58.

The Directory of Contemplative Life emphasizes this very thing, saying: “A religious community is never more united than when it gathers around the altar for the Sacrifice of the Eucharist, the sign of unity”59. Indeed, “the Eucharist is the bond of union of the Christian family”60, and obviously of our family. “Take away the Eucharist and fraternity among us will disappear,”61 St. Peter Julian Eymard told his own.

The contemplative life, spousal and sacrificial, is born of the Eucharist and proclaims it from the silence of the cloister62. Thus the life of each one of you must proclaim the Eucharist, the sacrament of Christ’s love to the end, and not only individually speaking, but as a community: acting with esprit de corps, as we said before.

St. Edith Stein, another marvel of a contemplative soul, said: “To live the eucharistic life is to go completely out of the small circle of one’s own life and grow into the infinity of the life of Christ”63.

The heart of the Church beats with a eucharistic rhythm64, said the Polish Pope. This is the rhythm with which the Heart of Christ continues to beat, and that is why it is welcomed by so many souls –as it was by the holy Co-Patroness of this monastery, Maria Elizabeth Hesselblad, who in her search for the true fold fell to her knees before the Blessed Sacrament as it passed by, upon hearing within her Christ saying to her: “I am the one you seek”– and so it will continue to beat until the end of time, until at last we are all one.

That is why your commitment on behalf of Christian unity highlights all the more the need for prayer before the Blessed Sacrament. St. John Paul the Great said: “Christians who aspire to unity must first of all lift their eyes to heaven and ask God to renew in us the longing for unity, through the inspiration of the Holy Spirit. Unity can be achieved only with the help of divine grace”65.

That is why, even though perhaps I need not tell you, I invite you to persevere always without losing heart in prayer, asking for the unity of Christians, among whom we ourselves are also found; so please always pray for the unity of our whole religious family. Indeed, “asking together already implies a step toward unity among those who ask,”66 Pope Benedict XVI affirmed.

May all who come here know that people do not hide behind the enclosure. Behind the enclosure there is love. With the love with which Christ loved to the end. This love for the Eucharistic Christ is the evangelical leaven: the leaven that leavens the whole mass of dough67 and that is so badly needed.

3. Our service to the Unity of the Church

Finally, as the third point of this talk, I would like to speak to you of the cause of ecumenism as one of the proper apostolates of our beloved Religious Family, which is the specific service we render on behalf of the unity of the Church.

Note that the Directory of Missions Ad Gentes affirms that “the ultimate aim of mission is to enable people to share in the communion that exists between the Father and the Son: the disciples must live in unity with one another, remaining in the Father and the Son, so that the world may know and believe”68. That is, the objective of mission is precisely that they may all be one which Christ entrusted to us, and for this he commands us to live in unity, to be of one mind, as we have already said. If this is not so, there happens what St. John Paul II so aptly said: “A divided apostolate annihilates itself,”69 and the work that ought to fulfill Christ’s desire –that all may be one– is hindered or distorted. For as Scripture says: a family divided cannot stand70; but if we are united, this gives our Family an enormous apostolic strength.

Therefore, in one way or another, the apostolates of our Religious Family carry within them that invitation to unity. I very much like what the Directory of Missions Ad Gentes says: “The missionary impulse therefore belongs to the inner nature of the Christian life and also inspires ecumenism”71. That is, if one is truly Christian, one is a missionary, and therefore necessarily tends to want others to come to the knowledge of the Truth72, as the Apostle St. Paul said. Fr. Buela says that from the notion of the Church as sacrament of salvation “it follows that the Church is not a reality folded in upon herself, but permanently open to missionary and ecumenical dynamism, for she has been sent into the world to proclaim and bear witness to, to make present and to extend the mystery of communion that constitutes her: to gather all people and all things in Christ73; to be for all an ‘inseparable sacrament of unity’74”75.

Therefore, the apostolate of ecumenism, as our Proper Law makes quite clear, is not a ‘mission on our own account,’ but rather our radical response to the call of the Church, especially through the Second Vatican Council76. It is not “a renunciation of the truth”77; “it is not a tactic of the moment, nor is it opportunism or a craving for novelty”78 .

Rather, we hold –as the Magisterium of the Church itself affirms– that “authentic ecumenism is the essential vocation of the Church; it is an affirmation of one’s own Catholic identity and at the same time an eager search for the sheep outside the fold; it is to enter into the mind of Christ, who prophesied that there will be one flock and one shepherd (cf. Jn 10:16); it is to enter into his heart, which prayed that all may be one (cf. Jn 17:21)”79.

In fact, the apostolate of ecumenism is one of the first to be listed by our Constitutions in the list of our proper apostolates80, and hence our commitment to work in the ecumenical endeavor81 particularly –though not exclusively, of course– through prayer82, as our Proper Law explicitly commands us repeatedly83. And our contemplatives of both branches in particular, whether they are in this monastery or anywhere else, are asked “to pray and offer penances […] for ecumenism”84. Fr. Buela says that it is a “particularly important task”85, which “strongly calls for everyone’s ecumenical commitment toward full communion in the unity of the Church; that unity ‘which Christ bestowed on his Church from the beginning, and which we believe subsists indefectibly in the Catholic Church and hope will grow until the consummation of the ages’86”87. That is also the concomitant desire in all that we do.

What is more, “all the missionary and apostolic work [of our beloved Religious Family] is founded on the conviction that He must reign88”89, as is explicitly stated in our Proper Law. That is to say, in all that we do we want all of us to be one under the most provident and most merciful rule of the Incarnate Word. Because the very nature of the Kingdom of God is the communion of all human beings with one another and with God90.

* * * * *

Our desire must also be the one St. Peter Julian Eymard instilled in his own, and which I too would like to leave you as a message: “That his kingdom may come, that it may grow, that it may be raised up and perfected: this is what we must desire; that where he is not loved or known, he may be; that all may complete in themselves the work of his incarnation and redemption. […] Ask continually for the conversion of bad Catholics who have almost no faith left. Ask that those who have it may keep it. Those who have a family, ask that all its members may keep the faith. […] Moreover, we must console Jesus Christ. Ask him to raise up holy priests in his Church, those apostolic and saving priests who give their character to their century, who conquer new kingdoms for God. […] Let us make him reign and let us work to extend his reign everywhere. […] Forward; make Jesus Christ reign in you. […] Serve our Lord; console him, kindle the fire of his love wherever it has not yet caught, and work to establish his reign, the reign of his love”91.

That is why I encourage you to take up with great courage and humility the share of the Church’s labor that falls to you, “knowing that unity is possible because it is a command and a promise of Jesus Christ himself: there will be one Shepherd and one flock”92. Fr. Buela says that “today more than ever, contemplative prayer and authentic ecumenism are necessary (not that of the “accommodation” of religious impiety)”93, not the false ecumenism in which all doctrinal distinctions disappear, even revealed ones”94, but that ecumenism which is authentic “according to the mind and heart of our Savior Jesus Christ”95, which demands heroism, which “does not exist without interior conversion”96, which requires a loving veneration for the truth97, and which is ultimately a grace that must be implored.

Dear Mother and Sisters: The unity so longed for by our Lord must begin with our own firm and constant unity, above all with Him, for from this arises the commitment of fraternal charity among ourselves as a family and consequently with the whole Church.

In a community gathered as a true family around the Incarnate Word, in which communion of hearts reigns –having one soul and one heart in God–; in which community of prayer reigns; in which communion of activities reigns; from such a community there flows a power that makes fruitful every particle of truth wherever it may be found.

I encourage you with all my heart to continue dedicating yourselves to this most important and urgent cause of the unity of the Church. Be always faithful to your vocation, guard the spirit of our Institute, and zealously preserve and make fruitful the gift that has been granted to you. May the search for God and the conquest of his love be the end of your lives.

Sr. Corpus Domini said that when she was in heaven she wanted us to ask her for many things98. Well, help me ask her that our Lord may send laborers into his harvest and that he may preserve –and increase!– the fervor and missionary zeal in all of us, and that we may be able to ‘attach,’ so to speak, to each of our missions a monastery that prays to awaken the will of non-Christians to hear the gospel and that makes the word of salvation fruitful in hearts…99

Paraphrasing our beloved Spiritual Father, today I too want to tell you: union within the Church and among the Christian churches, the ever firmer and more lasting union of our Religious Family, must be the daily obsession100 of each one of you.

May the consoling words of Exodus, The Lord reigns forever! proposed for meditation during the Octave for Christian Unity, encourage you in this most noble task.

May Most Holy Mary, our Blessed Mother, enlighten you and move you also to be always a witness of union in truth and charity. May our most pure Mother, the Virgin of Luján, enfold us all in a tender maternal embrace.

Thank you very much for the invitation, and I encourage you always to continue with these initiatives that do so much good for the cause of Christ. And I too commend myself very much to your prayers, all right?

God bless you.

Pontinia, Italy
January 25, 2018

“The devil you know not”
Part III

We continue with the publication of the Conference, following Louis J. Cameli’s book The Devil You Know Not. We have discussed the first strategy the devil uses, which is deception, and today we will speak of the second strategy:

  • Division – But Jesus, knowing their thoughts, said to them: “Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste, and house falls upon house. And if Satan is divided against himself, how will his kingdom stand? For—as you say—I cast out demons by the power of Beelzebul (Lk 11:17-18).

A second strategy of the devil is precisely to cause division. The devil tries at all costs to cause division and disunity. For his efforts are aimed at working directly against the heart of our redemption in Christ, which is precisely our reconciliation and our participation in communion with the unity of the Trinity. All the attacks of the devil that have to do with division represent the counterpoint to the work of Jesus Christ: healing, reconciliation, union.

For all of us, who are on the way to union with God, which is the unity for which Christ prayed at the Last Supper (“that they may all be one”), it is decisive that we know and be alert to the ways in which the enemy will try to undermine this unity. And we will try to see this at the different levels of our life: personal, interpersonal, social and spiritual.

  • Personal: as we struggle with “what-we-are-and-what-we-are-not,” the devil tries to stir up waves of discouragement (another great strategy that we will see in another talk). It is easy to become discouraged when one is trying interiorly to put all the pieces together and make something coherent of one’s life. We have all had moments of discouragement, with their ever-present companion, which is the temptation to stop trying (to stop trying). There we already begin to see a little of the devil’s basic strategy. He will try by every means to paralyze us in what concerns the project of giving unity to our life, of making what I say agree with what I think, of making the way I act a consequence of the faith I carry in my soul—in a word: while I try to be coherent. Generally, discouragement is quite effective in this regard. But he can also make us think that it is completely out of our reach (way beyond our reach), like the one who realizes that he is light-years away from holiness and therefore stops even trying. In others he instills fear, because he presents to them as something ghostly the “truly determining oneself” of which St. John of the Cross speaks. They do not even want to look at the book of the “Dark Night.” And yes, fear can easily make us retreat. The devil can also suggest that we compare ourselves with others, usually with the plan of making us overestimate the progress of others and underestimate our own: “Look how patient this one is… and I still get angry over nothing”; “How impressive! What a gift this one has for discernment, and it takes me years, and on top of that most of the time I get it wrong”; and so on, thousands of examples. What all those thoughts do is stop us abruptly. Others he makes impatient, because they think that holiness is like that, a straight ladder to heaven, where one only advances upward, and since he experiences that this is not his case he becomes agitated, gets angry… and then what does he do? He stops trying. Again and again, the devil’s plan is that we stop trying to reach holiness. And the easiest way is by making us draw back from the self-giving we have made of ourselves. A mistake. A grave mistake. The devil always tries to put an end to every possibility of self-giving and of self-donation to God and, for God, to our neighbor.

St. Isaac Jogues, a few months before his death, wrote a letter in which he said: “Although I am wretched in the extreme and have made bad use of the graces God has given me in this country, I do not lose heart. God still takes the trouble to help me to be better and gives me new opportunities to be better. […] My hope is in God, who really does not need my achievements to accomplish His designs. All we need is to try to be faithful to Him and not ruin His work with our miseries.” That is the spirit.

Now, in order to give ourselves we must first possess ourselves. Our Constitutions say it: “We want to form authentically free men, masters of themselves, who by possessing themselves can give themselves totally”1. This possessing oneself means that there must be unity, integrity and coherence in our life. I am not a priest only when I celebrate Mass, and afterwards a parish lad who goes off to the gym. I am not saying that going to the gym is wrong, but that at every moment I am a priest and therefore I must keep the conduct proper to a priest (in custody of the eyes, in the way I dress, in what I listen to while I work out, in the attention—always moderated by temperance—that I give to the care of the body, etc).

Obviously none of us here wants to be the devil’s plaything and fall into his traps. So what are the means we have to put all the pieces of our life together and offer them to God?

In the first place, coherence of life implies engaging in a process of change. It is what we call ongoing conversion. Our Proper Law is very clear on this: “A religious who is not willing to go through the second and the third conversion, or who does nothing concrete to achieve it, even though he is with us in body, does not belong to our spiritual family”2. Word for word. And then the usual: the ascetical practices that all of you know: the discipline of denying oneself in order to be freer and more purified of the attachments that hang on us. Fasting, the penitential practices we learned in the novitiate and that are still in force, the examination of conscience that explores how we have failed to fulfill God’s will and makes us aware of the ways in which God’s grace is active in our life. Spiritual direction—honest, serious, supernatural—which presents one’s experiences to the director so that he may help to identify the way in which God is leading him and working his sanctification. And of course, prayer. That is essential, and without it everything else we do will fall to the ground.

Now notice that all these means have as their ‘objective,’ if you will, to lead us to knowledge of ourselves. This self-knowledge leads us to order the pieces of our life in a coherent way, that is, according to God’s plan. Knowledge of oneself is key to self-acceptance, which is in turn key to our self-giving to God.

  • At the interpersonal level: that is, division among ourselves. It is undeniable: divisions among men exist, with greater or lesser consequences. That is sad and at times it breaks your heart.

Now, we must not blame the devil for all divisions. Each of us on our own can separate himself from the group and steadily maintain that separation. Nevertheless, one of the devil’s main works is to cause division, setting some against others. Certainly the devil can tempt us directly to separate ourselves from others, or he can exploit the divisions that we ourselves have caused. In either case, division is certainly one of the devil’s priorities.

On the other hand, it must be clear that unity among us is something willed by God and that our final destiny is union with God Himself. Anything that moves us in any direction other than that is clearly not from God.

The devil can use a variety of situations to create divisions and separations. For example, the promise of something better can draw us away or separate us from those who deserve our most loyal union. Like those who pursued studies paid for by their Institute at expensive universities, published books, traveled the world… and, for the promise of a paid job in a diocese, with the illusion of a ‘very good’ relationship with the bishop, and who knows what other motivations, separated from their Institute and ended badly. The promise of something better really is tempting—above all if one is going through ‘low moments,’ through times of desolation, through community difficulties, etc.—which can lead to painful and devastating divisions and separations, as happens so many times in cases of adultery or in the abandonment of friends.

Another springboard for separation and division is the feelings of anger, resentment and contempt that we may have toward other people. The story of Judas is an example of this process. First he gets angry because ‘they wasted the perfume’; Our Lord “rebukes” him by praising the woman’s gesture, and there resentment enters him; and finally he despises Christ by betraying Him, separating himself from Him and from the other disciples. Not infrequently, when we feel contempt for others, when their behavior exasperates us, we distance ourselves from those people and do everything to avoid them.

Yet one more motive that can cause separation is fear. At times we are afraid to show ourselves friends of others because of the potential consequences this may bring us. A classic example is Peter’s denial. I need not go into detail. But the story of the incredible separation of Peter, one of the apostles closest to Our Lord, in the decisive hour, shows how powerful the fear of what might happen to us can be, to the point of separating ourselves, ‘to save ourselves,’ at least momentarily, from those who deserve our fullest adherence, who have given us so much, etc.3.

Likewise, greed can be another of the causes that lead to the separation of people. Not only financial greed, as when someone says “there isn’t enough here for you and me,” and so the married couple separates to seek their fortune separately. But it can also be greed for other goods: like the religious who “separates himself” from the others because he wants attention only for himself, or an apostolate only for himself; and it is also true that some simply separate themselves from the Institute out of greed for goods… because they believe that with a cross on their shoulders (the discomforts associated with poverty, misunderstandings with their superiors, the harshness of the mission… and a long etcetera) they will get nowhere.

  • A division on a larger scale is the division that money, power, status, and invoking religion to force domination bring with them. It is precisely forgetfulness of the principle and foundation, but at a more universal level, and it is almost literally the following of the earthly king.

Schlier’s book “Principados y Potestades” describes very well how Satan exerts his influence to twist God’s plan in an almost universal context. It says: “He begins to exercise his power above all in the general spirit of the world, in the spirit of an age and in public life, as well as in the spirit of a people, of a country, etc. This spirit, in which «the age of this world» holds sway, is not a loose spirit suspended in the air, but is breathed in by men and through them is taken up into their institutions, in one circumstance or another. It also becomes condensed in particular situations. Thus it comes to be a historically intense and powerful spirit, from which no individual can escape. Man is ruled by this spirit, and it presents itself to him as self-evident. To act, think or say anything against it is nonsense, when it is not even unjust and criminal. In it life and all other things are carried on. And «in it» one thinks: just as this spirit presents things to us, with all the concepts and valuations proper to its presentation. The prince of this world, by means of the atmosphere he dominates, makes the world and existence itself, the things, the relationships and situations of this world, appear as his own and as he understands them.”

The counterpoint to the devil’s work of division is the reconciliation and unity that the Incarnate Word brought us. The most direct and solid affirmation of this unifying work of Christ is in the Letter to the Ephesians: But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ. For Christ is our peace; He has united the two peoples into one, breaking down the wall of enmity that separated them, and abolishing in His own flesh the Law with its commandments and ordinances. Thus He created out of the two peoples one single new Man in His own person, making peace, and reconciled them to God in one single Body, through the cross, destroying the enmity in His person. And He came to proclaim the Good News of peace, peace to you who were far off, and peace also to those who were near. For through Christ we all without distinction have access to the Father in one same Spirit. Therefore you are no longer strangers and guests, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God. You are built upon the apostles and the prophets, who are the foundations, while the cornerstone is Jesus Christ Himself. In Him the whole building, well joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord.

Clearly, the victory of Christ with His peace and His unity rises above all of Satan’s attempts to divide and separate us interiorly, among ourselves and at a universal level.

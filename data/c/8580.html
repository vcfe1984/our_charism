The specific end of our Institute, as we read in our Constitutions, declares with all vehemence that “we commit all our strength to inculturate the Gospel, that is, to prolong the Incarnation in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man1, in accordance with the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church2”3.

What is more, the proper charism of the IVE consists in “working, in utmost docility to the Holy Spirit and within the imprint of Mary, in order to bring under the lordship of Jesus Christ all that is authentically human, even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”4. This makes us “essentially missionary and Marian”5. And, for this reason, “pastoral undertakings are essential to us”6.

That is to say, we are a missionary Congregation, a Congregation of evangelizers. So much so that the evangelization of culture is the reason for being of our Institute. An evangelization which, as has already been said, is always understood under the guidance of the Church (“in accordance with the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church”); a universal and integral evangelization (“in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man”); an evangelization that takes due account of today’s needs and gives them evangelical answers (“working, in utmost docility to the Holy Spirit and within the imprint of Mary”); an evangelization that uses the most suitable means (“in order to bring under the lordship of Jesus Christ all that is authentically human”); an evangelization made witness through a truly religious way of life (“we commit all our strength” to “working… even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”); an evangelization preceded and accompanied by prayer (working “in utmost docility to the Holy Spirit and within the imprint of Mary” in order to obtain the “grace of knowing how to act, concretely, to prolong Christ in families, in education, in the media of communication, in men of thought and in every other legitimate manifestation of the life of man”7); driven by the love of Christ and of men (“to make each man ‘like a new Incarnation of the Word’”8); carried out under the imprint and protection of her who is the Star of evangelization (Most Holy Mary).

The 40 years of the Institute’s existence have proven the nobility, beauty and fruitfulness of this charism that has been bequeathed to us. Today we glory in it, and with holy affection for our Institute and for our Founder we consider it our duty to live the charism, to preserve it jealously, to deepen it and to develop it throughout history, in a homogeneous continuity, whatever the circumstances. For only if we keep alive the inheritance that Fr. Buela left us, that is, in generous fidelity to the original charism and in holy familiarity with the Incarnate Word9, will we be able to find the true motivations for our ecclesial and missionary service, and then we will have made the contribution that the Church expects of us in the building of the Kingdom of Christ.

In accordance with the above, “it is necessary to deepen fidelity to one’s own charism through an ever better knowledge of the history of the Institute, of its particular mission and of the spirit of its Founder, accompanied by a corresponding effort to live it personally and in community”10. In this sense, it is of capital importance that we refer this time to the pastoral formation required of all the members of the Institute in keeping with our proper apostolates and in harmony with the spirit of the Founder.

1. Proper apostolates

The Constitutions of the Institute of the Incarnate Word establish that, according to our charism, “the Institute of the Incarnate Word will take on the apostolates most conducive to the inculturation of the Gospel”11.

“In a merely illustrative sense, the preferential fields of action are set out:

a) In its spiritual dimension the Institute will undertake the evangelization of culture through the sanctification of individual persons. This will be done preferably by preaching the Spiritual Exercises according to the method and spirit of St. Ignatius of Loyola, and, in addition, through spiritual direction.

b) In its intellectual dimension the Institute will give priority to priestly formation in the Seminaries, to education at all its levels, to the formation of lay leaders by means of the Courses of Catholic Culture, and to the media of social communication.

c) In its dimension of popular ministry, preference will be given to helping parishes through preaching (triduums, novenas, patronal feasts) and the administration of the sacrament of Reconciliation. Likewise, priority will be given to popular missions in parishes and rural areas, according to the method and spirit of St. Alphonsus Mary de Liguori, and in all their forms, whether intensive or permanent missions, youth missions, children’s missions, missions to the sick, to factories, open missions, etc. In response to the requests of bishops, the direction of parishes may be accepted, preferably in mission areas or those in greater need. In everything great attention will be paid to respecting, recovering and elevating the religious and folkloric traditions of the place so that the Gospel may be better received and put down deeper roots in hearts.

d) Likewise, priority must be given to the care of the poor, the sick and the needy of every kind: the charity of Christ urges us (2 Cor 5:14), concretely practicing charity as a witness, in the first place, to all the members of our Institutes that “charity, charity alone will save the world; blessed are those who have the grace to be victims of charity!”12, and that charity is indispensable for evangelizing the culture, as the end of the one who acts and as the end of the work. Otherwise, ‘the civilization of love’ will not be attained”13.

“Likewise, a preferential apostolic task will be the care, as required, of the Servants of the Lord and the Virgin of Matará, as well as helping ‘with special diligence’ the institutes, associations, movements, or brotherhood of the third branch of lay Christian faithful, so that they may be shaped by the genuine spirit of our family”14.

“The members of the Institute dedicated to the eminently spiritual apostolate will work convinced of the immense value that the sanctification of the laity has for the life of the Church. This apostolate will drink, as from its source, from the works and example of the great masters of Catholic pastoral ministry”15.

The fact is that Fr. Buela fostered not only the proper apostolates but even new and untried apostolates, provided they were according to God. And this from the stage of formation onward, as all those who lived with him can testify, because what he taught he himself practiced. That is why, paraphrasing the message he gave to the Servants, we can say to all the members of the IVE: “We need to form religious who have spark, because untried apostolates must be undertaken which perhaps could not be done in other eras and today must be done. They must be very well prepared, experienced men, with a great deal of discipline”16.

2. How did Fr. Buela conceive the pastoral formation of the IVE seminarians?

The end of the pastoral formation of our candidates, Fr. Buela said, is “Jesus Christ, Shepherd of shepherds (1 Pet 5:4), inasmuch as He is the Supreme Model and inasmuch as the flock is His: feed my sheep (Jn 21:16). That is why all the formation of candidates for the priesthood is oriented to preparing them in a specific way to communicate the charity of Christ, the Good Shepherd’”17. Toward this our Founder directed his best energies, and on that basis he planned the entire formation of the IVE.

a. What is primary

Fr. Buela placed great emphasis on the fact that formation for the priesthood must be oriented to preparing the seminarians to “be shepherds of souls”18, since the salvation of souls is the end of the Incarnation19 and the greatest good of the exercise of the priestly ministry20.

Therefore, the priest’s field of action is the field of souls, of their relations with God and of their interior relationship with their fellow men. That is where the priest has an essential function to perform. Certainly, whenever circumstances require it, he should not exempt himself from also providing material assistance, through concrete works of charity. But, as has been said, that is ultimately a secondary service, which must never lose sight of the principal service of the priest, which is to assist souls spiritually so that they may love God above all things and their neighbor as themselves.

Accordingly, Fr. Buela −in a sermon that could in some way be considered emblematic on this subject− impressed upon us that “The good shepherd is the one who lays down his life for the sheep (Jn 10:11.15), not the one who takes advantage of the wool, the milk and the meat of the sheep. The good shepherd is the one who defends the sheep. The bad shepherd, the hireling, who is not a shepherd, to whom the sheep do not belong, sees the wolf coming, abandons the sheep and flees, and the wolf seizes them and scatters them, because he is a hireling and cares nothing for the sheep (Jn 10:12-13). And in this, as in other things, the faithful people have an infallible sense of smell given by the instinct of grace, by the interior Master, the Holy Spirit.

The good shepherd is followed by his sheep: the sheep follow him, because they know his voice (Jn 10:4). And this happens everywhere, even in the most difficult places, humanly speaking. That is why, for one who is a good shepherd, no assignment is a punishment. […] The bad shepherd, who uses the sheep for himself, is not usually followed by the flock as a whole, only perhaps by a small group, and he is the one chiefly to blame for his pastoral failures, some of them even resounding ones”21.

b. Characteristics of an IVE missionary

Our Founder taught, then, that this office −in imitation of the Good Shepherd− requires certain characteristics on our part22:

– “To wear oneself out, that is, to have the disposition of wanting to be consumed for the good of others; and to wear oneself out is to want to persevere in being consumed little by little for others until the end. It is to spend one’s time, one’s goods, one’s strength, in a word, one’s life.

– The Lord said to Peter three times: Feed my sheep (Jn 21:15.17), that is, feed them with your word, feed them with your example, feed them with your temporal help. And this is precisely what every shepherd must provide to the faithful.

– […] The priest must be clearly aware that he is a pontiff, a bridge-builder, and a two-way bridge: from God to men, and from men to God; and that he is good only for being walked on by men.

– He must spare no effort to bring the Gospel of Jesus Christ to as many men as he can. He must not claim anything else. That is why, above all now in these times, he must be ready for an incisive, enthusiastic ministry; not one of waiting, but of proposal. […]

– This program implies availability to administer the sacraments, even at the cost of many sacrifices. I am thinking especially of Confession, of the Anointing of the sick… even in the midst of the cruelest persecutions, the deepest desolations, the most terrible dark nights and the most frightful temptations.

– He does not flinch at any difficulty, nor shrink back before any obstacle! And he should reach nightfall exhausted from his whole day’s priestly work, worn out by pastoral endeavors and longing for the necessary restorative rest.

– The priest must not renounce a priori any of the forms of preaching the Word. One of the most important forms is writing, because the written word endures and reaches a greater number of people; publishing books, spreading the Catholic press.

– Likewise, one must think through new problems and seek effective solutions with creativity, with great trust in the power of God, who continues to work tirelessly in the world. We must not be afraid of untried forms of ministry, provided they are according to God.

– I am convinced that priestly happiness −and the happiness of the seminarian− lies in that spending and being spent. That is the mystique of priestly work. And what is the measure of spending and being spent? I consider it to be the rule St. Ignatius gives for penance: ‘the more and more, the greater and better, provided only that the subiecto is not ruined, nor any notable illness follows’23. We must prepare ourselves even to work in Heaven too, as St. Thérèse of the Child Jesus said: ‘My Heaven will be to go on doing good on earth’24”25.

– “Pastorally, there is nothing more effective than total death to one’s own self”26. And both by example and by his fatherly exhortation, that is what Fr. Buela transmitted with all his grit to all the members −in formation or not−. To the point that, in that key of self-denial or total death to one’s own self, he forged the concept of pastoral action for every IVE missionary: “For us pastoral work is a cross, not a pretext for escapism”27. “The priest,” he would say, “is a champion of sacrifice in a comfortable and hedonistic world”28. For this reason, “in shepherding, you must know how to give yourselves. […] One cannot demand the same of all [the faithful]; one must absolutely not coerce anyone, for anything, for any reason; you must not impose your own judgments and views, nor your opinions, nor your tastes”29. And in this sense he often cited the example of holy missionaries who were ‘failures’ according to the world but who gave great glory to God and to the Church precisely because of that, because they knew how to die to themselves totally and completely: St. Louis Mary Grignion de Montfort, St. Isaac Jogues, the Martyrs of China, the Martyrs of Barbastro, etc.

Now, all this generosity, availability, creativity or inventiveness, that capacity for sacrificing oneself to the utmost, this zeal for the salvation of souls that Fr. Buela strove to teach the members, was unfailingly accompanied by his example of magnanimity and magnificence when it came to doing apostolate. It was like the unmistakable seal of his ministry. That is why he left in writing that “for such [apostolic] works it is necessary to possess material goods, whose size and quality is established in relation to the work undertaken. We want religious who live poverty to the utmost, but who are also magnanimous and magnificent in undertaking apostolic works, according to the will of God, without shrinking back from the difficulties and expenses that must be incurred in the various apostolic commitments, trusting in divine Providence for this”30. Consequently, he set down in the Proper Law that “a pastor must be magnificent when it comes to works of apostolate for the good of others, but he must be poor when it comes to his own style of life: house, clothing, automobile, etc.”31. And years later he explained: “All the charitable activity of the pastor and of the Christian community must stand out for its uprightness, loyalty and magnanimity, and thus manifest God’s gratuitous love for man, who makes his sun rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the just and the unjust (Mt 5:45)”32. And the Directory of Consecrated Life insists: “the religious of our Institute will know how to make the efforts and expenditures necessary to fulfill adequately the apostolic ends we pursue, according to our proper charism”33.

Countless anecdotes could be cited regarding this distinctive note in the way of doing apostolate that Fr. Buela taught. Here we simply recall that time when some young people from Salta visited the major Seminary and how, even though there was scarcity, the Father produced money “from where there was none,” saying “we have to look after them well,” thereby giving us an example of the great work of charity that hospitality is. And if he did this with young people who occasionally visited the Seminary, his magnificence was even greater when it came to helping a priest; as on that occasion when one of them had to go to Rome to study and Fr. Buela, very aware of the good that this meant, gathered the money he had and helped him with the fare, even though this meant that he himself was left with nothing. Even so, Fr. Buela did not squander money.

All of us who had the opportunity to do our formation alongside Fr. Buela remember the great magnificence he instilled in us when it came to caring for the poor. Thus, for example, on pizza Fridays sometimes almost as many pizzas were made for the poor as for the seminarians. It was also the Founder himself who came up with the idea of organizing the dinner for the poor once a year.

Not to mention his magnanimity and magnificence in starting the humanities high school in San Rafael with nothing; but he was not daunted, and he did it. The same can be said of the many works he began in the Institute.

In the light of these examples it is even better understood why he left in writing in the Directory of Spirituality that it is a temptation for the missionary to be “avaricious,” that is, “to choose apostolates according to their financial return, more interested in stipends than in the salvation of souls; [to such a one] the expenses of popular missions, Spiritual Exercises, etc., seem an unforgivable waste; [that is why] he does not invest in pastoral ministry but in financial firms, [and considers that] all the poor who knock at his door are freeloaders; he does not understand that one must provide oneself with purses that do not wear out (Lk 12:33)”34

c. Demands of pastoral formation in the IVE

Consequently our Founder insisted that, as regards the seminarian:

– “One must demand the utmost of him in his doctrinal formation, in such a way that he is not afraid of confrontation with modern culture. Not to be content with a number of ideas held in his head by pins, but with solid truths, by which one lives and for which one is capable of giving one’s life if it were necessary;

– one must demand of him a life of authentic discipline, personally assumed as a value of his own, capable of shunning subjective whim, bourgeois mediocrity, the miserable and habitual waste of time. In a special way, young people are struck by meeting consecrated souls who are not secularized either in their thinking, or in their speech, or in their conduct, or in their dress35;

– one must teach him that authentic apostolate is a cross, and not a superficial picnic. And that where the apostolate is most difficult, perhaps the Lord has arranged for the most beautiful flowers and the most splendid fruits to come forth; and that if these do not come forth, the sacrifice made for Him will never be sterile, but will flower and bear fruit somewhere else. This is where it must be said that we must form young men ‘who do not shy away from the missionary adventure’36. If a young man shies away from mission, first he will settle in, then he will lose interest in ‘the sheep’ entrusted to him, and finally he will neglect them, imposing ridiculous bureaucratic obstacles on them, content to lament the wickedness of the world and the difficulty of the present times”37. That is why he insisted on the centrality of the cross in the apostolate and repeated like a refrain that “pastoral ministry is cross, difficulties, dangers, sufferings […]. Those who empty the cross empty pastoral ministry. Do they empty the Cross of Christ? Then they carry out another ministry that has nothing to do with Catholic pastoral ministry. He may be a priest or a bishop, but if he empties the cross of Christ he is a failure, and hence so many strident and resounding pastoral failures”38.

This is the missionary imprint that must emerge in those we are forming, because, as our Founder rightly pointed out: “for me, if someone who wants to consecrate himself to God does not find love and joy in arid study, in austere discipline, in poverty that stings, in the arduous apostolate and in the difficult mission, he does not love the Cross of Christ and probably does not have a vocation”39. In this way he selected vocations by the love of the cross and the apostolic zeal they showed. In fact, he made a rigorous selection of vocations, and in this regard he maintained that “a house of formation where there is no selection, where the superiors do not verify the suitability of the candidates, becomes deserted”40. “A wide door to leave but a narrow one to enter” is what he always told us. “Let only those remain who have a proven vocation”41.

At the same time, Fr. Buela at all times exhorted “that seminarians have a ‘right knowledge of the mentality of the present age’ so as to be ‘prepared in good time to dialogue with the men of their age’42. The most impressive cultural phenomenon of this age being atheism in all its variants”43.

d. Errors to be avoided in the formation of missionaries

And he also warned in many ways and on countless occasions about the errors to be avoided in the pastoral formation of our members, saying to formators and those in formation:

– “some refuse all apostolate because they believe it is activism (or that it can fall into it) and they fall into quietism: against this, dedicate ALL the assigned time to the apostolate;

– others, very restless, carried away by disordered zeal, fall into activism (or Americanism): against this, dedicate ONLY the assigned time to the apostolate;

– there are those who believe that the seminary should not form SHEPHERDS.

– there are those who believe that the seminary apostolate is a necessary evil (and so they cleverly excuse themselves from the apostolate);

– there are those who believe that the apostolate is an excuse to leave the seminary and go out for a stroll;

− there are those who believe that it is an appendix (and, no!, it is essential, although it must be graduated);

– there are those who believe that seminarians ‘do nothing’ (because they do not celebrate Mass, do not hear confessions…);

– there are those who believe that the apostolate hinders contemplation (they confuse the contemplative life with a vegetative life).

Let us take the example of Christ ‘the Good Shepherd’! And of Most Holy Mary ‘Star of evangelization’!”44.

Thus, then, in accordance with what the Proper Law establishes, that “to be formed for the priesthood is to learn primarily from the pastoral charity of Christ”45, he insisted: “The principal office of every good shepherd is the ‘officium amoris,’ the office of love. […] That is why if the office of love is lacking, there is no shepherd worth the name. He will be a bureaucrat, a pettifogger, a shyster…, but not a good shepherd. He may know the various kinds of pastoral methods, but if concrete and effective love of neighbor is lacking, he will be like sounding brass or a clanging cymbal… he would be nothing … it would be of no use (1 Cor 1:1-3). It is love that drives missionaries to an apostolate without limit of place: Go into the whole world… (Mk 16:15)”46. And on another occasion he added: “he is not a good shepherd, nor does he know pastoral ministry, who uses only justice or who uses only mercy”47.

It is well known, but worth recalling here, that “anti-sermon” Fr. Buela preached with the purpose of moving those who heard him NEVER TO DO what he said in it. That is why we mention it here as erroneous attitudes or behaviors that we must watch so that they do not take root in our ranks. Referring, then, to the subject of pastoral formation in the seminary, our Founder said with his usual roguish wit: “Teach them a pastoral ministry that mistreats people, the simple and faithful people; let them make people feel their authority, let them distrust everyone, saying to themselves: ‘nobody is going to fool me.’ Let them not visit the families of their apostolates, nor play with the children and young people. Every missionary thought must be erased from their young hearts. If they do not love our own people, how are they going to love others who, to top it all off, speak other languages?

Teach them to be very familiar with girls; that is how Catholic marriages are usually formed, which we need more than consecrated vocations.

Teach them that the laity must take the place of priests and that they do it more competently. So that the roles get mixed up”48. In this way, he went on instilling in the members the true temper of an IVE missionary.

Fr. Buela understood very well that the study and the spiritual, human and pastoral formation of a missionary are intimately intertwined and ordered to corresponding to the noble mission of the Institute49. Hence one must “never forget that there is no authentic Catholic pastoral ministry without a deep spiritual life, without a solid doctrinal formation and without a manly discipline”50.

For this reason he hammered home that one must not set up a dialectic between study and pastoral ministry. Moreover, he added that “the lack of an authentically metaphysical intelligence renders shepherds of souls incapable of knowing reality, making precise diagnoses and applying timely remedies. Thus, for example, after at least 7 years of the Pastoral Plan ‘Marriage and Family,’ the National Congress enacted the law of absolute divorce in Argentina. Thus, for example, the proliferation of the emotional search for the divine, forming novices, seminarians, pastoral agents incapable of transcending the sphere of the sensible. And since the just man lives by faith (Rom 1:17)51 and faith is of what is not seen (‘de non visu’)52, faith is the evidence of things not seen (Heb 11:1), that is why there are so many defections and capitulations, and it seems that nothing is done seriously. Thus, for example, few are capable of grasping beauty, and fewer still are capable of making beautiful, lovely things, from sermons to churches, from writings to media appearances, from liturgical chants to pastoral actions. Hence the tendency to exchange the good for the merely pleasant, the beautiful for the pretty, the true for the vague53”54. To avoid this, Fr. Buela, while deploring “formalist or essentialist ‘pastoral programs,’ without grit and without biting into reality”55, exhorted those in our formation to acquire “a metaphysics with grit, one that bites into reality and is not occupied with stratospheric disquisitions or cerebral swoons, entertained with estimating how many angels can sit on the head of a pin”56.

That is to say, Fr. Buela understood that a solid and serious intellectual formation is a fundamental requirement of evangelization. That is why he wanted a Thomistic formation for our Institute, since there one will find the basis for total personal self-giving to Jesus and to His Church and because, incidentally, “it is the most lucid answer to the problem of atheism”57.

Now, if, as we said earlier, pastoral undertakings are essential to us58, consequently our task is to work, and not only to work but to do so committing all our strength to inculturating the Gospel, so as to be precisely tireless evangelizers59, as our Proper Law asks of us.

Notwithstanding this, with great realism our Founder warned us −and we should do the same with our candidates− that even though it is difficult to maintain the Catholic spirit when one is incessantly attacked in the faith, it must be borne in mind that “the temptation is to withdraw into oneself and not even use words that are ours but that the progressives so paw over −e.g., the poor−; for it would be a very grave error to shut oneself up in a clique spirit, concerned only with parochial interests, which ultimately end up following ‘parallel magisteria, ecclesially unacceptable and pastorally sterile,’ isolating oneself from the People of God and from their legitimate Pastors. Or did not St. Vincent de Paul, St. Elizabeth of Hungary, St. Joseph Benedict Cottolengo, St. Luigi Orione and so many others −none of them progressives− truly and without demagoguery take care of the poor, seeing in them Christ Himself? We must defend our Catholic faith and translate it into works: ‘Today, of the faith only what is defended is preserved,’ said Albino Lucinai [who later became John Paul I]. We must defend the Catholic faith, which wants to pour itself out over public and social reality, magnetizing everything for Christ the King. Not working explicitly so that Christ may reign over our peoples implies a certain apostasy from the faith”60. Along the same line, the Directory of Spirituality points out: “Whoever does not want to be an apostle, let him leave the Congregation: today, whoever is not an apostle of Jesus Christ and of the Church is an apostate”61.

So if on the one hand the missionary can become paralyzed, pastorally speaking, in the face of difficulties, on the other hand it is also possible to fall into activism. Fr. Buela forcefully warned us about this. For this reason, in one of his books he quotes a writing of St. Alberto Hurtado entitled “The sins of a man of action,” which in some way reflects the erroneous attitudes into which one must not fall and about which, therefore, those in formation must be warned. It reads as follows:

“The sins of a man of action:

‘To believe oneself indispensable to God.
Not to pray enough.
To lose contact with God.
To go too fast.
To want to go more quickly than God.
To make a pact, even a slight one,
with evil in order to succeed.
Not to give oneself wholly.
And to prefer oneself to the Church.
To esteem oneself more
than the work to be done,
or to seek oneself in action.
To work for oneself.
To seek one’s own glory.
To become proud.
To let oneself be cast down by failure.
To cloud over in the face of difficulties.
To undertake too much.
To give in to one’s natural impulses,
to one’s thoughtless or proud haste.
To stop controlling oneself.
To depart from one’s principles.
To work to do apologetics
and not out of love.
To make the apostolate a business,
even a spiritual one.
Not to strive to have a vision
as broad as possible.
Not to step back to see the whole.
Not to take account of the context of the problem.
To work without fear.
To improvise on principle.

Not to plan ahead.
Not to finish.
To rationalize excessively.
To be hesitant,
or to drown in details.
To always want to be right.
To give all the orders.
Not to be disciplined.
To evade small tasks.
Not to respect others,
not to leave them initiatives,
not to give them responsibilities.
To be hard on one’s associates
and on one’s superiors.
To despise the little ones,
the humble,
the less gifted.
Not to be grateful.
To be sectarian.
Not to be welcoming.
Not to love one’s enemies.
To treat everyone who opposes me
as if he were an enemy.
Not to accept contradiction gladly.
To be destructive through unjust or vain criticism.
To be habitually sad or in a bad mood.
To let oneself be drowned by worries about money.
Not to sleep enough.
Not to eat enough.
Not to preserve, through imprudence and without valid reason,
the fullness of one’s strength and physical gifts’”62.

So much for the errors to be avoided in seminary formation within the Institute.

e. What is truly important

Our Proper Law is very clear: “to evangelize consists principally in bringing the grace of God to all men, making a new humanity, that is, of new men created according to God in justice and true holiness (Eph 4:23-24)”63.

In accordance with this, Fr. Buela never tired of insisting that “the most important thing is grace, the absolute necessity of grace. The great teaching of the Doctor of the Church, Thérèse of the Child Jesus, is the absolute necessity of grace. The primacy, therefore, of the interior, of the spiritual”64. For “authentic evangelization must lead to and culminate in the worthy reception of the sacraments, since through them the grace of the Holy Spirit is ordinarily communicated”65.

“There is absolutely no true pastoral ministry if it does not have Jesus Christ as its beginning, center and end […], St. John Paul II: ‘It is not, then, a matter of inventing a new program. The program already exists. It is the same as ever, gathered from the Gospel and the living Tradition. It is centered, in the end, on Christ Himself, who is to be known, loved and imitated, so as to live in Him the Trinitarian life and with Him to transform history until its fulfillment in the heavenly Jerusalem. It is a program that does not change with the changing of times and cultures, although it takes account of time and culture for a true dialogue and effective communication. This ever-same program is ours for the third millennium’66”67.

Hence Fr. Buela could not conceive of pastoral activity that did not lead to Christ in the Eucharist. “‘The Eucharist −sacramental memorial of the Death and Resurrection of Christ, real and effective re-presentation of the one redemptive Sacrifice, source and summit of the Christian life and of all evangelization−68 is the means and the end of the priestly ministry, since ‘all ecclesiastical ministries and works of the apostolate are intimately bound up with the Eucharist and ordered to it’69. The priest, consecrated to perpetuate the Holy Sacrifice, thus manifests his identity in the most evident way’”70. And he explained: “The new evangelization must mean for the faithful [and also for us] a likewise new clarity about the centrality of the sacrament of the Eucharist, summit of the whole Christian life71. On the one hand, because ‘no Christian community is built up unless it has as its root and hinge the celebration of the Holy Eucharist’72, but also because ‘the other sacraments, like all ecclesiastical ministries and works of the apostolate, are united with the Eucharist and ordered to it. For in the Holy Eucharist is contained the whole spiritual good of the Church’73”74.

Indeed, all the apostolates that have been carried out since the beginnings of the Institute have had a marked Eucharistic note, either because they begin or end with the Eucharistic celebration or because they lead to it. By way of illustration, we cite the example of the camps. Fr. Buela always wanted Mass to be celebrated at the camps, and therefore a priest always went, sometimes at the cost of great sacrifices, so that the children, young people or whoever it might be could take part in Holy Mass. Our Founder said, then: “The only sensible way to conceive of camp for those in our formation is as a ‘school of life’. Camp must be formative; it must help the child or young person to know his homeland better and love it, to understand concretely what it is to be Catholic, that is, to know how to live his religion well, to learn to be disciplined, clean, responsible, helpful, well-mannered… In my day I grew tired of hearing in every tone and sequence that Christianity is life. But that was always mere intellectualism, because concretely they did not teach the life that the Gospel entails. This happens, concretely, when the child or young person takes part in a ‘school of life’ camp, or in popular missions, or in serious Spiritual Exercises. And we call camp a ‘school of Life’ because of Him who said: I am… the Life (Jn 14:6) and came so that we might have: …life and life in abundance (Jn 10:10)”75. Note the emphasis Fr. Buela placed on giving camp participants the possibility of having personal and sure contact with Jesus in the Eucharist.

“On the other hand,” he would say, “a camp that is a kind of free-for-all is not usually of much use; without governance, without schedules, without work, without order, there is not usually any formation in the good with something so shapeless. Above all, without a set time for getting up, they cease to be a ‘school of life.’ They are of very little use. It is nothing more than a bit of tourism”76.

3. Some pastoral criteria and ways of doing pastoral ministry in the IVE

Our Institute not only has its proper apostolates but also a particular style of doing apostolate77. Therefore, with the same energy with which Fr. Buela repudiated apostolic activism in some members, he also repudiated those who, dialectically, under the excuse of “saving” religious life and the corresponding formation, neglected the works of apostolate that were to be carried out. To avoid one thing or the other, our Founder left us this criterion: “It is a matter of fulfilling the duty of one’s state78 and giving each thing its place according to its objective hierarchy79, and according to the charism of the Institute, in order to fulfill the will of God perfectly”80. What is more, we have committed ourselves by solemn vows “not to shy away from the missionary adventure”81, and that is much more than a slogan…

Consequently, our Proper Law considers as one of the general difficulties we members of the Institute may go through the fact that “there are no enthusiastic projects for the future; when there are no present endeavors exultant with ideals […] when generosity in self-giving diminishes in the community and one gradually falls into the bourgeois spirit of disordered comfort; […] when, because of the tree of difficulties, one loses sight of the forest of things that are going well…”82.

In this sense, we must not forget that we are asked to be “tirelessly evangelizers”83, that is, to tend continually and in an orderly way toward the realization of apostolic projects.

That is why, from the beginning, great emphasis was placed on the seminarians learning to do pastoral ministry in practice according to the Institute’s particular style of doing apostolate. We consider that this must not be lost but, on the contrary, must be firmly rooted in each of the members −in formation or not− in order to preserve the missionary and charismatic identity of the Institute.

In his work El Señor es mi Pastor. Memoria y Profecía, Fr. Buela relates that he had a very definite idea of how he wanted the pastoral formation of the members of the IVE to be, based not only on the Magisterium of the Church but also on his own experience. We transcribe the account here since it seems to us that it can be quite illuminating, both for those who give or receive formation in the Institute and for those who already have some years of mission behind them and are experiencing the well-known apostolic tribulations that no one lacks, nor does God want anyone to lack. It reads as follows: “I realized then that they talked all day long about the poor, but did nothing for the poor. They talked about pastoral ministry, but they did not know what vocational ministry was, or youth ministry, the Ignatian Exercises, popular missions, catechism, oratories, Youth Days and Family Days, etc. And that is why we later brought out so many pastoral Directories. Teaching our own how to do pastoral ministry. We follow the perennial magisterium to the letter, but in pastoral matters we are in the vanguard”84.

In fact, Fr. Buela had all the apostolates that are in some way like a tradition in our Institute very clearly in mind from the beginning. That is why leaving the premises of the diocesan Seminary in San Rafael and being able “to have our own Seminary gave us more independence,” Fr. Buela related, “thus making it easier to form our seminarians as we wished, for example, to have Greek, Hebrew and modern languages studied; to emphasize the pastoral dimension of formation; to set aside the manual-based study of philosophy; to use the very texts of St. Thomas; to incorporate computing as a working tool; to facilitate the organization of the Thomistic Conferences; to be able to have our own journal and publishing house; to be able to select the professors; etc.”85. It is important to keep this always in mind since, we insist, “pastoral undertakings are essential to us”86. This last phrase is remarkable, it has a great deal of substance, and Fr. Buela explicitly wanted it to be included precisely in the section dealing with the formation of the members; which means it calls for a certain deepening on our part.

Next, then, with the intention that our candidates may have the opportunity of a true familiarity with the history of their Institute, its spirit and its mission, so as to live faithfully the charism of the Founder, we list the proper and untried apostolates that Fr. Buela fostered and promoted in the seminary while he lived at the Finca. For, in truth, he encouraged the seminarians to undertake untried apostolates, and he himself had a great deal of creativity and inventiveness for this.

Here we shall simply mention some criteria set out by him in his books, which everyone in formation should learn and assimilate. It is not an exhaustive list, but general guidelines that in some way define the uniqueness of our way of doing apostolate, since the Directories specify in detail everything that is expected of an IVE missionary and, therefore, everything that his formation requires, as well as everything regarding the objectives and the specific proper manner of carrying out each apostolate. Certainly, as regards formation, this is what those of us who lived with him in those times saw, lived and experienced.

  • Permanent mission

The first apostolate that arose was the ‘permanent mission,’ which was carried out by visiting house to house on Saturdays and some Sundays. “With the permanent popular mission, too, we sought to form the seminarians, who thus got a feel for the reality of the society in which they lived. It was done this way because we had to form shepherds, not theorists. And shepherds are formed by having them shepherd. You will find practically no congregation where the members know how to teach catechism, know how to do popular missions, preach Spiritual Exercises, know how to do pastoral ministry, have oratories, etc. Because they do not have them practice pastoral ministry”87.

  • Popular missions

Ours being an essentially missionary Congregation, popular missions according to the method of St. Alphonsus Mary de Liguori arose connaturally at the very beginning of the Institute.

Fr. Buela said that “popular missions… must seek the conversion of sinners”88. To learn well how popular missions are done, “we have a Directory of Popular Missions, which is quite complete. We held missions in Paso Grande (San Luis), 2 or 3 in Cristo de la Quebrada , 4 popular missions in Villa Progreso, 2 youth missions in the secondary school of Villa Progreso, in

Malacatos (Ecuador), in San Rafael a permanent mission with house-to-house visits for more than 30 years, etc.”89.

“In the past, missions revolved around two most principal elements: sermons and confessions. Today another is added: visiting families”90. Therefore, it is of capital importance that candidates learn to make home visits already from the novitiate.

Using the expression of Archbishop Malcolm Ranjith, our Founder wrote that “mission is not one more activity for the parish; it is its very essence”91. And that is a criterion that must be instilled in those who will later dedicate themselves to the care of souls.

  • Catechism

“To catechize means to explain the mystery of Christ in all its dimensions, in such a way that the Word of God bears the fruits of new life. For this reason, besides the intellectual transmission of the faith, which must not be lacking, catechesis must transmit the joy and the demands of the way of Christ”92. An example of this was not only his lucid and catechetical ministry with all kinds of people and at all times, but also the Catecismo de los jóvenes that he wrote in his own hand and which −after the Congregation was founded− he had some seminarians help update for a new edition. Today that Catechism has been translated into several languages, also thanks to the collaboration of several members who were in formation at the time.

“We strongly recommend as an active method of teaching catechism the books of Canon Charles Quinet, published by José Vilamala, Barcelona, for children from 6 to 9 years old: Para mis pequeñuelos93; for the youngest, El catecismo por el dibujo94; the great work in 3 volumes: Carnet de preparación de un catequista95. And other catechetical works such as plates, workbooks, practical exercises, etc., by this brilliant author. None of these works aims to replace the diocesan, national or universal catechisms; rather, they presuppose them and only aspire to complement them”96.

  • Intellectual ministry

Fr. Buela always encouraged the members to read, to study, to write, to do research, to hold sound disputationes and all the activities that the intellectual apostolate entails. “The redemption that must reach all authentically human reality must reach specifically the world of man’s thought; that is why the ministry of the intellect must be the first ministry, always remembering that:

– ‘nothing human is foreign to me’ (Terence)97;

– ‘what is not assumed is not redeemed’98, and we cannot help in the redemption of man if we do not know what constitutes and distinguishes man, which is his thinking. Hence the supreme importance of truly knowing modern thought, in order to know how to ‘vaccinate’99 modern man against modern culture, which is atheistic, immanentist, secularist, consumerist, a culture of death, liquid, etc.”100.

An example of this was the Thomistic Conferences that he promoted so fervently in the Institute. It was common to see Fr. Buela on Saturdays preparing some Thomistic paper −generally on Deo Uno− to present at the Conferences.

Another example we can cite was the examinations de Universa philosophia and de Universa theologia, which he expressly wanted to be public101. During the examinations the Father would come by and sometimes ask the hardest questions. Truly, with great generosity, he was present in all aspects concerning the formation of the seminarians.

At the same time, it was Fr. Buela who introduced us to essential Thomism by way of Fr. Cornelio Fabro, and he tirelessly contrasted living Thomism with formalist and fossilized Thomism. Thus, in the midst of his many occupations, with great self-giving on his part, he gave us an example by being a professor in the Seminary with all that this task entails.

  • Preaching authentically Ignatian Spiritual Exercises

That is, “never forgetting that their essence lies, above all, in conversion and right election”102.

“‘One of the essential elements of the Ignatian Exercises is adaptation’103, rightly understood”104. Fr. Buela explains, then, that “adapting the Exercises is essential to an authentic and deep sense of fidelity to the Ignatian text, since different variables are involved: the exercitant, the enemy, grace and the director. Adaptation presupposes leaving intact the principal end, the means and the modes”105.

The preaching of the Spiritual Exercises according to the method of St. Ignatius is, without any doubt, one of the apostolates with the greatest influence on the evangelization of culture. And he always encouraged that Spiritual Exercises be preached in all the jurisdictions of the IVE and that for this the priests prepare themselves well and the best means be used.

Who will not remember that he always brought seminarians and deacons to help him preach the Exercises, and at the same time was thus forming them and helping each one personally to become excellent preachers according to their abilities?

How can we forget, at this point, that time when he announced the celebration of his hundredth Spiritual Exercises preached with a great asado on Sunday, to the great joy of the seminarians, accustomed as they were to eating pasta. In this way he conveyed the great importance of that apostolate and the relevance it has for the inculturation of the Gospel.

Fr. Buela himself left behind a magnificent example of this: “according to my notebook of Exercises preached, counting only those of 5, 8, 15 or 30 days, from 1974 through 2011, I preached 134 Spiritual Exercises to 3830 exercitants”106.

Another anecdote was the fact that he wanted to travel to the United States when the Institute acquired a property in the state of New York intended to be a house of Spiritual Exercises. Having a house of our own for that purpose was like a golden dream. He wanted it to be called “St. Patrick,” and he encouraged a great celebration for that new acquisition destined for so noble an end.

  • Ministry with men

“We gave importance to various topics of concrete pastoral formation, [among them] to ministry with men, giving ‘Forming the Family of God’ (an experience from Chicago for bringing groups of Mexicans and Puerto Ricans to the faith)”107. He began and promoted in the major Seminary the apostolate of the men’s groups at the Finca. And Fr. Buela himself taught the seminarians the method of how to lead those groups.

  • Youth ministry

“A youth ministry that is not enthusiastic will never be a seedbed of vocations”108.

The Youth Days in the IVE were born as an initiative of Fr. Buela, since in the jubilee year 2000 some could not travel to Rome to take part in person in those days. Then it occurred to Fr. Buela that we should hold a Youth Day in San Rafael, which in fact was done, with great sacrifice but with great fruit year after year. It was a great event at the level of the Religious Family; indeed, from the postulants (of both branches) to the priests and the monks, all were involved in what was at the time an opportunity for large-scale apostolate for our small Religious Family.

At those first Youth Days there was no lack of campfires, the procession with its Eucharistic dialogue (which the young people followed by reading from little booklets prepared for the occasion at the Seminary’s printing press), confessions, solemn Masses and a choir that shone, time for sports and, fundamentally, superb formation conferences for the young people. The expense was not questioned. Fr. Buela himself gave a plenary conference109 to the young people at that first Youth Day in San Rafael, and they took notes on the sheets that had been printed and arranged in a very simple but very pretty little folder prepared for the occasion. There was an expo on the congregation with photos of the missions, a stand for buying the books our members were publishing at the time, etc. Some seminarians, with the help of a priest, composed the hymn for the Youth Days. There was great participation by IVE priests, who were called upon to help with the confessions that took place during adoration, before Mass and, to tell the truth, at any hour the young people requested them.

In truth, all these things left an indelible impression on the souls of those who were then in the houses of formation −those in formation and formators− because it was the first time. And in this way he was stamping the missionary imprint on both the seminarian and the priest of the IVE. What is more, what began at that first Youth Day is what is replicated today in the vast majority of events of that kind that we have today in the various jurisdictions. To look no further, the IVEMeeting was something similar on a large scale.

  • Camps

“From the beginning of our seminary, the camp, the time of living together, was part of the seminarian’s formation. It was Bishop León Kruk who wanted it, with the argument that three months of vacation were too much for the seminarians. We had camps for 4 years in El Nihuil (Mendoza) and then many more in Bariloche, both in Colonia Suiza […] and on the shores of Lake Mascardi, Lake Guillelmo, etc. Where, in addition to what is required for a Catholic camp, there was the hour of Adoration and Benediction with the Most Blessed Sacrament, and a half-hour formation conference. They were ‘school of life’ camps for reaching the Mountain, which is Christ”110.

All those camps left indelible memories of the seminary years. “The mountain is very formative,” he used to say. And he rejoiced that the seminarians could reach some peak and have the marvelous experience of a Mass celebrated on the mountain. When he was 17 years old, the experience of Mass on Cerro López (in Bariloche) marked him deeply; in fact, for one convivium he had the original idea of presenting a piece of writing recalling that experience 30 years later.

Again, at the camps Fr. Buela displayed his magnificence, with all the expenses involved in taking such a large number of seminarians to Bariloche, taking them to climb Cerro Tronador, getting boats, fishing rods, etc. All that was a luxury at that time, and even more so for the number of people such an event involved.

  • Family Days

“Every year Family Days are held in various parts of the world, and they are valued for the many fruits they leave. Topics proper to the family and marriage are treated and, at the end of the presentations, the floor is given to anyone who wishes, so that they may ask their questions or make their contributions or their criticisms”111.

  • The siblings’ celebration

“The Sunday evening dinner [of the Family Day] was the ‘Siblings’ Celebration,’ that is, of those religious who have blood brothers or sisters in the congregation. This was done for the first time in 1999 at the initiative of Fr. Buela, our Founder, at the Sunday dinner of the 3rd Family Day. And truly there is reason to celebrate: there are more than 236 [blood] siblings among the members of our religious family, distributed among more than 96 families of two, three and even six siblings in religious life. During tonight’s campfire, several groups of siblings took turns going up on stage to perform some musical or comic number”112.

  • Conferences on Sensus Ecclesiae

They arose somewhat in response to the accusations being made against the Institute at that time. At the end of the conferences, in which seminarians, priests and Servant sisters took part, one of the IVE priests asked the commissary at the time “what opinion he had of how those conferences had gone. And he answered that we had attained 98% of sensus ecclesiae. He asked him what was missing to complete the remaining 2%, and he said that the ‘Latin American Magisterium’ was missing. So the following month, a series of conferences was given on Medellín, Puebla and Santo Domingo. The commissary said that now we had 100% of the sensus Ecclesiae”113. And it was a pontifical commissary who said it; several people forget this now…

  • Vocational ministry

“The new evangelization requires being able to count on an adequate number of priests: centuries of experience teach that a large part of the affirmative responses to vocation arise through spiritual direction; moreover, it requires the example of life of priests faithful to their own identity, interiorly and exteriorly”114.

In truth, for Fr. Buela vocational ministry was like an overflowing fire that he strove to make burn in all the members. That is why he said: “Although this vocational work belongs principally to the pastors of the Church, the whole community must feel this apostolate as a responsibility and a ‘problem’ of its own. […]

It may be useful to recall some fundamental principles regarding the nature of the vocation to consecrated life, both to ‘refute’ some misunderstandings on this very important subject and to indicate possible lines of action for an effective vocational ministry.

– The ordinary way in which God raises up vocations is interior, through the divine promptings of the Holy Spirit to the soul. It is characteristic of the divine call to impel men to higher things. That is why the desire for religious life, being so exalted and elevated, can never come from the devil or the flesh. […]

– It must be remembered that it is false to believe that there are no vocations; quite the contrary, there are many […]

– Then, always keep in mind that ‘life begets life.’ That is why there will be vocations where there are living communities, whether authentically Christian families or religious and parish communities that strive to live according to the proper identity God has given them.

– There must be no fear of proposing the vocation to young people.

– Finally, one must also know that without good formation God does not bless with an abundance of vocations. ‘Intense efforts must be made to foster vocations and to procure the best priestly formation possible in the seminaries’”115.

“In vocational ministry, it is fundamental to present the cross; it is the condition of every disciple of Jesus: If anyone wants to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross each day, and follow me (Lk 9:23)”116. And he added: “to the young people who want to enter one of our Congregations −in formation− I offer and promise only: ‘poverty and persecution,’ which is what I have always asked of God for us. And let no one think in a worldly way that asking for these things is something negative; it is the most positive thing, and perhaps the most beautiful that can be asked for −even if those who consider themselves our enemies gnash their teeth−, because it is asking to be able to live the eighth beatitude, which is the confirmation of the seven previous ones, and it is asking for what is most effective for converting our world, because it is bearing witness that ‘the world cannot be transformed and offered to God without the spirit of the beatitudes’117”118.

Another important criterion to bear in mind for vocational ministry: “As long as there is suitability, they may enter”119. That is why he firmly and with great realism condemned the widespread custom among some “of delaying entrance, precisely because they do not believe, or doubt, that it is God who is calling. […] When one expects vocations to mature in the world, the world generally swallows up the vocations. There are even some who say outright that a candidate has no vocation, and not because they have verified that there is no suitability, but for other subjective reasons of their own. Because it seems so to them, from a hunch, a gut feeling, from not liking the candidate, or because they think they have the gift of reading hearts. If they are young, because they are young; if they are older, because they are too old”120.

Such was the importance he gave to the subject of vocations for the fulfillment of our specific end as an Institute that, when writing the Directory of Vocations, he wanted the objections found in numbers 25 to 63 of that Directory to be made explicit.

  • Humanities High School

In general terms our Founder longed for a school that would be:

– Free of charge: “We are going to start a school. First problem: ‘will it be paid or free?’ I already had experience with this question. It has to be free […]. We did not want to do business, we did not want to make money […]. Of course, but who pays the teachers? Well, the State pays part: it does not pay for electricity, it does not pay for caretakers, it does not pay for maintenance … In the end one has to have a minimum; that is why what private schools do is save the State expenses”121.

– Catholic: “To have a school, yes, but to have a school that is Catholic, where the faith is taught. Where the young person who enters knows that the Catholic faith is taught there, and that he can come to know it, because there are many schools that call themselves Catholic but whose students never come to know the Catholic faith, nor live the Catholic faith, and they come out of there atheists […]. So, the clear idea, the fundamental idea: to make a Catholic school where the children come to know Christ and seek to imitate Christ”122.

– Humanist: “Humanist formation consists substantially in forming the whole man, in particular by teaching him to think; and for man to learn to think, the study of the classical languages is necessary -above all Latin and Greek […]. The Humanities High School is dedicated specifically to human formation; it is not like a technical school that teaches how to use a machine or teaches a technique, but it teaches man to be a man”123.

– “We asked ourselves: coeducational or not? From my previous experience, although in primary school it can be positive, it usually is not so in secondary school”124.

– No graduation trip at the end of secondary school: “From the beginning we said that in our

schools there would be no so-called graduation trip, which is usually a ‘trip of sins.’ Instead we would make a great act of love and gratitude to Jesus Christ for the formation received, by doing mission work in humble places such as the backwoods of Santiago del Estero”125.

Worthy of mention is that our Founder launched the Humanities High School with practically nothing. Some of the seminarians of that time went to teach classes, others were proctors, etc. In short, what could be done was done. That is why Fr. Buela points out that ours is perhaps “the first school built by the teachers themselves, because with what the seminarians were paid, the walls of the High School were paid for”126.

But that was a great experience, since the “Humanities High School also turned out to be an arena for the seminarians, where they could teach, and so what Aristotle says was fulfilled, that ‘only he knows who is able to teach.’ The Humanities High School was also a very great grace from God for the Major Seminary as well”127. That remains a very desirable experience for all the present and future seminarians of the Institute.

  • Printing press and publications

From the beginning Fr. Buela wanted the seminarians to be proficient in everything having to do with printing and the spreading of the good press. He tirelessly repeated that in the field of the intellectual apostolate one had to give priority to “publications, since what is written remains and spreads more widely”128. Thus arose the journal Diálogo, the Vox Verbi Bulletin, the magazine Ave María, etc.

The journal Diálogo, published since 1992, was born and developed as an apostolate of the major Seminary under the direct supervision and direction of Fr. Buela. He himself took care of naming the journal, and he had a very clear idea of its objectives, which he naturally conveyed to the seminarians who worked at the printing press, but in fact to everyone, since those who did not work at the press suddenly found themselves distributing the journal.

Fr. Buela’s attention to detail in everything concerning the journal showed the importance he gave to such an apostolate. There is the anecdote of when a seminarian showed him a draft of the cover design that included some dashes or flourishes on both sides of the journal’s title. Fr. Buela immediately said that those flourishes should be removed. Moreover, he tirelessly wrote articles for the journal129.

Once again, this apostolate involved significant expenses for the Seminary. And yet the best means and the best human capital were devoted to that undertaking. To the point that Cardinal Quarracino, after receiving a copy of the journal, wrote a letter to the Founder in which, among other things, he says: “It seems that with your journal you have overcome two difficulties: the laziness about writing proper to Latin Americans and, of course, to Argentines; and the financial difficulty, because I can imagine the outlay that each issue of ‘Diálogo’ means. Here is a modest contribution of mine”130.

But this apostolate was not to remain confined to the houses of formation; rather, “even as part of the good press,” Fr. Buela said, “if circumstances permit, one can try to set up a parish printing press. Don Bosco used to repeat: ‘First a printing press, then a great printing press, and then many printing presses…’”131.

  • Oratory

Our Proper Law establishes that “every Superior should see to it that a festive Oratory is organized in his parish, school, house and other works of his jurisdiction, considering that this work is one of the most important of all those entrusted to him”132. That is why Fr. Buela added: “In our Directory of Oratories what it is about is well treated. It must be read, explained and implemented”133.

“The ‘oratorian spirit’ is the Oratory in the broad sense; it is the spirit that must be present in our families, in our communities, in the major and minor seminaries, the schools, retreats, camps, study trips or pilgrimages, outings, conferences, congresses, poetry contests, etc. All our activities and pastoral works must be imbued with this oratorian spirit”134.

If there is one thing that characterized Fr. Buela, it was his festive spirit or, as his own brother described him: “he was a homo festivus.”

  • Preparation of sermons

Certainly the seminarians who are preparing for the care of souls must know that they are to beget spiritual children through the word135. Therefore, it is imperative −and Fr. Buela gave great importance to the subject− to prepare sermons well.

“Parishioners,” he said, “love it when the pastor prepares his sermons; they listen to them gladly and with great spiritual profit. And they are supremely bored when the priest habitually does not prepare his sermons”136.

For this reason, he asked the priests of the Institute “not to be boring. We are tired of humdrum clerics who bore people with their singsong of failed functionaries, of mere administrators, of ecclesiastical bureaucrats”137. Instead, he exhorted them “to know how to be poets, that is, to know how to bring to light the non-trivial dimension of existence. […] Let them be, therefore, creative, that is, not mere repeaters of known things, of things even badly said, of things that have no force because they are repeated wearily. […] Let them be original, not in order to repeat foolish novelties, which in the end are

nothing more than ancient heresies, but let them know how to lead to the authentic origins [that is, to God]”138.

“The preparation, let us say proximate, should begin on Sunday afternoon, reading the readings of the following Sunday and bearing in mind the particular topic chosen. (It may have happened, or may yet happen, that some notable event occurs that makes it preferable to choose another topic). In the remaining days the sermon is gradually written”139.

In this regard Fr. Buela said that it was “important to read the beginning of St. Alphonsus’s work Sermones abreviados para todas las Dominicas del año, arranged so as to serve in missions and spiritual exercises”140, although in his view “the great work on this subject is that of Fray Luis de Granada, Retórica Eclesiástica”141.

He was a great promoter of Courses in rhetoric among our members; he had Fray Luis de Granada’s book digitized and ordered that our own edition of it be published. At the same time, he himself began the classes in oratory and its exercises.

To evaluate whether or not the sermon had been understood, he had the following irrefutable criterion: “If one learns that the parishioners were talking about the sermon at lunch, it is a sign that they understood it”142.

  • Ecumenical Conference

“Pastoral zeal and charity must also extend to the members of non-Catholic Christian Churches and communities. For this purpose, an ecumenical formation is necessary…”143. Fr. Buela himself, with the help of other IVE priests, organized at the major Seminary “Mary, Mother of the Incarnate Word” the 1st Ecumenical Conference in San Rafael.

  • Care of the poor

We would say that the care of the poor arose almost simultaneously with the beginning of the Institute. It was a work very dear to our Founder, and he stressed the “importance of concrete charity when speaking of the subject of the poor”144 and of the Hogarcitos in the pastoral formation of the seminarians. In fact, food began to be given to the poor daily as soon as the seminary at La Finca began to operate.

Using the text of Mt 25:34-45, where we read ‘Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty, or a stranger or naked, or sick or in prison, and did not help you? He [Christ] will answer them: ‘Truly I tell you: what you did not do for one of these, the least, you did not do for me either’; Fr. Buela taught that that is why “in our Homes, at the foot of the cross, a sign is placed that says: ‘This is how to love’ and also: ‘Look, it is I!’, for it is Jesus Christ who speaks, referring to our guests. That is what we aim to do with the Hogarcitos for persons with special needs or with any need, orphans, elderly people without help, parents of priests or of women religious, etc.”145.

“If the Princes of the Apostles, Peter and Paul, were Christ’s poor, how could we refuse to help the one who comes to us with some need? And to help them, as far as possible, as if they were Christ Himself”146. That is the way a member of the IVE cares for the poor, the sick, the elderly, orphans, etc.: as if they were Christ. And he said it with “the conviction that only charity, as St. Luigi Orione said, will save the world”147.

Fr. Buela strongly emphasized that charity must be concrete. Because “holiness consists in love lived concretely. And our neighbor needs bread, a home, clothing, a word, our time so as to confide to us his problems and his torments. […] Love of neighbor, too, needs the gift of the heart. Is it really necessary to love God with all one’s heart and one’s neighbor only with one’s head?” 148.

In this sense, he suggested “that in every parish there should be a Hogarcito, because ministry with the poor obliges us to concrete charity, against which there is no arguing, and it is an irrefutable form of charity. It is always a ministry loved by the people”149. And he insisted, “I know from experience that when we care for God’s poor, He always helps us generously. […] Having the poor with us, no Parish would be dying. In some cases, as in countries with a Muslim majority, it is almost the only visible and possible apostolate”150.

As we mentioned earlier, it was Fr. Buela who promoted the festive dinner for the poor who habitually came to our houses and for others who wished to join for the occasion. It was prepared like a party, with food somewhat better than what was usually served. The seminarians themselves cooked and waited on the tables; some were assigned to sit among the guests in order to do apostolate, others prepared a campfire, etc. And so, concretely, the seminarians learned to practice charity with the poor: by serving.

  • Apostolate of presence

Concretely, so as not to shy away from the missionary adventure, our Founder stressed that “there must be young priests ready −for example− to be custodians of the holy places. There must be young priests who have that disposition, which is a disposition for martyrdom”151. For they will not know churches full of parishioners and, very probably, will not be able “to know popular missions with all their fruits”152 if they are on mission in a place where the Christian population is a minority, and in a time of persecution because Christians are regarded as second-class; and even more so in a highly conflictive situation due to Muslim fundamentalism153. Likewise, he would announce to all the priests and seminarians, as if it were a grand event, whenever he received a request for a foundation in some emblematic place: Chad, Sudan, China, Papua New Guinea, Tajikistan, etc. And so he kept feeding the thirst for souls, the missionary drive that must stand out in every member of the IVE, and he prepared them for a magnanimous self-giving in places that were in fact very difficult. He taught the seminarians to be ready to go anywhere, and that is how several offered themselves for China, Equatorial Guinea, Australia, Rwanda, the Czech Republic, Russia, etc.

It is told that when the letter arrived from the bishop requesting a foundation in Chad, and after Fr. Buela had announced it to the whole Seminary body, he placed the bishop’s letter in the hands of Our Lady of Sorrows in the Seminary church. The next day, when the seminarians came in for Mass, they found the Virgin holding the letter.

However, in some cases −because of how challenging or dangerous the situation was−, Fr. Buela advised “the apostolate that has been called ‘of presence.’ [Because] an authentically Christian presence means the witness of charity, which is a most effective way of preaching the Gospel”154. Those who are preparing for mission in our houses of formation must be convinced of this.

  • A markedly Marian imprint

From the moment our candidates set foot in the novitiate −as the sound traditions of our Institute attest, which are replicated in the houses of the IVE whatever the language, the culture, the country, the years of religious life, etc.− all learn to love Our Lady with filial and trusting devotion: whether with the kiss on Our Lady’s mantle, the song to Our Lady at the end of recreation, the renewal of the consecration to Most Holy Mary before the final blessing at Mass, the daily praying of the rosary, etc.

And this same filial love and true devotion to the Mother of God is manifested whatever the form of apostolate to which the members are devoted: with the dawn rosary in the popular missions, in the Marian processions with the corresponding decoration of the float that carries the image, the invocation of Mary in the peroratio of a sermon, teaching children to pray the Hail Mary and to bring flowers to Our Lady in the Oratory, the promotion of consecration to Mary according to the method of St. Louis Mary Grignion de Montfort, the celebration of Marian feasts with all solemnity, pilgrimages to the shrines of Our Lady, and so many other apostolic practices inherited from our Founder which today are connatural to our pastoral style and which should not be lost; rather, they should always be practiced with new fervor. It is the testimony of those who were seminarians and formators at the time when the Founder lived at the Finca that “if there was one thing that was omnipresent in the Seminary, it was the maternal care of Our Lady and Fr. Buela with his great love for her. That was constantly perceived, and so we learned it, connaturally.”

In this regard Fr. Buela reminded us “that in the Constitutions it says that ‘for the suitable formation of the seminarians [and women religious] they must be taught to value and to love [ … ] the Most Blessed Virgin and the saints as intercessors and models of life [ … ].’ Let us bear in mind that progressivism even today continues to vilify, in one way or another, the cult of the saints. Among us that must never happen. We must love and honor the saints, and invoke their protection, especially that of those we have as protectors or patrons. They are our brothers in the faith, who have already reached the goal and who, therefore, can help us; that is why we must relate to them”155. And if that is said of the saints, how much more of the Queen of all saints.

  • Apostolate with women religious and the laity

It is certainly a grace and an enormous advantage to be able to count on the support of women religious and of the laity −men and women− for carrying out our apostolates. For the consecrated woman enters places where we [priests] cannot enter. […] The feminine presence does great things in the pastoral ministry of the Church, and in fact a very great pastoral complementarity can come about”156. Our Proper Law says the same about the laity, since they can “take and carry Christ to the most distant, most hidden places, where priests cannot reach or enter, evangelize cultures from their deepest, most intimate core, bringing reality under the lordship of the Lord, inculturating the Gospel in the most remote corners of human life”157. It is important, therefore, that the seminarians learn to work with them.

The seminarians must be taught to “exercise great prudence in dealing with women”158. “‘But, Father −you will tell me−, we women are not bad…’ Certainly. And certainly we −so we have been taught− must see a woman as we see our mother, as we see our sister, as we see the Most Blessed Virgin. Yes, sir, but one must be prudent, cutting off familiarities in good time. But one must also know that there are women who are not good, who are bad women and who seek out the priest in order to ruin him. A priest ran into a former seminary classmate who had abandoned the priesthood, in a bar in the Flores area, and the man said to him: ‘Look, all I can tell you is: go and tell the cardinal that there are women here, paid by Freemasonry, who go looking for priests, even in the confessional.’ Even so, one must know that sometimes the more dangerous ones are those who pretend to be ‘good’”159. “Between a holy woman and a holy man, a wall of stone and mortar,” Fr. Buela used to point out to his own in a fatherly way.

  • Care of family members

“We have the particularity that, instead of setting aside our natural family, we draw it into our Congregation. Great friendships even form among them. In short, they help us in every way”160.

“Hence the importance of including families in the Institute of the Incarnate Word: for us it is a great joy when the fathers and mothers of our men and women religious visit us, because it is as if they were our own relatives. We have founded a home for the care of the relatives of priests, seminarians, sisters… who need it”161.

“It is what the fourth commandment of the law of God teaches: to honor, respect, obey −while one lives in one’s parents’ home… it ceases with the emancipation of the children− love and serve one’s parents in their needs, when the children are grown. And analogously, one’s brothers and sisters, grandparents, pastors, catechists, teachers, friends…”162.

It goes without saying how much respect, esteem and consideration he had for his own relatives and for the relatives of our members. And he instilled that same attitude in the other members of the Institute. Thus, for example, once the parents of a seminarian traveled from another province to visit him at the seminary, in such a way that one of the days of the visit coincided with the seminarian’s apostolate day. He went to consult Fr. Buela: ‘Do I go to the apostolate and leave my parents here? Or do I look after my parents and miss the apostolate?’ To which the Father answered with complete simplicity: “these days your apostolate is your family.”

  • Internet

For Fr. Buela the internet or, as he himself says, “the new world of cyberspace”163 represented a “call to the great adventure of using its potential to proclaim the Gospel message”164. He realized the “magnificent opportunities for evangelization” that the use of the internet entailed if “it is used competently and with a clear awareness of its strengths and weaknesses”165. Fr. Buela was very aware that at present it was necessary to work in the areopagus of the media of communication with due precautions. We already mentioned earlier how from the beginning he wanted computing to be used as an instrument of apostolate.

Our Founder was the great originator and promoter of the Spiritual Exercises website. Moreover, we were perhaps among the first religious in San Rafael to have a system of communication with the whole world from El Nihuil, with the “BBS” system that was brought from the USA on the occasion of the ’94 chapter.

  • Help to diocesan priests

A special note of charity, one that speaks of the nobility of his soul, was his great interest in helping priests, even diocesan ones. For he considered it “of great importance that all priests, diocesan or religious, help one another”166.

In this sense he recommended: “Do not forget… hospitality; cultivate beneficence and the sharing of goods; be especially solicitous for the sick, the afflicted, those overburdened with work, those who are alone,

exiled from their homeland, victims of persecution; [feel yourselves] especially obliged toward those who suffer difficulties; to those who have failed in some respect, show fraternal charity and magnanimity, praying for them to God unceasingly, and show yourselves continually to be true brothers and friends to them”167.

How many times have we witnessed our Founder’s great example of priestly fraternal charity toward diocesan priests or priests belonging to other orders.

An example of that concrete charity is the case −almost emblematic, we might say− of the elderly Fr. Victorino Ortego, whom Fr. Buela personally invited to live in the major Seminary, and in fact he lived 15 years among our members. But many other diocesan priests of San Rafael also benefited from his generous fraternal charity, for example, when −given the poverty there was in Argentina at that time− Fr. Buela offered them some Mass intentions. That is, he asked them to pray for the Congregation and gave them a stipend for it. Indeed, there was a chart of the diocesan clergy of San Rafael on which were counted the Mass intentions given to each one, in order to keep track that they did not lack what was necessary to live.

Another example of his solicitude for the good of priests is shown by the fact that when Fr. Buela learned that in Ukraine there were many married clergy close to the Institute (some of them even members of the Third Order), he took care that they would not lack what was necessary, given that they also had to support their families. But above all he encouraged them to go to mission places, especially eastern Ukraine, near Russia. Some went, and Fr. Buela supported them with Mass intentions and other monthly aid.

  • Prefects

Fr. Buela wanted the major seminarians, “preferably from the Theology years”168 and, “if possible, [with] one of them a deacon”169, to have the experience of serving as prefects during formation.

Although the prefects are not formators, Fr. Buela wanted some to have the experience of working side by side, so to speak, with the Superior of the house of formation, not only to organize some activities, to watch over the discipline of the minor seminarians or the novices, and even −in some cases− “to act as an older brother”170, but also so that they might learn criteria, the way the formation of young men is carried out in the Institute, so that they might better understand the difficult task of governing, etc.

*****

By way of conclusion it seems to us more than fitting to quote here the words of the Founder about the formation he longs for those who are preparing to be priests and missionaries in our Institute.

“Well then, we have the aspiration −I do not know whether we will achieve it, but we have the desire− to form young priests who throw down the gauntlet to the Antichrist, that is, who are not only good but who, in addition, pick a fight with evil in the name of Jesus Christ.

How can that be achieved? I think it must be with three principal things.

The first thing necessary to form priests who throw down the gauntlet to the Antichrist is: Striving for them to be ‘self-motivated.’ It is absolutely necessary to form authentically free priests, not ones who use freedom as an excuse to fall into licentiousness, but who live according to the ‘freedom of the children of God’ (cf. Rom 8:21). Not so that they may be foolish rebels as the world wants them, but so that they may be rebels against the spirit of the world in order to be faithful to God […]. For this it is necessary to teach future priests to think. Nothing is achieved with knowledge held together by pins. Nothing is achieved with a kind of philosophical blah-blah learned by heart as if it were a catechism of first notions. We must form young men who make the effort and have the courage to think, even at the risk of being mistaken, because it is less serious to be mistaken than to be deprived of what distinguishes and constitutes man as such, which is his thinking. […]

The second thing necessary to form priests who throw down the gauntlet to the Antichrist is: Forming them so that they are ready to lay down their lives for the sheep. They will be shepherds and they must be so in the image of the Good Shepherd. The Good Shepherd knows his sheep, calls them by name, goes ahead of them; The Good Shepherd lays down his life for the sheep (Jn 10:11). He is not like the hireling shepherd who sees the wolf coming and abandons the sheep, and who lives off their wool, their milk, their meat. He uses the sheep for his own profit, without caring for their good.

Today we are fed up with so much nominalist pastoral ministry, desk-bound ministry. Fruitless, sterile, and with the sterility of the incapable. With schools, teacher-training colleges, novitiates, seminaries and universities that are Catholic only in name, in the little sign at the entrance. Because there is no one who lays down his life for the sheep. Never as now have so many meetings of shepherds been seen, such that if Christ came now He would not find us united, but He would certainly find us meeting. Unfortunately, as experience shows: ‘when the shepherds meet, the sheep dies,’ as the Spanish proverb wisely says.

It is difficult to form shepherds ready to lay down their lives for the sheep, but the promise of Jesus Christ and the power of the Holy Spirit are at stake.

The third thing necessary to form priests who throw down the gauntlet to the Antichrist is: That they be men of deep prayer.

Today, more than before, it is absolutely necessary that the priest be a man of true prayer. Only personal contact with the One and Triune God can save him from the trivialization of his priestly life and even from the loss of his priestly identity. The priest who prays is saved; the priest who does not pray is damned.

In a particular way he must be the man of Eucharistic prayer. At Mass, acting in persona Christi, he transubstantiates the bread and wine into the Body and Blood of the Lord, thus perpetuating the sacrifice of the cross on our altars. The Mass is the heart of the Catholic priesthood. No other action, not even the most exalted, can be compared to it. It is the most important thing of the day, so much so that the true priest divides the day in two: before Mass, preparing for it, and after Mass, giving thanks for it”171.

“If the seminaries still fail to form priests of great human, spiritual, intellectual and pastoral stature, if we settle for mediocrities that are even very far from the aurea mediocritas, if every means is used to have young men without lucidity and without courage, if we do not succeed in forming young priests who are poets, contemplatives and warriors, who have a metaphysical intelligence and know how to grasp beauty so as to be capable of dialoguing with and refuting modern culture in what it has that is anti-Christian, when even ‘the traditionalist sector of the clergy,’ which ‘has remained sound in its cultural formation but without the vigor to take a position in the face of modern culture,’ has been sterile in evangelizing the culture”172.

May Most Holy Mary, always available for the service of her Divine Son and of men for God’s sake, obtain for us the grace of giving the Church shepherds after the Heart of Christ, that is, tireless evangelizers.

A.M.D.G.

SAINT JOSÉ GABRIEL BROCHERO

AND THE SPIRITUAL EXERCISES

September 30th, 2016. Dear Fathers, Brothers, and Seminarians:

This coming 16th of October, the Holy Father will raise the Argentine priest José Gabriel del Rosario Brochero (1840-1914) to the honors of the altar. Brochero was a distinguished preacher of the Spiritual Exercises of Saint Ignatius of Loyola, and Saint John Paul II called him “the Curé of Ars of Argentina.”1

In addition to filling us with happiness, his canonization is wrapped with a particular importance for us, since, as an Institute, we want to “accomplish [the] evangelization of the culture by sanctifying people individually. This will be done preferably by using the Spiritual Exercises according to the method and spirit of Saint Ignatius of Loyola.”2

For this reason, it seemed opportune for me to remind all the members of the religious family of the importance that the apostolate of the Spiritual Exercises has as an efficacious means for the preaching of the Word of God and their “irreplaceable value . . . for the renewal of Christian life.”3

1. The Example of Fr. Brochero4

José Gabriel del Rosario Brochero was ordained a priest on November 4th, 1866, at the age of 26. In December of 1869, at the young age of 29, he was named pastor of the vast parish of San Alberto, in the west of the province of Córdoba (Argentina), with a territory of 4,336 square kilometers of steep mountains and inhospitable paths. The population of little over 10,000 inhabitants lived in remote areas, without roads or schools, entirely in isolation because of the Sierras Grandes, which tower over 2,000 meters high.

There, in the midst of the moral and material poverty of his parish, suffering from the unwarranted criticisms and misunderstandings of other priests and the indifference of authorities, the merciful heart5 of this heroic priest urged the preaching of the Spiritual Exercises for his rough and simple people. His faith and conviction in the miraculous fruits of holiness6 from the Spiritual Exercises—which he had known as a seminarian—were so strong that he did not hesitate to throw himself completely into this apostolic endeavor. Hence, he would say: “The priest who has scarce pity for sinners is only half a priest. These vestments I wear are not what make me a priest; if I don’t have charity in my heart, I am not even a Christian.” Far from being scared at the difficulties or making excuses because of the lack of support, he wrote in one of his letters: “I trust in God and in the Virgin Most Pure,” and in this way, this man of faith bore witness that being a pastor and being a missionary are one same reality.

His biographers recount that he would put together caravans that would surpass over 500 people, in order to undertake a journey of more than 200 kilometers (which required three days journey on mule) in order to withdraw from the world for nine days where he would teach them to conquer themselves and to order their lives according to God. At the end of the Exercises, he would bid them farewell, telling them: “Well, now go on and be on guard against offending God by returning to your old ways. The priest has already done his part so that you might be saved if you want. But, if anyone insists on being condemned, may a thousand devils carry him off.”7

Years later, Fr. Brochero himself, together with parishioners, built his own House for the Exercises, and God—who does not let Himself be outdone in generosity—hurried to reward him with abundant blessings. The story is told that his happiness was so great when the foundations were laid for the House of the Exer-cises that he himself wanted to lay the first stone and, foreseeing hell’s opposition against the building from which he awaited so many fruits, he threw it with exu-berance, as if he was crushing the head of a serpent with it, and exclaimed: “Devil, you’re done for!”

The house was opened in 1877 with groups of over 700 people. The following year, the exercitants grew to 4,000, and ultimately more than 40,000 people made the Exercises in the House during the parochial ministry of Fr. Brochero. Moreover, since the House’s opening until the present no fewer than 100,000 people have “taken” the Exercises in that house,8 spiritually renewing themselves (Fr. Brochero called the Spiritual Exercises “bathes for the soul”).

It’s worth mentioning that as soon as the construction of the House for the Exercises was finished, Fr. Brochero brought a religious community—the Hand-maids of the Sacred Heart of Jesus—to look after it.

Eager to fulfill an old dream, he also built a school for girls (cared for by the same sisters) and a residence for priests. In his inexhaustible missionary zeal, and in an effort to bring his beloved parishioners out of the poverty in which they were found, he built more than 200 kilometers of roads, several churches, founded towns, and planned a railway branch line that would unite Valle de Traslasierra with Villa Dolores y Soto. “Abandoned by everyone but not by God,” he used to say.

Setting aside the misunderstandings of his peers because he preached the Ex-ercises to the men and women of the mountains, who were simple, suspicious, and often illiterate, he recommended to the priests that sometimes came to help him: “The more sinful, or more rough, or more rude my parishioners, the more sweetness and friendliness they must be treated with in the confessional, from the pulpit, and in daily contact.”

Convinced that the “parish must produce faithful people who are able to make the seed of the Gospel blossom in the place where they are,” “Cura Brochero preached to, confessed, directed, and helped the participants, dedicating himself entirely to them.”9 He would often say: “I would be happy if God would take me from this earth seated hearing confessions and preaching the Gospel.”

Authentically Ignatian in his preaching, the holy priest invited all to serve under Christ’s standard. He would tell the exercitants: “Jesus invites to follow Him and to place yourself under His standard with a most pleasant manner and with the sweetest words. Our health and our life, the strength of our heart, the joy of our spirit, and the hope of heaven is in the Cross.”

At last, when he was diagnosed with the terrible sickness of leprosy, which he contracted through his care of and companionship with, even to the point of shar-ing his mate, a man ill with the same illness, Brochero could see how many of those in whom he had trusted had abandoned him, frightened by his awful infirmity, and how he was left with his sister Aurora for his only companion.

On February 2nd, 1908, at the age of 68, almost blind and deaf, Brochero gave up his parish, impossible as it was for him to care for it. With admirable resignation, he embraced the heavy cross with which God wanted to test his arduous old age, and his last years were an eloquent lesson of tried virtue. Both his leprosy and agonizing solitude revealed in an unexpected way the fruitfulness of his surrender as a priest.

While he was already sick, he was heard to say: “I am very content with what God has done with me with regard to my sight, and I give Him great thanks on account of it. When I could serve humanity, my senses remained healthy and robust. Today, now that I cannot, one of the senses has been rendered useless. It is a great favor that God our Lord has done me in freeing me entirely from the active life and leaving me the task for seeking my end and of praying for men of the past, present, and those who will come until the end of the world.”

Our Founder calls our attention to the following point: “His life of faith was fed by the Spiritual Exercises of Saint Ignatius, daily Mass, even during his long trips and on his sick bed, his rosary, and the breviary that he carried at his waist and that he prayed daily. ‘He lived by faith,’ noted one witness. From there arose his great pastoral charity and his death on the cross. In it he found his support and strength in his long illness and, thanks to it, before he died he could say, ‘I trust in God’s mercy.’”10

He died, a blind leper, on January 26th, 1914, at the age of 74.

2. Spiritual Exercises in Order to Evangelize

“The preaching of the Ignatian Spiritual Exercises in our parishes is a characteristic proper to the Institute. The way in which said Exercises strengthen the life of the parish by means of the strengthening of the spiritual life of the faithful has been proven.”11 For this reason, “we must enthusiastically return to this practice, which has given so many fruits of holiness,”12 and which constitutes one of the preferential apostolates of our Institute.13

In this sense, Blessed Paul VI wrote: “We know that the most effective preaching is precisely that of the Spiritual Exercises. . . . We must spread this foundation of salvation and of spiritual energy; we must make it accessible to all classes of people.”14 Our spiritual father, Saint John Paul II, exhorts us: “I hope that… priests, religious, and lay people continue being faithful to this experience [of the Spiritual Exercises] and that they give them increase.”15

For this reason, dear brothers in the Incarnate Word, by means of this circular letter I want to exhort you all to renew your fervor for the preaching of the Spiritual Exercises, that each one should take this as your own responsibility, each one col-laborating with what corresponds to him.16 I implore you not only to preach the holy Exercises, but also to do everything to organize them, even at the cost of great sacrifices, making use of the help of the lay members of our Third Order and of the Sisters Servants of the Lord, who can be of great help. God will not cease to bless the efforts that are made so that He can reign in souls! We, too, like Saint Fr. Brochero, have offered ourselves totally to “our Lord, the Eternal King,” whose “will [it is] to conquer the whole world.”17 Hence, we must not spare any sacrifice in carrying out this magnificent apostolate. This “should bring us to know the Ex-ercises deeply, to prepare ourselves to preach them fruitfully,18 and to have the necessary dispositions so as not to miss any opportunity to preach them.”19

It turns out to be of great importance to know how to adapt the Exercises to all classes of exercitants and to promote them “so that all parts of the world take advantage of the benefits” of the Exercises, making use of “new methods for evangelization, as the use of the Internet can be, also for the preaching of the Spiritual Exercises”20 according to the language of the place, and also seeking—in the measure that it is possible—to have an adequate and proper place dedicated to the preaching of the Exercises, even though many times this entails great sacrifices and, in short, it might be a cross.

It is from this same cross that innumerable blessings for our souls, for our Institute, and for the universal Church are born, as the great work of Saint Cura Brochero shows. Among the most valuable and outstanding fruits we can include: the efficacious influence that they have on the holiness of people, the numerous conversions, the great number of vocations to the path of perfection that are decided during the Exercises, the grace of perseverance, and more. All of these are fruits that promote an authentic and profound renewal of the Christian life, in this way collaborating in the establishment of Christ’s reign.

Therefore, may our desire always be to know how to take advantage of this precious instrument in order to carry out the new evangelization, which is “a ‘fascinating adventure,’ one that is ‘arduous and exciting, that summons all of us together.”21

In this context, I cannot help but mention another great grace for the nation of Argentina and for the universal Church that has recently taken place. I refer to the beatification of María Antonia de la Paz y Figueroa, Mama Antula, that heroic woman who was a great apostle of the Spiritual Exercises of Saint Ignatius, above all after the expulsion of the Jesuits from the Americas.22 She also founded the House of Exercises in Buenos Aires (which still operates), fighting against innumerable misunderstandings and difficulties as a result of her sex, the era, and from certain ecclesial spheres. The strength and apostolic drive of this apparently frail female figure of holiness should move us to put into place the means and to know how to discomfort ourselves so that the greatest possible number of souls might benefit from that spiritual bath which are the Exercises.23

For this reason, I raise with all fervor my prayer to our Lord that, on the occasion of the canonization of Saint Cura Brochero, the ardor might be renewed and the efforts and initiatives intensified that are directed towards promoting, improving, and increasing the opportunities for preaching the Spiritual Exercises of Saint Ignatius as a most appropriate means for the evangelization of the culture, that is, as a means to transform the world from within through the influence of grace. This is because, as the saint said so well, the Exercises help to “encourage man here on the earth, but with his sight fixed on heaven.” In a special way, I urgently recommend that Provincial Superiors put into place the means so that the priests of the Institute might make their knowledge of the Exercises of Saint Ignatius more profound, and that the preaching of those Exercises to all classes of exercitants might be increased. To neglect this aspect of our apostolate or to not properly encourage it would be an offense to our specific purpose and to our mission in the Church, who awaits from us the exercise of the apostolates proper to our charism.

May the new saint José Gabriel del Rosario Brochero intercede for our beloved Institute and obtain for us the grace of a total, permanent, and happy surrender in the exercise of our priesthood, and the immense grace of preaching authentically Ignatian Exercises everywhere, and this for the glory of God alone and the good of many souls.

Wishing you a beautiful feast for the canonization of Blessed José Gabriel del Rosario Brochero, I greet you in Christ, the Incarnate Word, and His Most Holy Mother.

We find ourselves on the shores of the Sea of Galilee. Jesus has chosen the Twelve; He has spent the night in prayer; He wanted to speak to His own, but a multitude appeared, so He went up a mountain and began to proclaim the beatitudes, thus beginning the Sermon or Discourse on the Mount.

The Christian spirit in the interpretation of the law

Jesus Christ is about to purify the law and give the true meaning to the messianic promises. From now on He will set things forth in harmony with the beatitudes He has declared. He is going to purify the law; He is going to declare the Christian spirit in the interpretation of the law: you have heard that it was said to you … but I say to you1. He has not come to abolish it but to bring it to its fullness.

But before this, what is it that we will hear in the Gospels of tomorrow and the day after? For the moment He pauses only to emphasize two things:

– first, the validity of the law, which is not abolished but purified

– and second, delicacy in fulfillment, in observance. And this second point is curious, and it is what I want to speak about, because Jesus highlights in minute detail how the observance of our duty must be. The Gospel speaks insistently of the obligation to be delicate in fulfilling the law, in such a way that not one iota nor one comma of the Law will pass away2, and He insists: Whoever does not keep the least of these commandments, and teaches others to do the same, will be considered the least in the Kingdom of Heaven3.

Having emphasized this, He will begin to set out the plan of utmost demand: You have heard that it was said: “You shall love your neighbor” and hate your enemy. But I say to you: Love your enemies, pray for those who persecute you4; You have heard that it was said: “You shall not commit adultery.” But I say to you: Whoever looks at a woman with desire has already committed adultery with her in his heart5. Our Lord is very strict in His statements.

That is why it is good for us to pause today to consider this aspect of the fulfillment of our obligations, which is thoroughness: not one dot, not one iota, down to the smallest thing; what some spiritual authors call the delicacies of observance. Jesus Christ made this clear before purifying the law.

There are many ways of doing what is asked of us, even while being dutiful; thus one can speak of infinite degrees and shades in observance. Alongside an ideal of punctual, meticulous, joyful and persevering observance, many middling, lax, slack observances can be mixed in, full of cracks and leaks.

Every religious who tries to live the demands of the Gospel must make the unbreakable resolution to be extremely careful in keeping the schedules and obligations required of him.

Characteristics of fidelity to the duty of one’s state

There are three main characteristics of fidelity to the duty of one’s state, of exact fidelity to our obligations:

1. The perfect and finished manner. Generally it is not enough to do materially what is commanded; rather, everything asked of us must be carried out in a perfect and finished way. We must pay homage to the finished thing.

How many religious do things haphazardly, however they turn out, however they cost the least effort! They attend prayer bodily, but with their soul absent and distracted; they rush through the breviary, half-pronouncing the words; they carry out their duties reluctantly and sluggishly. And they forget the words of the prophet Jeremiah: cursed is he who does the work of the Lord negligently6.

Of the Curé of Ars his biographer tells us: “He said nothing and did nothing that could have been said or done better.”

2. Love of detail: “What is small is small—said St. Augustine—but to be faithful in small things is a very great thing.”

In terms of fidelity, what is asked of us is to go down to the last detail. In a certain sense it is necessary to pay more attention to small things than to great ones, and this for several reasons: great things present themselves rarely, small things at every moment; great things draw everyone’s attention, small things go almost unnoticed even by ourselves; fidelity in small things is the best guarantee of fidelity in great ones (Le 16:10); he who despises small things will little by little fall in great ones (Sir 19:1).

St. Thérèse of the Child Jesus, for example—the Doctor of the Church—was called by some authors the saint of detail. She did not do strange or extraordinary things, but she always had, as someone has said, “a stubborn and tremendous obstinacy in fulfilling her duty in the smallest things out of love for the Beloved.” To that exquisite fidelity! in small things it is owed that she is, in the words of St. Pius X, “the greatest saint of modern times.”

3. Exact punctuality. The third characteristic of perfect observance is exactness. Doing everything at the appointed hour, neither before nor after. Not disrupting the various occupations. Always being in the place where obedience calls us. Avoiding any voluntary delay, however small. At the sound of the bell, obeying at once.

These may be trifles. But it is these trifles that distinguish the saints from those who are not. Or, as one author rightly says: “holiness does not consist in that, but it is manifested in that.”

Pope John XXII used to say: “Give me a religious who has always been meticulously faithful to his obligations and I will canonize him without further examination.” Jesus Christ, before clarifying and making explicit the spiritual summit that He set forth in the beatitudes, paused to speak of fidelity down to the perfect, the finished, down to the iota, down to the dot, down to each one of these least commandments.

Let us ask Him for the grace to have this spirit in fulfilling the new law, since whoever keeps them and teaches others to keep them in this way will be considered great in the kingdom of heaven7.

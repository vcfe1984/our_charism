Introduction

  1. The evangelization of cultures is the specific end of our Institute1. In particular, in the present context of relativism and cultural pluralism in a globalized world, there is a special urgency to carry out the work of inculturation. That is, on the one hand there emerge the challenges of cultural relativism and de-Christianization, principally in countries of ancient Christian tradition. On the other hand, the Gospel comes into contact with cultural areas until now outside the range of Christianity's radiance, especially among the peoples of Africa and, even more, of Asia, in which there exists a plurality of ancient cultural and religious traditions that opens new tasks for inculturation2.

Nevertheless, inculturation constitutes, above all, an intrinsic requirement of evangelization3. For this reason, the Christianity of the third millennium must respond ever better to this requirement of inculturation4.

Culture in general

1. Its essence

2. First of all, we must have a clear idea of the nature of culture. The noun culture derives from the Latin verb colo (colo, colis, colere), which means to cultivate. Thus, etymologically, by culture we designate the essence and art of cultivating the field. It implies –as can be noted in the words agriculture, pisciculture, apiculture, etc.– a process or activity directed toward obtaining better fruits from nature.

3. From this first sense it came to signify, metaphorically, cultivation in man himself and in all his human dimensions; that is, a second form of cultivation, immanent to man, an activity –intelligent and free– directed immediately to the perfection of man himself, through the unfolding of all his powers in harmony with his true nature. It is a matter of the cultivation of the whole man, body and soul, in a harmonious hierarchy. Cicero called it cultura animi. Thus, culture has a markedly personal sense. But it also has –and today this is predominant– a social sense, including in its meaning all the achievements of man as a social being5. It is a collective tradition that informs the life of all individuals. We can then speak of Hellenic, medieval, Spanish, Argentine culture, etc.

4. This culture in the social sense Fr. Meinvielle calls civilization, distinguishing it from culture in the personal sense: “although the connections between them are great, they cannot be considered identical. Culture… would seem to indicate the activity of man applied to human nature so that it may yield the fruits of which it is capable… it connotes preferably the perfecting of the human personality, while civilization looks first to that of society. We would say, then, that man seeks his culture in civilization; to signify that culture arises as a conquest of the free and personal effort of man, achieved with the help of civilization, which has provided the means to attain it”6. In this latter sense it can further be pointed out that civilization, in its deepest meaning, belongs rather to human culture, since it responds to the spiritual and moral needs of man7.

5. St. John Paul II, uniting both senses –the personal and the social–, defines culture as the “manifestation of man as a person, as a community, as a people and a nation”8. We have, then, that culture is that particular way in which men and peoples cultivate their relationship with nature and with their brothers, with themselves and with God, in order to attain a fully human existence9. Hence it can in a certain way be said that man, through his intellect and freedom, is the creator, in a broad sense, of a better world, built upon the natural world; that is, he can transform natural beings and his own being, perfecting them. Thus, for example, a man through study acquires the habit or perfection of science, or through the practice of the virtues becomes an honest man10.

6. St. Thomas, commenting on Aristotle, says that “the human race lives by art and reason”11. Relying on these words St. John Paul II affirmed that culture is a “characteristic of human life as such”12; “it is a specific way of man's ‘existing’ and ‘being’”; only man –because he transcends matter by virtue of the life of his spirit– “is the only ontic subject of culture” and is “also its only object and its end.” That is, culture is of man, by man and for man. It embraces the whole activity of man, his intellect and affectivity, his search for meaning, his customs and his ethical resources. Culture is so connatural to man that his nature does not attain its full expression except through culture. Therefore, “culture is that through which man, as man, becomes more man, ‘is’ more, has more access to ‘being’”13.

7. The teaching of the Church in recent times, and more particularly in Gaudium et spes14, has left us a clear teaching about culture and its essence: “It is proper to the person to arrive at true and full humanity through culture, that is, through the cultivation of the goods and values of nature.” That is, man must, by means of a process –culture–, come to be man in fullness, with all his corresponding good or perfection. This includes man's activity in its different dimensions, through which he is integrally perfected: “The word culture in its general sense indicates everything whereby man refines and develops the manifold gifts of his soul and body.” This process or development necessarily implies a repercussion in the social order; and hence in the same document this other dimension or sense of culture is also affirmed: “man strives to bring the world under his control by knowledge and labor; he renders social life more human, both in the family and in the whole civic community, through the progress of customs and institutions.” In this way there is constituted what we may call the cultural patrimony of the human race in its various manifestations: “and finally, throughout the course of time, he expresses in his works his deepest spiritual experiences and aspirations, communicates them and preserves them, so that they may be of advantage to many, and even to the whole human race”15.

8. It is from this “historical and social aspect” of culture that we can speak of a “plurality of cultures”: “for from the different ways of using things, of working and of expressing oneself, of practicing religion and of shaping customs, of establishing laws and juridical institutions, of fostering the sciences and the arts and of cultivating beauty, there arise different conditions of common life and various ways of combining the values of life”16. Thus we have the diverse cultural patrimonies proper to each people, from which man learns the values he receives by tradition.

9. Finally, we may point out, on the basis of all that has been said, the decisive influence of culture on the life and perfecting of men. Culture is a fundamental dimension of the person and of human communities. Since culture is the result of the life and activity of a human group, the persons who belong to that group are formed, to a great extent, by the culture in which they live. As persons and societies change, the culture also changes with them. When culture is transformed, it likewise transforms persons and societies17. From this it is easy to deduce the fundamental importance, together with the decisiveness, of carrying out an evangelization that deeply penetrates the cultures of men and of peoples.

2. Foundations of culture

10 . We have said that culture is the activity man carries out in order to perfect himself. But not everything he does truly contributes to his perfecting, and therefore not all his activities are truly “culture.”

Hence the importance of establishing the true foundations so as to be able, on their basis, to discern which manifestations of man are truly culture and which are not.

The foundation must be maintained principally on the philosophical plane and in a threefold domain: metaphysical, anthropological and ethical, for in this way we reach what is essential –what endures– above the vicissitudes of history and of the various cultural creations.

a) Metaphysical foundation

11. It is metaphysical reflection that provides the necessary openness for all true thought and on which all true thought must rest. If human knowledge were strictly limited to the world of sense experience, that is, without a true metaphysical horizon, it could never find the ultimate meaning of things and of man himself. St. John Paul II expressed it thus: “It is not possible to stop at experience alone; even when experience expresses and makes manifest the interiority of man and his spirituality, speculative reflection must reach his spiritual nature and the foundation on which it rests”18.

Therefore, not every philosophical reflection grounds a true human culture, since there are many currents of thought widespread today whose errors lead to the distortion of any culture that rests upon them.

By way of example, we cite some, following St. John Paul II in Fides et ratio. In the first place, eclecticism, which accepts the different ideas that come from the different philosophical currents without discerning the portion of truth each of them may have. Historicism, which in a similar way considers that each philosophy has its truth, but only provided it is seen within the historical period and purpose in which it was formulated. Scientism, which admits as true only the knowledge of the positive sciences. Nihilism, which denies the humanity of man and his very identity, since it denies being and therefore all objective truth.

These and other currents of thought show the need to ground culture in a true metaphysics, which can be none other than the metaphysics of being. For it is the metaphysics of being, and only the metaphysics of being, that can restore to man the true place he occupies in society and provide the true foundations on which man must build his culture.

And this is so because it is the philosophy of being that allows a full and comprehensive openness to the whole of reality, surpassing every limit and making it possible to reach Him who brings all things to perfection19.

The philosophies we have described, and all those that are not grounded in the act of being, are far from satisfying the deepest aspirations of man. They are far from providing man with “an absolute capable of giving an answer and meaning to all his searching. Something ultimate and the foundation of everything else. […] Hypotheses may be fascinating, but they do not satisfy”20. For there are many systems that speak of God, that affirm the existence of God, but it is not the God we men need and whom the Christian faith proclaims21.

It is from a philosophy of being that man can find his true foundation, which is being, and can find his ultimate end, which is Being by Essence, and also find his depth, which is freedom. In this way he will be able to discover the true cultural values.

For ultimately, all cultural values –of science, of art, of laws and customs, etc.–, insofar as they are objective expressions of beauty, truth and goodness, are grounded in the act of being22.

12. The pastoral care of culture will be able to offer a positive and effective response to the great challenges or even dramas of “postmodern” man, principally from the metaphysical level, through the philosophy of being. For the nihilist position, the present horizon of many philosophies that have strayed from the sense of being, denies all objective truth and, consequently, that which grounds human dignity and freedom23. Hence the urgency of recovering the metaphysics of being, a dynamic philosophy that allows full and comprehensive openness toward the whole of reality, until reaching Him who brings all things to perfection24.

b) Anthropological foundation

13. The anthropological conception is decisive in the consideration of culture, for man is the author and at the same time the recipient of culture. Culture is grounded in human nature, in particular in its spiritual openness to the universal and to transcendence. The nature of man constitutes the measure of culture25.

14. Therefore, it is necessary to preserve an integral view of man, one that overcomes a partial and reductionist reading, capable of including his different aspects and dimensions as they are given in reality, that is, united in that unique and unrepeatable subject, at once corporeal and spiritual, that each man is. Pope St. John Paul II affirmed: “This fundamental dimension is man, man in his integrity, man who lives at the same time in the sphere of material values and in that of spiritual values”26.

15. From the perspective of an integral reading of man, we have in the first place the culture of material values, which correspond to the human being in his bodily dimension.

16. So then, an authentically human culture requires the values of human work and of technology ordered to the production of material goods, economic and political relations, the sciences directed toward health and natural prosperity, etc.; that is, everything necessary for the material aspect of human and earthly life, and in conformity with the dignity of the human being.

17. We have, in the second place, the culture of spiritual values, corresponding to the spiritual dimension of the human being.

18. Man, by reason of his spiritual dimension, exercises a series of activities that transcend matter, for the soul, by reason of its immateriality, is “in a certain way all things.” Here is found the foundation of man's transcendence; for by his spirituality he is open to all beings and, therefore, to the divine Being. This is what Grabmann affirms: “St. Thomas has grounded psychologically the meaning and value of the bodily, and the right as well as the duty of earthly culture, in his doctrine of the spiritual human soul as the formal principle of the body… but at the same time, and I would not want to omit this, he has also pointed out the transcendent and the ordering of this earthly life toward an eternal existence of the richest and purest spiritual life, by demonstrating that the human soul is a subsistent essence according to its nature, spiritual and personally immortal”27. From the spiritual dimension of man and his consequent openness to the universal and to transcendence, there emerges the culture of spiritual and universal values related to beauty, truth and goodness, ultimately to God.

19. The bodily and spiritual dimensions of the human being, as we were saying, must be considered from the perspective of an integral and, at the same time, unitary view of the human person; although different, both dimensions form a single subject, at once material and spiritual.

20. Consequently, an authentically human culture must always strive to achieve and maintain this synthesis of spirit and matter: “Man is always in his totality: in the integral whole of his spiritual and material subjectivity; in culture one must always seek the integral man, the whole man, in the whole truth of his spiritual and bodily subjectivity; this is a sufficient basis for not superimposing on culture –an authentically human system, a splendid synthesis of spirit and body– preconceived divisions and oppositions. Indeed, neither an absolutization of matter in the structure of the human subject nor, inversely, an absolutization of spirit in that same structure expresses the truth about man or renders any service to his culture”28.

21. Benedict XVI, in the context of eros and human love, affirms: “Man is truly himself when his body and soul are intimately united; the challenge of eros can be said to be truly overcome when this unification is achieved. Should man aspire to be pure spirit and to reject the flesh as pertaining to his animal nature alone, then spirit and body would both lose their dignity. On the other hand, should he deny the spirit and consider matter, the body, as the only reality, he would likewise lose his greatness. The epicure Gassendi used to offer Descartes the humorous greeting: ‘O Soul!’ And Descartes would reply: ‘O Flesh!’ Yet it is neither the spirit alone nor the body alone that loves: it is man, the person, a unified creature composed of body and soul, who loves. Only when both dimensions are truly united does man attain his full stature”29.

22. On the basis of this integral and, at the same time, unitary view of the human being, in his bodily and at once spiritual dimension, it is necessary to affirm, finally, the supremacy of the spirit over the body and, therefore, of the culture of spiritual goods over the culture of material goods, and the ordering of the one to the other. The body exists for the soul, since the soul is its form; therefore material goods are subordinate to spiritual ones. The body is limited by matter, to the immediacy of the here and now; the soul, on the other hand, being spiritual, is in a certain way unlimited and infinite, open to all being. For this reason St. John Paul II urged us to “mobilize all the forces that channel the spiritual dimension of human existence, that bear witness to the primacy of the spiritual in man, of what corresponds to the dignity of his intellect, his will and his heart”30.

23. For this reason he defines culture as “that through which man, as man, becomes more man, ‘is’ more, has more access to Being”31.

24. From this there also follows the primacy of being over having, which is restricted to the field of material goods.

25. For this reason, “culture is always situated in an essential and necessary relationship to what man is, whereas its relationship to what man has, to his ‘having,’ is not only secondary but entirely relative; and it is a factor of culture only when man, by means of his ‘having,’ can at the same time ‘be’ more fully as man, become more fully man in all the dimensions of his existence, in everything that characterizes his humanity.” Therefore, the Holy Father concludes: “Culture is thought of and spoken of principally in relation to the nature of man; and then only in a secondary and indirect way in relation to the world of his products”32.

26. We can conclude by pointing out that what is at stake in a pastoral care of culture is to restore to man his condition as a creature “in the image and likeness of God”33. That is, it consists in the anthropological grounding of culture through a view of man as that single subject, at once bodily and spiritual, open to the universal and to transcendence. Furthermore, in a Christian anthropological view, one must emphasize the fundamental truth of Jesus Christ as the fullness of man and of every authentically human culture34.

c) Ethical foundation

27. Man, starting from the foundation of his human nature, through the dynamism of his acts in conformity with the truth of the good, must tend toward his full realization. St. John Paul II said that “in order to perfect himself in his specific order, the person must do good and avoid evil, safeguard the transmission and preservation of life, improve and develop the riches of the sensible world, cultivate social life, seek the truth, practice the good, contemplate beauty (cf. Th., I-II, 94, 2)”35. That is why “there is no doubt either that the first and fundamental dimension of culture is sound morality: moral culture”36.

28. One speaks of perfecting oneself in one's specific order because one starts from the nature common to all, that is, the human nature that each one possesses singularly. On the contrary, establishing a separation between freedom, which expresses the whole domain of man's activity, and human nature, “as a certain current of contemporary philosophy would have it, obscures reason's perception of the universality of the moral law”37.

29. It must be pointed out that we are witnessing a disorientation of man's ethical conscience, or moral subjectivism, which derives principally from the crisis concerning the truth about the good. In this way man, by means of his conscience, grants himself the privilege of fixing autonomously the criteria of good and evil, and of acting accordingly38. Man, then, must orient himself to the truth of the good, which presupposes a philosophical anthropology and a metaphysics of the good. The present challenge regarding the ethical grounding of cultural values lies in defending and interpreting the values rooted in the very nature of the human being39.

30. We must affirm that there exists something permanent that transcends all concrete cultures and that grounds their reason for being, and that “something is precisely the nature of man: precisely this nature is the measure of culture and is the condition for man not to be the prisoner of any of his cultures, but to defend his personal dignity by living in accordance with the profound truth of his being”40. On the other hand, it is this nature of man, common to all, that alone justifies the natural law, which constitutes the universal “grammar” by which a fruitful dialogue can be established between cultures and religions, a dialogue ordered to the search for truth within the framework of respect and mutual love.

31. In summary, man, as the image of God, is an “artisan.” For although God alone is the Creator, the one who gives being itself to things, drawing them out of nothing –ex nihilo sui et subiecti–, nevertheless man too, precisely because he is the image of God, has the task of subduing the earth (cf. Gen 1:27), using something already existing and giving it form and meaning41. He carries out this task, above all, by being the artisan of his own life, of which he must make a “masterpiece.” “The human person is the author of his own acts and responsible for their moral value”42. Man must form his own personality and realize himself, and he accomplishes this in the process of his “life” through free decisions that must be directed toward truth and goodness: “The way in which man establishes his own relationship with being, with truth and with goodness, is by living and working”43.

32. [Conclusion] By way of conclusion to this chapter devoted to the foundations of culture, we can say that at present there is a “crisis of meaning,” for human thought shuts itself up within the limits of its own immanence, without any reference to the transcendent. Therefore, in the first place, Philosophy must renew the awareness of ultimate values and give a foundation to the sense of man's ultimate end44. In the second place, man's capacity to attain knowledge of objective truth must be affirmed45. These two requirements entail the third: the need for a Philosophy of genuinely metaphysical range, capable of transcending empirical data in order to reach, in its search for truth, something absolute, ultimate and foundational. A great challenge facing us at the end of this millennium is to know how to make the passage from phenomenon to foundation. The metaphysical element is the necessary path for overcoming the situation of crisis that today affects large sectors of Philosophy and thus for correcting certain erroneous forms of behavior widespread in our society46.

3. Spheres of culture and hierarchy of cultural values

33. From what was said in the preceding chapter we can deduce the various spheres found in culture and the just hierarchy of cultural values. For on the basis of the different dimensions or formalities of man and of his moral dynamism, we have the different spheres of culture. These cultural values possess a just hierarchy and ordering that corresponds to the hierarchy existing among the various formalities of man.

a) Cultural spheres

34. We have, in the first place, everything that refers to the sphere of the “culture of making,” that is, the activity of man directed to material things in order to transform them into useful goods. This is the dimension proper to human work and to the technology it uses as an instrument. Here too belongs artistic making, the art of man that transforms material things, making them beautiful. This dimension of “human making,” although directed to the immediate perfection of things external to man, nevertheless starts from man himself and is ordered to him. It is man who possesses –because he is spiritual– that intelligent dominion over things and who has in himself those habits –or skills– by which he transforms them. The human person is always the subject, center and end of all human activity.

35. In the second place, we have the cultural sphere ordered directly to the perfection of man himself: “physical culture,” which perfects his body (for example, by means of diet or gymnastics), and, above all, the “culture of acting,” which refers to the perfection of the human soul, or better, of man insofar as he is man. That is, intellectual activity ordered to the search for and acquisition of truth, and the activity of the will which, by means of the moral virtues, tends toward the perfection of man in the possession of his true good. In this way man attains his moral perfection and becomes morally good. This moral dimension constitutes the decisive dimension of human culture, as St. John Paul II affirmed: “there is no doubt either that the first and fundamental dimension of culture is sound morality: moral culture”47.

36. Hence the fundamental importance in the life of man of the exercise of his free will, for, as St. Thomas says, “everyone who has a will is called good insofar as he has a good will, because by the will we use all the things that are in us. Hence a man is called good not because he has a good intellect, but because he has a good will”48. “A good will makes a man good (bonum simpliciter); and for this reason the virtue (of the appetitive part of the soul) by which the will is good is the one that makes the virtuous man truly good (simpliciter bonum)”49.

37. Why is man good, that is, why does he attain his perfection, especially through the unfolding of his will? Because the will looks to the good as its proper object; it is not simply a matter of the particular good of some power, for example the good of being an artist or of knowing the natural sciences, of handling technology, etc., but of the good of man considered integrally, in all his powers. This is the sphere of the will, which moves itself and all the other powers of man, leading him to his good simpliciter

38. [Transcendent dimension of culture] We have pointed out that the moral dimension of human culture constitutes its first and fundamental dimension. But this moral dimension has its foundation and ultimate end in God; that is, the moral dimension, in order to be fully such, necessarily includes the transcendent-religious dimension of the human person; it is, therefore, an ethical-religious dimension. The culminating point of man's ascent to “being more” is reached in the possession of God, which is why St. Thomas sums up the whole of human morality as “the movement of the rational creature toward God”50.

39. Man is essentially a religious being, which is why the transcendent dimension constitutes the heart of culture. “Every culture is an effort to ponder the mystery of the world and in particular of man: it is a way of giving expression to the transcendent dimension of human life. The heart of every culture is its approach to the greatest of all mysteries: the mystery of God”51. The great cultural challenge of today lies in the sphere of man's openness to truth and goodness, ultimately to transcendence and to the mystery of God. Man's “ontological” dependence on God is the ultimate foundation of the full development of human freedom and life, as well as of respect for the dignity and life of every human being. For this reason the central place in every culture is occupied by the attitude man takes before the mystery of God52.

40. We may further point out that values are not simply pure creations of the human spirit. St. John Paul II noted: “in some currents of modern thought freedom has come to be exalted to the point of being considered an absolute, which would be the source of values”53.

41. Cultural values, insofar as and to the extent that they constitute authentic and objective expressions of beauty, truth and goodness, are grounded in and related to being, and ultimately to God, Being by Essence, first Truth and supreme Good, ultimate foundation and first Cause of being by participation, of its truth and goodness.

42. On the other hand, man, through his spirit, remains open to the universal and the transcendent, to the divine being insofar as it is the highest reality and the supreme Good.

43. As St. Thomas explains, the object or reality toward which man's will is directed is the universal good, as the object of the intellect is universal truth. “Hence nothing can bring rest to man's will except the universal good, which is not found in anything created, but only in God, because every creature has participated goodness”54.

44. In this way, the perfection and perfect happiness of man is found only in God, inasmuch as He, as infinite and perfect good, “can fill man's will”55.

45. This is the profound meaning of what our Lord Jesus Christ answered the rich young man (Mt 19:16ff.). He asks Jesus: Teacher, what must I do to gain eternal life? “For the young man, rather than a question about rules to be followed, it is a question of full meaning for life. That is, this question is ultimately an appeal to the absolute Good which attracts us and beckons us; it is the echo of a call from God, who is the origin and goal of man's life”56.

46. What does Jesus answer him? That God alone is good: One alone is the Good (Mt 19:17)57. For God alone is good by essence and the source of all participated goodness. Jesus Christ with His answer goes straight to what is fundamental: “indeed, to ask about the good ultimately means to turn toward God, the fullness of goodness”58. Ultimately, one can speak of goodness, and more particularly of morally good actions, in relation to God, supreme Goodness, source and end of all participated goodness. Hence the fundamental importance of the transcendent dimension of the human being and of every culture: “Jesus relates –said St. John Paul II– the question of morally good action to its religious roots, to the acknowledgment of God, who alone is goodness, fullness of life, the final end of human activity, and perfect happiness”59.

b) Hierarchy of values and subordination

47. We have, then, the different spheres of cultural values, which must be considered according to a just hierarchy dependent on the various formalities of the human being and, in particular, on the subordination of man's material and instinctive dimension to his spiritual and interior dimension. This is what the philosopher Aristotle already taught in antiquity: the choice or possession of natural goods, goods of the body, riches, friends and other things, will be good if they help us to contemplate God. It is fitting –that is, good– for man to act in accordance with his determined nature60. Thus it is natural for him –man is a social animal– to live in society, for, not being self-sufficient, he needs the help of others. Man, insofar as he possesses a certain animality, naturally disposes of lower things for the needs of his life. Furthermore, man has –as something proper to him– rationality, and, according to the nature of his being, man has a body for the sake of the soul, and the lower powers of the soul for the sake of reason; and therefore it is fitting that the body help the soul, and the lower powers help the higher powers of the soul, it being thus “naturally right that man should look after his body and the intimate tendencies of the soul in such a way that the act of reason and its good are in no way hindered… Finally, the good of reason, which is virtue, culminates in the possession of God: those things are naturally fitting to each one by which he tends to his natural end; since man is naturally ordered to God as his end, it follows that all those things that lead man to the knowledge and love of God are naturally right”61.

48. To the extent that men do or do not live this subordination of goods in a just hierarchy of values, we will have the different forms of cultures or, on the contrary, of “anticultures.” Therefore, explains Fr. Meinvielle, man “can rise, then, from the lowest reality to God by participation; or he can be content with being only man, as happened in the rationalism of the classical age; or he can become an animal, as happens in the men of the nineteenth century; or he can be simply a ‘thing,’ as the proletarian dictatorship strives to make him”62.

49. That is, in man there are found four formalities: that of reality or thing, that of animal, that of rational being and, finally, the supernatural or divine; these formalities explain the four possible stages of a cultural cycle. In a normal culture, these four formalities must be articulated in a hierarchical ordering that ensures their unity of dynamism. “And thus man is something in order to feel as an animal, he feels as an animal in order to reason and understand as a man, he reasons and understands as a man in order to love God as God. For this reason, just as there is no culture ‘more anticultural’ than the atheistic one, there can be no culture more cultural (of greater cultural density) than one that stands under the sign of holiness”63.

50. [Conclusion] In the just hierarchy of cultural values, and decisively in the emergence of the ethical-religious dimension of man, the destiny of the life and culture of men, and of each man, is at stake. The Holy Father John Paul II ended his address at the UN with this exhortation: “Consciences must be moralized! The efforts of human consciences must be increased in proportion to the tension between good and evil to which men are subjected at the end of the twentieth century. We must be convinced of the priority of ethics over technology, of the primacy of the person over things, of the superiority of spirit over matter64. The cause of man will be served if science allies itself with conscience. The man of science will truly help humanity if he preserves ‘the sense of man's transcendence over the world and of God's over man’”65.

51. On the contrary, when the impossibility of knowing the truth is upheld and the search for the transcendent and absolute is abandoned, then man's reason is placed as an instrument at the service of utilitarian ends, of pleasure or of power. In this way, for example, scientific research, deprived of all ethical reference, yields to the temptation of demiurgic power over nature and over the human being himself; and what man produces, in particular the products that contain a special portion of his genius, can turn, indirectly but radically, against man himself, as for example everything related to nuclear weapons or to dominion over human life through the new techniques in the field of bioethics66.

52. Therefore, this effort to recover in men's consciences the need to respect the just hierarchy and subordination of cultural values and the primacy of the moral and transcendent dimension of the human person does not constitute a peripheral and useless effort, but on the contrary the essential and most urgent task for responding positively to the challenges of the present and to the deepest aspirations of every human heart.

53. Let us conclude with a text of St. Thomas: “The whole man exists for the sake of his ultimate end, which transcends him, namely, for the sake of the possession of God… rational creatures, besides this image of the divine Goodness, are directed to God as their ultimate end in a special way, inasmuch as by their activity, by their knowledge and love, they can attain God”67.

Evangelization of cultures

1. Its essence

54. Having seen what culture is, its foundations, its spheres and its hierarchy of values, we now go on to consider what it means to evangelize cultures.

a) Gospel

55. To speak of evangelization, the first thing required is to clarify the notion of the Gospel. Etymologically it means “Good News.” The newness brought by our Lord, the Word made flesh. The New Law consists principally in the grace of the Holy Spirit infused into the heart of man. St. Thomas says: “the New Law is principally the very grace of the Holy Spirit, which is given to the faithful of Christ”68. St. Paul calls it the law of faith (Rom 3:27), the spiritual law (Rom 7:14) and explicitly the law of the spirit of life (Rom 8:2). A New Law which, secondarily, is written in the book of the New Testament. This written law has no other reason for being than to be ordered to grace: whether as a disposition, containing all that our intellect must know for faith and what the love of our will must despise so that man may be capable of grace; or by teaching everything relating to the use of grace, such as the works of the virtues and the reception of the sacraments.

56. This New Law, or grace of the Holy Spirit, is the only thing that saves man: I am not ashamed of the Gospel, which is the power of God for the salvation of everyone who believes (Rom 1:16). It saves because it justifies man through the forgiveness of sins, healing his wounded nature and elevating it to a participation in the very divine nature, making men children of God. For this reason, in all truth, “the good of grace in one person is greater than the natural good of the whole universe”69.

b) Evangelization

57. Evangelization is a rich, complex and dynamic reality, and “means for the Church bringing the Good News into all the strata of humanity”70. Therefore, to evangelize consists principally in bringing the grace of God to all men, making a new humanity, that is, of new men created according to God in true justice and holiness (Eph 4:23-24). In this sense, the end toward which evangelization tends is “this interior change, and if it had to be expressed in one sentence the best way of stating it would be to say that the Church evangelizes when she seeks to convert, solely through the divine power of the message she proclaims (cf. Rom 1:16), both the personal and collective consciences of people, the activities in which they engage, and the lives and concrete milieux which are theirs”71.

58. Evangelization is carried out, as the same document explains, by means of:

59. – The witness of life: “through this wordless witness these Christians stir up irresistible questions in the hearts of those who see how they live: Why are they like this? Why do they live in this way? What or who is it that inspires them? Why are they in our midst? Such a witness is already a silent proclamation of the Good News and a very powerful and effective one. Here we have an initial act of evangelization… all Christians are called to this witness, and in this way they can be real evangelizers”72.

60. – The explicit proclamation of “the name, the teaching, the life, the promises, the Kingdom and the mystery of Jesus of Nazareth, the Son of God, which is so necessary and important in evangelization that it is often taken for the whole, although it is no more than one aspect”73.

61.– Finally, and this is the most important, the vital adherence to the program of life –a life in fact already transformed– that He proposes. It is a matter of adherence… to a new way of being, of living, of living in community, which the Gospel inaugurates74.

62. For this reason, authentic evangelization must lead to and culminate in the worthy reception of the sacraments, for through them the grace of the Holy Spirit is communicated in the ordinary way. “One cannot sufficiently stress the fact that evangelization does not consist only of the preaching and teaching of a doctrine. For evangelization must touch life: the natural life to which it gives a new meaning, thanks to the evangelical perspectives that it reveals; and the supernatural life, which is not the negation but the purification and elevation of the natural life. This supernatural life finds its living expression in the seven sacraments and in the admirable radiation of grace and holiness which they contain”75. Therefore, through the word and the sacraments, the Church brings the Gospel to men, that is, she offers them and communicates to them instrumentally divine grace, making men new creatures vitally united to Christ and to the Church. Finally, it must be pointed out that, together with the proclamation of the Word and the administration of the sacraments, the service of charity also forms part of the essential spheres of evangelization76.

c) Meaning of evangelization of cultures

63. We come to the central point: the question of the meaning of the evangelization of cultures. St. Paul VI, in Evangelii nuntiandi, specifies that evangelization bears upon the culture and cultures of man, thereby making explicit the whole content and force that evangelization virtually contains. “All this could be expressed in the following words: what matters is to evangelize man's culture and cultures –not in a purely decorative way, as it were by applying a thin veneer, but in a vital way, in depth and right to their very roots– in the wide and rich sense which these terms have in Gaudium et spes, 50”77.

64. Therefore, one must evangelize not superficially but in a radical way, incarnating the Gospel in the heart of cultures, that is, in the life and cultural values of men. Gaudium et spes devotes a point to the relationship of culture with faith, inasmuch as culture “can serve as a preparation for the reception of the message of the Gospel, and thus be informed with divine charity by Him who came to save the world. In this sense, by means of culture, the soul of man, freer from servitude to things, is led without obstacles to the worship and contemplation of his Creator. And even more, under the impulse of grace, it is disposed to know the Word of God, who was already in the world as the true light that enlightens every man (Jn 1:9-10)”78. Therefore, culture disposes the human spirit to receive the Gospel message and, at the same time, constitutes a resource for “spreading and explaining the message of Christ. For its part, the Gospel message can enter into true communion with the various forms of culture. The Gospel, in this way, from within, makes fruitful, defends, perfects and restores in Christ, with its own heavenly riches, the spiritual riches and characteristics of each people and age”79.

65. This is precisely, following the analogy with the mystery of the Incarnate Word, what has come to be called the “inculturation of the Gospel,” or the incarnation of the divine message in the heart of cultures. The evangelization of culture, or inculturation, as Redemptoris missio teaches, “means the intimate transformation of authentic cultural values through their integration in Christianity and the insertion of Christianity in the various human cultures… Through inculturation the Church makes the Gospel incarnate in different cultures and at the same time introduces peoples, together with their cultures, into her own community. She transmits to them her own values, at the same time taking the good elements that already exist in them and renewing them from within”80. This is what Evangelii nuntiandi clearly teaches: “For the Church it is a question not only of preaching the Gospel in ever wider geographic areas or to ever greater numbers of people, but also of affecting and as it were upsetting, through the power of the Gospel, mankind's criteria of judgment, determining values, points of interest, lines of thought, sources of inspiration and models of life, which are in contrast with the Word of God and the plan of salvation.” All this means “to evangelize man's culture and cultures… always taking the person as one's starting-point and always coming back to the relationships of people among themselves and with God”81.

66. We may emphasize, finally, the fundamental influence of culture on each man and, consequently, the need to inculturate the Gospel. That is, since culture is a fundamental dimension of the person and of human communities, it is also the privileged place of encounter with the message of Christ. So much so that evangelization itself is at stake here, for “a faith that does not become culture is a faith not fully received, not thoroughly thought through, not faithfully lived”82. For their part, cultures, to the extent that they are rooted in all that is authentically human, remain open and tend toward their perfection in Jesus Christ.

2. The mystery of Christ and the evangelization of cultures

67. We have already briefly considered the meaning of the evangelization of cultures. Now we shall refer to its foundation and to the different aspects of this single process of inculturation.

a) The Incarnation as the foundation of inculturation

68. [Gospel and culture] We must point out, in the first place, that Gospel and culture are two distinct and therefore autonomous realities. The Gospel is the revelation that God makes to man through Jesus Christ, the Word made flesh, who comes to bring to humanity “truth” and “grace”: grace and truth have come to us through Jesus Christ (Jn 1:17). Culture, on the other hand, “flows immediately from the rational and social nature of man”83. In this sense, “the Gospel, and therefore evangelization, are certainly not identical with culture, and they are independent in regard to all cultures”84. The Gospel message transcends every culture. For this reason, “at the same time, sent to all peoples of every time and place, the Church is not bound exclusively and indissolubly to any race or nation, to any particular way of life, to any customary practices, ancient or modern”85.

69. But, in the second place, it must be pointed out that, although distinct and legitimately autonomous, in analogy with the mystery of the Incarnate Word –two really distinct natures, the human and the divine, but united in the one divine Person of the Word–, culture and the Gospel are called to enter into communion, forming a positive and fruitful synthesis. That is, the Gospel must become incarnate in the culture and cultures of man in order to make them fruitful with its divine power and, at the same time, be enriched with new cultural expressions. This bond between the Good News of Christ and human culture takes its origin in divine revelation itself, “for God, revealing Himself to His people to the extent of a full manifestation of Himself in His Incarnate Son, has spoken according to the culture proper to each epoch”86. The Church, in her mission of evangelizing all peoples, has continued this same process, making use of the “language” of the different cultures she has encountered in order to transmit the message of Christ.

70. [Foundation] One must, then, carry out a profound evangelization that “incarnates” the Gospel in the heart of cultures. The foundation of this inculturation is the mystery of the Incarnate Word: “From this derives, at its root, one of the principal elements of our spirituality. […] through the Incarnation, nothing authentically human is foreign to us. Therefore, we must work to prolong the Incarnation in all reality, specifically by evangelizing the culture, because ‘no human activity is foreign to the Gospel’”87.

71. In this sense St. John Paul II taught: “the term acculturation or inculturation, neologism though it may be, expresses very well one of the elements of the great mystery of the Incarnation”88.

72. St. John Paul II himself, in Pastores dabo vobis, refers to the “Catholic principles” of “inculturation” and points out that these principles “are linked to the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word of God and to Christian anthropology, and they illumine the authentic meaning of inculturation”89.

73. The foundation and model of inculturation is the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word of God, because in this mystery the divine nature and the human nature are safeguarded, each with its respective autonomy, and at the same time there is manifested the unique bond that places them in mutual relationship without confusion90. Analogously, through the task of inculturation, “the Gospel penetrates vitally into cultures, becomes incarnate in them”91. That is, the Gospel, through inculturation, enters into profound communion with cultures, through a mutual relationship which, without confusion and with respect for their respective autonomy, at the same time assumes and transforms with its divine power all the authentically human values present in cultures, thus achieving a unique bond and a vital synthesis that enriches and perfects cultures and at the same time the Church as well, through new cultural expressions of her one Gospel message.

74. We may further point out that just as the Word assumed human nature in His one divine Person –a union of assumption that leaves the human nature of Christ intact while elevating it to the dignity of being the human nature of the divine Person of the Word–, in an analogous way the Gospel assumes the cultures that are to be evangelized, which, remaining intact in their own cultural values, are at the same time consolidated, renewed and perfected with the riches of the grace of Christ and of the Good News of the Gospel. “A mere union is not enough for us, nor is merely putting a nominalist label on reality, for it to be truly ruled by Christ”92; rather, the Gospel must penetrate into the heart of culture93; “what matters is to evangelize man's cultures –not in a purely decorative way, as it were by applying a thin veneer, but in a vital way, in depth and right to their very roots”94. It is a matter of bringing the Good News to men following the style of the Incarnation: penetrating and transforming human culture “from within”95.

75. In conclusion, on the basis of the foundation, and in analogy with the mystery of the Incarnation, the Church in her mission of evangelizing all peoples must strive in the task of inculturation, that is, of the insertion of the Gospel and of the Church into men, their values and their ways of living, in a word, into the heart of cultures96.

b) The process of inculturation: assumption, purification and divinization

76. Inculturation constitutes a single process of assumption, purification and perfection of cultures, in analogy with the mystery of the Incarnation. Let us see what each of these stages means, and what it implies both for the mission of the Church and, particularly, for cultures.

Assumption

77. In the process of inculturation we have, in the first place, the task of the assumption, on the part of the Church and of the Gospel, of the authentically human values present in cultures. That is, evangelization must assume all the elements of truth and goodness present in cultures, which reflect all that is authentically human; in particular, the human openness to transcendence in the groping search for the Absolute that is expressed in religious traditions, which constitute a principal part of human cultures. That is, one must assume the “seeds of the Word,” which constitute a preparation for the Gospel and find their perfect fulfillment in Jesus Christ.

78. This does not consist in a mere “adaptation or accommodation,” but in a true assumption of all that is authentically human. “The proclamation of the Church is not afraid to make use of contemporary cultural expressions. Indeed, these, by a certain analogy with the humanity of Christ, are called, so to speak, to participate in the dignity of the divine Word”97.

79. This is what Evangelii nuntiandi calls “permeation”: the Gospel, “independent in regard to cultures… not necessarily incompatible with them, but capable of permeating them all”98.

80. It must be emphasized that if the Church in her evangelizing mission does not manage to assume the authentically cultural values of the peoples with whom she comes into contact, then such cultures will in fact remain, and be considered, separate from and foreign to the faith. On the contrary, through this ever-renewed process of assuming authentically human values, the Church “becomes a more intelligible sign of what she is, and a more effective instrument of mission”99. That is, by speaking a cultural language intelligible to the men of different peoples and cultures and by assuming their authentically human values and customs, they will be able to discover that the Gospel is not only not something foreign and strange, but above all constitutes the definitive answer that sustains and gives ultimate meaning to all those values, and, ultimately, the Good News that all men, in the depths of their hearts, long to hear. For we must not forget that those values or seeds of the Word constitute a preparation for the Gospel and find their perfect fulfillment in Jesus Christ. That is, cultures, to the extent that they are rooted in what is authentically human, “tend” toward their perfection in Jesus Christ, toward their full realization through the reception of the Gospel.

81. We may also emphasize the need and urgency of the assumption of cultures in the evangelizing mission of the Church, recalling once again that culture is a fundamental dimension of the person and of peoples and, therefore, the “privileged place of encounter” with the Good News of the Gospel. In this sense St. John Paul II taught, for example, that missionaries must “learn the language of the region in which they work, become familiar with the most important expressions of the local culture, and discover its values through direct experience,” for “only if they have this kind of awareness will they be able to bring to people the knowledge of the hidden mystery in a credible and fruitful way”100. In this same sense the Pope said in Cameroon: “on the other hand, it is true that the Christian faith must be good news for each of the peoples; therefore it must respond to the noblest expectations of their hearts, it must prove capable of being assimilated in their language, of finding an application in the age-old traditions that their own wisdom would have gradually elaborated to guarantee social cohesion and the maintenance of physical and moral health”101.

82. Furthermore, only through this process of assumption can the purification and transformation of cultures be accomplished, because what is not assumed is not redeemed102. “Through inculturation the Church makes the Gospel incarnate in different cultures. She transmits to them her own values, at the same time taking the good elements that already exist in them and renewing them from within”103.

83. In summary, this assuming, on the part of the Gospel and of the Church, of the cultural elements of truth and goodness in order to transform them with its divine power consists in discovering with joy and respect the seeds of the Word scattered in the national and religious traditions of the different peoples104 and, at the same time, in continuing the requirement that “has marked her whole historical journey but which today is particularly acute and urgent”105.

Purification

84. In the process of inculturation we have, in the second place, the task of the purification of cultures. The Magisterium emphasizes that in the process of inculturation all that is truly human and, therefore, good must be assumed106. All that is not human, and even more, anti-human, analogously to what happens in the mystery of the Incarnation, cannot be assumed. This is one of the two principles that St. John Paul II establishes as fundamental in the process of inculturation: “compatibility with the Gospel of the various cultures to be assumed, and communion with the universal Church”107.

85. Just as the divine Word assumed human flesh, like us in all things but sin, thereby assuming all and only what is human, in an analogous way the Gospel must assume all that is truly human –the values found in cultures– and in no way error and sin, the anti-values that do not belong to human nature as such but only in fact, by reason of its sinful condition. We must not forget that culture is “a product of man and, consequently, marked by sin. In the work of inculturation, discernment must be exercised, since there is the risk of passing uncritically from a sort of alienation from culture to an overvaluation of it. For culture too must be ‘purified, elevated and perfected’”108.

86. Furthermore, just as the divine Word, in becoming incarnate, came to destroy the old man and his concupiscences (cf. Eph 4:22), analogously the Gospel –and essentially grace– must heal wounded human nature together with all its perverse consequences in the life of men. The Church, through her evangelizing mission, offers her contribution and collaborates in the purification and overcoming of all that is contrary to the dignity, freedom and fulfillment of the human person.

87. That is why “we must go to the culture and cultures of man not to be converted to them, but to heal and elevate them with the power of the Gospel, doing, analogously, what Christ did: ‘He suppressed what was diabolical, assumed what was human and communicated to it what is divine’”109.

88. On the one hand, “nothing authentically human must be rejected, since Christ assumed an integral human nature”110; but, on the other hand, we must always remember that “only what has dignity or necessity can be assumed. Neither the inhuman, nor the anti-human, nor the infra-human can be assumed. The irrational, the absurd and all their derivatives cannot be assumed”111.

89. Whatever has malice is not assumed –that is impossible–; on the contrary, it is redeemed, purified. The teaching of the Church on this point is very clear and constant. St. John Paul II taught that in the process of incarnating the Gospel message in cultures, referring specifically to the catechetical task, one must always remember that “the power of the Gospel everywhere transforms and regenerates. When that power enters into a culture, it is no surprise that it rectifies many of its elements. There would be no catechesis if it were the Gospel that had to change when it came into contact with the cultures.” And he goes on to list certain risks that follow from misunderstanding this incarnation of the Gospel in cultures, such as allowing catechesis to be impoverished “through a renunciation or obscuring of its message, by adaptations, even in language, that would endanger the precious deposit of the faith (cf. 2 Tim 1:14), or by concessions in matters of faith or morals”; on the contrary, “true catechesis eventually enriches these cultures by helping them to go beyond the defective or even inhuman features in them…”112.

90. The Second Vatican Council already taught: “The good news of Christ constantly renews the life and culture of fallen man; it combats and removes the errors and evils resulting from the permanent allurement of sin. It never ceases to purify and elevate the morality of peoples”113.

91. We can sum up this aspect of the purification of cultures with what St. John Paul II teaches in Pastores dabo vobis: “This obedience (to preach the Gospel to the whole world) does not mean syncretism, nor a simple adaptation of the Gospel proclamation; rather, the Gospel penetrates vitally into cultures, becomes incarnate in them, overcoming those cultural elements that are incompatible with the faith and with Christian living”114.

92. Without this task of purification, if “everything” were assumed without discerning values from what are not values, one would simply bring about a contradictory and pernicious syncretism, which would deprive cultures of the possibility of overcoming the defective or even inhuman features in them –the fruit of sin–, and which would at the same time prevent the transformation and regeneration of cultures through the communication of the fullness of Christ to their legitimately human values. In this way one would bring about what St. Paul calls emptying the cross of Christ (1 Cor 1:17).

Elevation and divinization

93. Lastly, in the third place, in this process of inculturation we have the task of the elevation or divinization of culture. Through inculturation an intimate transformation and renewal of cultures takes place115. That is, the Good News of Christ not only purifies but at the same time elevates and perfects the cultural values of peoples116. The Word took flesh and elevated human nature to the dignity of His one divine Person. In this way, by a certain analogy with the humanity of Christ, cultures are called, so to speak, to participate in the dignity of the divine Word117.

94. In this sense, the culture of man transcends the purely human plane, reaching the supernatural level that corresponds to the children of God. Human culture in relation to the Gospel is autonomous, but it is also theonomous; that is, it is assumed by the Gospel in order to be divinized, attaining at the same time its own human fullness. For Jesus Christ brings about the fullness of human existence, as Gaudium et spes teaches: “The truth is that only in the mystery of the Incarnate Word does the mystery of man take on light. Christ… fully reveals man to man himself and makes his supreme calling clear.” This vocation in Christ is a supernatural vocation, and all men without exception are called to participate in it: “Christ died for all, and the ultimate vocation of man is in fact one, and divine”118. Therefore, the Church, through inculturation, transmits the values proper to the Gospel, perfecting cultures and giving them a new impulse and new possibilities. The Good News of Christ enriches cultures, communicating to their legitimate values the “fullness of Christ”119. Christ, through grace, heals and elevates human nature; for their part, man and his cultures “tend” toward their fullness in Jesus Christ.

95. This can never be stressed enough. Men need Jesus Christ, for He alone grounds in all its fullness the good of man, authentic humanism. For every human being “is, in a certain sense, a stranger to himself. Jesus Christ not only reveals God, but fully reveals man to man himself”120. That is, the Son of God, by becoming man, has introduced into the history of humanity all the evangelical riches of truth, goodness and beauty, establishing at the same time concern for man and his destiny. The Incarnation constitutes “God's valuing of man”121.

96. For this reason the presence of the laity in the privileged places of culture “is intended not only for the recognition and eventual purification of the elements of the existing culture, critically weighed, but also for their elevation through the original riches of the Gospel and of the Christian faith”122. In this way “the good news of Christ constantly purifies and elevates the morality of peoples”123. The Gospel, on coming into contact with cultures, produces a transformation; it “becomes incarnate in them, overcoming those cultural elements that are incompatible with the faith and with Christian living and raising their values to the mystery of salvation which comes from Christ”124.

97. By way of summary, regarding the meaning of this single process of inculturation, we may conclude by quoting Gaudium et spes: “The Good News of Christ continually renews the life and culture of fallen man; it combats and removes the errors and evils resulting from the permanent allurement of sin. It never ceases to purify and elevate the morality of peoples. By riches coming from above, it makes fruitful, as it were from within, the spiritual qualities and traditions of every people of every age. It strengthens, perfects and restores them in Christ”125.

c) The organic bond between faith and cultures

98. We have dealt with the foundation and the process of inculturation, with its aims of assumption, purification and elevation of cultures. We now try to go a little more deeply into the bond between the Good News of Jesus Christ and the cultures of peoples, also in the light of the problems that arise today in the field of inculturation.

History of inculturation

99. The Church, in her evangelizing mission of incarnating the Gospel in the heart of cultures, enters into dialogue with them and establishes a vital communion that enriches cultures from within and, at the same time, enriches the Church through new and inspired expressions of the one mystery of Christ. In fact, this has been the historical path of the Church in her universal task of evangelizing all peoples.

100. It is enough just to review briefly the history of inculturation to verify the historical effort of the Church to incarnate the Gospel in the heart of cultures126.

101. We may recall the figure of St. Benedict (480-547), whose monasteries –through the cultivation of the fields and the copying of manuscripts– were the first cultural centers of Europe; he was declared patron of Europe by St. Paul VI in 1964127. Sts. Cyril and Methodius (885), evangelizers of the Slavic world, considering that in Great Moravia the Mass celebrated in the Roman rite was not very successful, translated the liturgy into the Slavic language, thereby inviting the people “to be aware of their national and cultural identity”128; St. John Paul II named them co-patrons of Europe and devoted his fourth Encyclical to them: Slavorum Apostoli. We may also recall the work of Matteo Ricci (1532-1610) in China, notable for his desire to respect the Chinese rites129. And the luminous example, in the work of evangelizing America, offered to us by the pastoral labor exercised by the Jesuits in the Guaraní reductions.

102. From the very beginning the Gospel was proclaimed to all peoples, and Christians lived their faith within the framework of their own culture, as the Letter to Diognetus testifies: “Christians are distinguished from other men neither by country, nor language, nor customs… their manner of life has nothing singular about it… they conform to local usages in dress, food and the rest of life, while at the same time displaying the extraordinary and truly paradoxical laws of their way of living.” They live, therefore, according to the customs of their own cultures, but elevated by the divine power of the Gospel: “they are in the flesh, but they do not live according to the flesh. They pass their days on earth, but they are citizens of heaven. They obey the established laws, and their way of living surpasses the laws”130.

103. Pope Alexander VII (1659), giving directives to the first missionaries in Asia, urged them: “do not make any attempt, and do not in any way seek to persuade those peoples to change their customs, their way of life, their usages, when they are not manifestly contrary to religion and morals. There is nothing more absurd than to want to bring France, or Spain, or Italy, or any other part of Europe into China. Bring none of that, but the faith, a faith that does not reject or offend the way of life and usages of any people, when it is not a question of evil things. On the contrary, the faith wants these things to be preserved and protected”131.

104. This is, in a special way, the thought of the Church in the twentieth century, which has been progressively clarified. Benedict XV, in Maximum illud (30/11/1919), cautions that in evangelization the characteristics of each people be taken into account, that a native clergy be established capable of understanding the inner life of the peoples, and, for the same reason, that foreign clergy know the languages of the peoples to be evangelized. Pius XI, in Rerum Ecclesiae (28/2/1926)132, insists on the formation of a native clergy. For the mission has as its purpose “to found and naturalize the Church of Jesus Christ in those beloved regions,” and the native clergy better understands the soul of its people, their traditions, customs and language. Pius XII, in Evangelii praecones (2/6/1951), exhorts missionaries to acquire the formation necessary to understand the country where they are going to work, including medicine, agriculture, history, ethnography and geography; he also insists on a proper hierarchy and the formation of a native clergy, and says that “it is necessary to follow the very prudent norm that, once peoples embrace the Gospel, nothing should be ruined or destroyed of what is good, honest and beautiful in their character and their own genius.” Better still, such customs –as he likewise affirms in Summi Pontificatus (20/10/1939)–, if they are not indissolubly bound up with superstition or errors, should as far as possible be “preserved intact”133.

Gospel and evangelized cultures

105. Nevertheless, at present some insist on emphasizing that in the past the evangelizing mission of the Church did not always respect the cultural and religious values of the evangelized peoples and that, on some occasions, it even imposed a sort of “cultural-religious colonialism.” Certainly, in the task of bringing the Gospel to all cultures, one cannot simply impose a particular culture on the peoples being evangelized. For the Gospel is one reality and the culture in which it becomes incarnate is another. No culture is essential to the Gospel, as we have already pointed out: “the Church does not identify herself with any culture, not even with Western culture, notwithstanding that she has been bound to it by history”134.

106. What is more, evangelization must incarnate the Gospel in the culture and “take as its starting point, with prudence and discernment, elements –religious or otherwise– that form part of the cultural patrimony of a human group, in order to help people to understand better the integrity of the Christian mystery”135.

107. However, this does not mean that in this task of inculturation the Gospel message must simply be completely isolated from the cultures in which it has historically been inserted and expressed. St. John Paul II says in this regard: “the Gospel message cannot be purely and simply isolated from the culture in which it was first inserted (the biblical world or, more concretely, the cultural milieu in which Jesus of Nazareth lived), nor, without serious loss, from the cultures in which it has already been expressed down through the centuries”136. Therefore, one must discern and appreciate the values of the cultures that are being evangelized; but, at the same time, one must preserve and duly value those cultural milieux in which the Gospel has in fact been inserted and expressed from its very beginnings. “This message does not spring spontaneously from any cultural ‘humus’; it has always been transmitted by means of an apostolic dialogue which inevitably becomes part of a certain dialogue of cultures”137.

108. We find an example of this in the evangelizing work of Sts. Cyril and Methodius, who carried out a true inculturation or “Incarnation of the Gospel in native cultures.” For “by creating, in an original and inspired way, an alphabet for the Slavic language, they made a fundamental contribution to the culture and literature of all the Slavic nations.” But “the brothers from Thessalonica were heirs not only of the faith but also of the culture of ancient Greece, continued by Byzantium.” The Holy Father, in saying this, refers not only to the verification of a fact but, above all, to the positive character of it: “everyone knows the importance of this heritage for the whole of European culture and, directly or indirectly, for universal culture”138.

109. Therefore, the Church cannot simply isolate or leave behind all that she has already acquired so brilliantly through the inculturation of her Gospel message in Greco-Latin culture and in other cultures, “without serious loss”139. This principle is also valid for the Church of tomorrow, which will be enriched by new contributions arising from her contact with other cultures in which the Gospel has not yet become incarnate. That is, the evangelizing mission first encountered Greek Philosophy, but this does not exclude other approaches. New tasks continually open up for inculturation140.

Some difficulties

110. We have already pointed out some difficulties relating to the dialogue between Gospel and cultures. But, more profoundly, in this field serious objections arise today. In this sense, some even postulate the need for a revision of the contents concerning the definitive character and fullness of divine revelation in Christ and the unique and universal value of His saving mediation, which would allow the recognition of a plurality of saving mediations parallel to and autonomous from that of Christ, while being complementary to one another. At the basis of this position there is evident the cultural orientation of relativism that prevails today in the West141, and the philosophical position of “historicism” regarding the question of truth.

111. Consequently, these authors maintain that no historical religion –Christianity included– can receive and express fully and definitively the truth or manifestation of God. In the same sense, no particular historical event –including that of Christ– can claim to express in principle a unique and universal saving content. Christ, as a historical particular, would have a necessarily contingent and limited significance. To justify this position, some attempt to introduce a separation –contrary to the faith– between Jesus and the Word of God142.

112. In this same perspective, with respect to cultures, the legitimate claim to the specificity and originality of the thought of a cultural heritage is asserted –for example, Hinduism, or the great cultures of China, etc.–, while at the same time maintaining that each cultural tradition must shut itself up within its own difference143.

113. As a consequence, there is an attempt to reduce the mission of the Church to a task solely of the temporal order and to a dialogue consisting in an exchange of opinions –without the intention of going deeper and attaining a common and universal truth, which would be impossible!–, in opposition to the proclamation of the Gospel.

114. In relation to these serious difficulties, St. Paul VI already pointed out in Evangelii nuntiandi that “many, even generous Christians who are sensitive to the dramatic questions involved in the problem of liberation, in their wish to commit the Church to the liberation effort are frequently tempted to reduce her mission to the dimensions of a simply temporal project. They would reduce her aims to a man-centered goal; the salvation of which she is the messenger would be reduced to material well-being. Her activity, forgetful of all spiritual and religious preoccupation, would become initiatives of the political or social order. But if this were so, the Church would lose her fundamental meaning. That is why we wished to emphasize, in the same address at the opening of the Synod, ‘the need to restate clearly the specifically religious finality of evangelization. This latter would lose its reason for existence if it were to diverge from the religious axis that guides it: the kingdom of God, before anything else, in its fully theological meaning’”144.

115. St. John Paul II, in this regard, in his magnificent Encyclical Redemptoris missio, presents with complete clarity the principal points of the present problematic surrounding the evangelizing mission of the Church, especially in the field of inculturation and interreligious dialogue, also indicating the principles for its solution. “Nevertheless, also as a result of the modern changes and the spread of new theological ideas, some people ask: Is missionary work among non-Christians still relevant? Has it not been replaced by interreligious dialogue? Is not human development an adequate goal of the Church's mission? Does not respect for conscience and for freedom exclude all efforts at conversion? Is it not possible to attain salvation in any religion? Why then should there be missionary activity?”145.

116. He shows, furthermore, that at the basis of this problematic there emerge certain incorrect theological views that maintain the salvific equality of religions146 and that therefore affect authentic faith in Christ, the only Savior, with all its negative consequences for the whole of missionary activity147.

117. Therefore, an undifferentiated cultural and religious pluralism is proposed, which places all positions on the same level. But in this perspective every possibility of establishing a dialogue as a search for truth is closed off. That is, one accepts the contradiction of identifying the essence of cultural and religious values with those that are not values, given the impossibility of verifying and distinguishing in a culture and in a religion what is positive –elements of truth and goodness in an objective and universal sense– from what is, on the contrary, negative, that is, error and superstition, the fruit of sin.

118. Concretely, from this perspective, in relation to inculturation, the path to establishing a dialogue between Gospel and cultures would be closed. For it would not be possible to discern the authentic values present in cultures and religions from those elements that are, on the contrary, the fruit of sin. That is, the different cultural forms would always constitute –in principle– “valid” and at the same time “contingent” expressions, that is, expressions valid148 for their own cultures in a given context, but not in a universal sense. Therefore, cultures would remain in principle closed to dialogue –understood as a search for truth– with other cultures and closed to the Gospel. Moreover, it would be unnecessary and useless to offer a Gospel that is valid for “Christian cultures” but foreign to other cultures, and moreover contingent, since its revelation would not be definitive and the salvation it offers not universal. As a result, the Church would have to limit herself to a dialogue –set in opposition to proclamation– that tends to be identified in practice with a human development reduced to the merely horizontal dimension of human life. Therefore, in the field of the task of inculturation –and of interreligious dialogue– it is urgent to renew awareness of the universal character of the content of the faith, in particular of faith in Jesus Christ, the “only” Savior, which lies at the basis of the evangelizing mission149.

119. Cultures, for their part, also contain a universal value in their different expressions “proper” to “objective and universal truth.” Cultures manifest in themselves the typical openness of man to the universal and to transcendence, and in this sense they “tend” toward their fulfillment. Therefore, it is “possible” for cultures to receive divine revelation. Cultures, in their objective elements of truth, of goodness, of groping search for the unknown God, “tend” toward their perfection in Jesus Christ150.

120. St. Paul VI affirmed in Evangelii nuntiandi that “only a Church which preserves the awareness of her universality and shows that she is in fact universal is capable of having a message which can be heard by all, regardless of regional frontiers”151.

121. In this perspective of universality, St. John Paul II indicates the criteria to be followed in inculturation: a) The universality of the human spirit, whose basic needs and aspirations are the same in the most disparate cultures. b) When the Church enters into contact with great cultures not yet evangelized, she cannot leave behind what she has gained from her inculturation in the world of Greco-Latin thought. This criterion is valid also for the Church of the future. c) In the third place, one must not confuse the legitimate defense of the specificity of a cultural tradition with the idea that it must shut itself up within its difference and affirm itself by opposing other traditions, which is contrary to the very nature of the human spirit152.

122. [Conclusion] Gospel and culture are called to a fruitful communion, which allows culture to attain its authentically human fullness in Jesus Christ. Between them “there exists an organic and constitutive link”153, to which Christian Europe bears special witness154. Therefore, although a culture is not the criterion of truth with regard to the revelation of God, nevertheless the Gospel is not contrary to any culture. On the contrary, the Gospel proclamation leads cultures to their real liberation and to full truth.

123. In the process of inculturation, the Church transmits her truths and values, renewing cultures from within, while also drawing from them the positive elements already present. In this way, the Church brings about “an intimate transformation of authentic cultural values through their integration in Christianity and the insertion of Christianity in the various human cultures”155. In turn, the particular churches will be able to express and translate their own Christian experience and the treasure of the faith in an original way, in accordance with their own cultural traditions and in the legitimate variety of their expressions156. That is, the various cultures, when purified and renewed in the light of the Gospel, can become true expressions of the one Christian faith.

124. In this way, the Church establishes a fruitful dialogue with the cultural and religious values of the different peoples. When she knows and understands these various aspects of culture, she can begin the dialogue of salvation, offering the Good News of the Redemption157.

Orientations of present-day culture

125. We have considered the essential aspects of culture and of the meaning of its evangelization. We now go on to reflect on the principal characteristics of present-day culture.

126.[Introduction] In order to evangelize the culture it is indispensable to know present-day culture sufficiently in its major lines of thought and orientations, in order to discern its positive aspects and also its shadows and imbalances.

127. All the more so in a world that has undergone profound changes, as Gaudium et spes observes: “the living conditions of modern man have been so profoundly changed in their social and cultural aspects, that we can speak of a new age in human history”158. In the same sense Pope St. John Paul II, in Dives in misericordia, pointed out that our generation “is aware of the approach of the third millennium and profoundly feels the change that is occurring in history”159.

128. What is happening in the world is also experienced –in another order– in the Church, which is inserted in it. There is “such an upheaval of religious and social situations that it is difficult to apply concretely certain ecclesial distinctions and categories to which we had grown accustomed. Even before the Council it was said that some Christian cities and countries had become mission territories. The situation has certainly not improved in the years since”160.

129. Present-day culture has certain characteristics that we can describe following the Magisterium of the Second Vatican Council and of the recent Popes.

1. Positive aspects

130. There has been a true “growth of the natural and human sciences, including the social sciences,” as well as “progress in technology” and “advances in the use and organization of the means of communication”161. The heritage of knowledge and wisdom has been enriched in various fields: Logic, the Philosophy of language, Epistemology, the Philosophy of nature, Anthropology, the in-depth analysis of the affective ways of knowledge, the existential approach to the analysis of freedom162. So much so that man, through his intellect and work, has “brought about profound changes, both in the domain of science and technology and in social and cultural life. Man has extended his power over nature; he has acquired a deeper knowledge of the laws of his social behavior”163.

131. This progress has benefited man with a series of advantages. The great advances of science and technology have succeeded in extending man's power over nature and in providing new material goods164. But not only new material goods, for, for example, the growth of the positive sciences “sharpens critical judgment to the utmost, and studies in psychology explain human activity more profoundly”165. That is, all these new acquisitions of the human sciences –biological, psychological or social– will help “man to penetrate better into the riches of his own being”166. These advances offer the men of our time new opportunities for education, “above all through the wider diffusion of books and of the new instruments of cultural and social communication”167. In particular, the development of information technology “will allow access to the intellectual and cultural riches of other peoples. The new communication techniques will foster greater participation in events and a growing exchange of ideas”168. These new conditions of life and progress, characteristic of present-day culture, favor a more universal culture, as the Second Vatican Council foresaw: “a more universal form of human culture is being prepared, which will be all the more apt to foster and express the unity of the human race the more it respects the particularities of each cultural unit”169. A more universal culture that can collaborate in the creation of a culture of greater “solidarity”: “The present generation feels privileged because progress offers it so many possibilities, undreamed of only a few decades ago. […] It has seen the obstacles and distances that separate men and nations collapse or lessen, through an increased sense of the universal, through a clearer awareness of the unity of the human race, through the acceptance of mutual dependence within an authentic solidarity, and finally through the desire –and the possibility– to come into contact with its brothers and sisters beyond the artificial divisions of geography or national or racial frontiers”170.

132. There is, then, at present a growing sense of solidarity among peoples171, as Benedict XVI describes it in the Encyclical Deus caritas est: “Today the means of mass communication have made our planet smaller, rapidly narrowing the distance between different peoples and cultures. This ‘togetherness’ at times gives rise to misunderstandings and tensions, yet our ability to know almost instantly about the needs of others challenges us to share their situation and their difficulties. […] At the same time, one notes that there are innumerable means today of offering humanitarian assistance to our brothers and sisters in need, not least in modern systems of distributing food and clothing, and of providing housing and care. Concern for our neighbor transcends the confines of national communities and has increasingly broadened its horizon to the whole world”172.

133. In summary, the Church recognizes and gladly welcomes the authentic values of the culture of our time, which we can sum up in the enormous advances of scientific knowledge and technological development, and in a new awareness of human rights, of religious freedom and of solidarity.

2. Shadows and imbalances

134. But alongside these positive aspects, there also exist great shadows and imbalances in the culture of our time, which we must identify, uniting our efforts in order to overcome them. Imbalances that are connected with that other fundamental imbalance rooted in the human heart173, and that are related to “the profound response that man knows he must give”174. That is, there is a profound imbalance between the very rapid growth of technology and the much slower growth of moral resources.

135. We must not forget that all human life is marked by sin, as “an integral part of the truth about man”175. St. John Paul II pointed out that “the panorama of the contemporary world also presents shadows and imbalances that are not always superficial”176 and, referring to the statement of Gaudium et spes about “the imbalances under which the modern world labors”177, he asked himself: “In the course of these fifteen years since the conclusion of the Second Vatican Council, has that picture of tensions and threats that mark our epoch become less disquieting? It seems not. On the contrary, the tensions and threats that in the Council document seemed only to be outlined and not to manifest in depth all the danger hidden within them have revealed themselves more fully in the space of these years; they have in a different way confirmed that danger, and do not permit us to cherish the illusions of the past”178.

136. There are in present-day culture multiple antinomies that require a solution:

a) The increase of exchange between cultures, which in itself can favor a fruitful dialogue, also contains the possibility of disturbing the life of communities –and in fact it sometimes gives rise to misunderstandings and tensions–, of overthrowing traditional wisdom and of endangering the character proper to peoples179.

b) The culture that arises from the progress of science and technology, and from the ever more fragmented study of particular disciplines, carries with it the risk of not harmonizing with the cultivation of the spirit that is nourished by classical studies according to the different traditions, and with the need to arrive at a synthesis of them180, preserving the faculties of contemplation and wonder that lead to wisdom181.

Furthermore, the one-sided development of the sciences and of technology, which by themselves are “incapable of penetrating to the intimate essence of things, when their method is unreasonably considered the supreme norm for the search for truth, fosters a certain phenomenalism and agnosticism; and, together with this, when man trusts ‘too much’ in these kinds of discoveries, he runs the danger of believing himself ‘self-sufficient’ and of no longer seeking higher realities”182.

c) Hence the third antinomy, which goes to the heart of the modern drama: “the legitimate autonomy of culture may seek to extend itself into the undue and harmful claim of a purely earthly humanism, and even one opposed to religion”183.

137. In this sense, St. John Paul II describes secularism as “a movement of ideas and customs which advocates a humanism totally without God, completely centered upon the cult of action and production and caught up in the heady enthusiasm of consumerism and pleasure”184.

138. Consequently –and this aspect must be emphasized among the shadows and challenges of our age– there is the phenomenon of a true eclipse of conscience. “It is a matter of a true overturning or collapse of moral values, and the problem is not only one of ignorance of Christian ethics, but rather of the sense of the foundations and criteria of the moral attitude”185. We are witnessing, in this sense, “the decline of so many fundamental values that constitute an unquestionable good not only of Christian morality but simply of human morality, of moral culture, such as respect for human life from the moment of conception, respect for marriage in its indissoluble unity, respect for the stability of the family. […] Linked to this are the crisis of truth in human relationships, the lack of responsibility in speech, the purely utilitarian relationship of man with man, the diminished sense of the authentic common good and the ease with which it is alienated. Finally, there is the desacralization that at times turns into ‘dehumanization’: the man and the society for whom nothing is ‘sacred’ are in moral decline, despite appearances”186.

139. This dehumanization is experienced above all in the multiplication and intensification of threats to the life of persons and of peoples, with the new prospects opened up by scientific and technological progress, and in a new cultural situation that justifies attacks on human life in the name of the rights of individual freedom, even obtaining the authorization of the State and the cooperation of the health-care system. With the dramatic result of the elimination of so many human lives at their beginning or close to their end, and the “no less serious” fact that conscience itself finds it ever more difficult to perceive the distinction between good and evil in what concerns the very fundamental value of human life187.

140. Benedict XVI describes in a few strokes the central points and the dangers contained in the culture predominant in the West: “From this there derives a new wave of enlightenment and secularism, according to which only what can be experienced and calculated would be rationally valid, while in practice individual freedom is set up as the fundamental value to which all others must be subjected. Thus God is excluded from culture and from public life, and faith in Him becomes more difficult, among other reasons because we live in a world that almost always presents itself as our own work, in which, so to speak, God no longer appears directly; He seems already superfluous, indeed foreign. In close connection with all this, there takes place a radical reduction of man, considered a mere product of nature, as such not really free, and who in himself may be treated like any other animal. Thus there occurs a genuine overturning of the starting point of this culture, which was a claim to the centrality of man and his freedom. Along the same lines, ethics is placed within the confines of relativism and utilitarianism, excluding any moral principle that is valid and binding in itself. […] Furthermore, the insufficiency of a rationality closed in on itself and of an overly individualistic ethics is felt ever more clearly; concretely, the seriousness of the danger of being cut off from the Christian roots of our civilization is perceived”188.

3. Atheism

141. The fundamental concern regarding the dark side of present-day culture, profoundly evident, and the root of the problem, emerges in the sphere of contemporary nihilism-atheism. “How can we fail to think of the persistent spread of religious indifference and atheism in its more varied forms, particularly in its perhaps most widespread form of secularism? Inebriated by the prodigious conquests of a continuous scientific-technological development and fascinated above all by the oldest and ever new temptation of wanting to become like God (cf. Gen 3:5) through the use of a liberty without bounds, man cuts the religious roots that are in his heart: he forgets God, or considers Him without significance for his own existence, or rejects Him, putting himself to adore the most diverse ‘idols’”189.

142. “The present-day phenomenon of secularism is truly serious; and it affects not only individuals, but in some way entire communities, as the Council already observed: ‘Growing numbers of people are abandoning religion in practice.’ At other times I myself have recalled the phenomenon of de-Christianization which strikes long-standing Christian peoples and which continually calls for a re-evangelization”190.

143. This entails serious consequences for man. For the reality we experience is not the absolute; God alone is the absolute; therefore, every illusion of autonomy that ignores essential dependence on God leads to dramatic situations that destroy the meaning of human existence191. In this sense, “when not acknowledged and loved in his dignity as the living image of God (cf. Gen 1:26), the human being is exposed to more humiliating and degrading forms of ‘manipulation’ that most assuredly reduce him to a slave of the strongest. And ‘the strongest’ can take the names of an ideology, economic power, political and inhumane systems, scientific technocracy or the intrusiveness of the mass media. Once again we find ourselves before many persons, our sisters and brothers, whose fundamental rights are being violated, owing also to the excessive tolerance and even the patent injustice of certain civil laws: the right to life and to integrity, the right to a home and to work, the right to a family and responsible parenthood, the right to participation in public and political life, the right to freedom of conscience and the practice of religion”192.

144. We are, once again, before “the drama of man of all times, which is also the drama of man today: ‘his Babel-like character,’ that is, opting for the merely horizontal dimension of work and social life, paying no attention to the vertical dimension by which they would have found themselves rooted in God, their Creator and Lord, and oriented toward Him as the ultimate end of their journey”193.

If we try to discover the philosophical root of the great present-day problems concerning man, we can find it in the common horizon of the nihilist position. Modern philosophy has concentrated its search on human knowledge, highlighting its limits and conditioning, and leaving aside the radical question of the truth about the person, about being and about God194. Modern philosophical thought gradually distanced itself from revelation and explicitly opposed it. Different forms of atheistic humanism arose that presented faith as harmful and alienating. In scientific research a positivist mentality has gradually prevailed that forgets every relation to the metaphysical and moral vision; nihilism, with the primacy of the ephemeral, has gained ground195. The nihilist position seems to constitute the common horizon of many philosophies that have strayed from the sense of being. This forgetfulness of being inevitably entails the loss of contact with objective truth and, consequently, with the foundation on which the dignity of man rests. The humanity of man and his identity are denied196.

145. In summary, certain currents of thought related to “postmodernity” maintain that the time of certainties has irremediably passed; man would have to learn to live in a perspective of total absence of meaning, characterized by the provisional and fleeting197. In summary, the root is evident in the present philosophical situation, in its forgetfulness of being and of the search for an objective truth –the foundation on which the dignity of man rests–, in a perspective of total absence of meaning and of affirmation of the ephemeral, with all its tragic consequences.

146. This is what constitutes contemporary nihilism-atheism198. Nihilism, or radical atheism, as the denial of the transcendent, of the existence of “a metaphysical world that transcends the sensible world”199. Modern philosophy, under the influence of Hegel, with the –contradictory and dialectical– identification of being and non-being, has “forgotten being as foundation and has brought about the collapse of speculative thought, to such a point that one cannot even speak of weak thought… because to anchor oneself in nothingness is not to think: nothingness, in fact, is the absence of everything, total privation, irrecoverable flight, irremediable defeat”200.

147. In “this dialectical interchange… of being and non-being, contemporary nihilism-atheism stands at the door as the point of arrival; although one should rather speak of a blocking of the spirit within itself and of a slipping or closing-in of thought because of the unstoppable advance of nothingness. The human spirit, closed in on itself, no longer opens itself to the real –to the act of being– and, therefore, to God, and so it becomes incapable of expressing the truth and of grounding freedom”201.

Evangelization of cultures and our specific end

148. Our Religious Family has as its specific end “to inculturate the Gospel”202, the singular end of “dedicating ourselves to the evangelization of culture”203. Inculturation is an intrinsic requirement of evangelization, but in present circumstances it takes on, in addition, a special urgency, for present-day culture, together with its positive aspects, presents at the same time profound imbalances. This culture has progressively distanced itself from and set itself against the Gospel. In this sense, the Second Vatican Council points out that “the split between the faith which many profess and their daily lives deserves to be counted among the more serious errors of our age”204. In the same way, Evangelii nuntiandi affirms that “the split between the Gospel and culture is without a doubt the drama of our time, just as it was of other times”205. Therefore, since we find ourselves “before the development of a culture that is taking shape as cut off not only from the Christian faith but even from human values themselves, as well as before a certain scientific and technological culture powerless to respond to the pressing demand for truth and goodness that burns in the hearts of men, the Church is fully aware of the pastoral urgency of reserving a very special attention for culture”206. “This is a requirement that has marked her whole historical journey, but today it is particularly acute and urgent”207. We must, then, “make every effort with a view to a generous evangelization of culture, or more correctly of cultures”208.

149. Put briefly: faith must become culture. That is, faith must become incarnate in the life and culture of men, for otherwise, in the case of a faith that does not become culture, it must be said that “it is a faith ‘not fully received, not thoroughly thought through, not faithfully lived’”209.

For this task of inculturation, on the one hand, we have the advantages offered to us by present-day culture, which we must take advantage of as a starting point in order to build from there a new culture purified and elevated by the Gospel; on the other hand, there appear alongside this –or better still, within this very thing– shadows and profound imbalances that cause well-founded concern and that challenge us to offer positive and long-term solutions. The Church must respond, above all, with an evangelization –or, as the case may be, a new evangelization– that reaches the heart of cultures in order to transform them in Jesus Christ. Our Institute must also collaborate in this work of the Church, all the more so as it has as its specific end the evangelization of cultures.

150. We now go on to consider, in the first place, what the cultural contribution of our Institute must be according to our charism and specific end. In the second place, we shall reflect on our proper apostolates and the selection of ministries with a view to a more effective inculturation.

1. The contribution of religious life in relation to our specific end

151. [Introduction] In the field of inculturation new answers must be worked out for the new problems of today's world, acting with boldness in the respective fields of one's own founding charism210. If we try to point out some characteristics of our apostolates in their relationship with the charism and specific end of our Institute, perhaps the first thing that appears is their breadth and variety, which is in conformity with our specific end of evangelizing the culture. For everything authentically human –technology, art, intellectual and moral life, the contemplation of divine things– forms part of culture and, therefore, can and must be evangelized, since “no human activity is foreign to the Gospel”211. The evangelization of culture consists in “prolonging the Incarnation in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man”212, in imbuing all human activity with the Gospel213. Therefore, everything that concerns man, both in his body and in his soul, in his individual and also in his social life, can and must be purified and elevated by the grace of Christ; and consequently we can affirm that every form of apostolic activity is in conformity with our specific end, although in a hierarchical way.

152. In this sense, our Constitutions mention a great variety of apostolates, such as the preaching of the Spiritual Exercises and spiritual direction214, priestly formation in seminaries, education at all levels, the formation of lay leaders, the means of social communication215, help to parishes through preaching and the administration of the sacrament of Reconciliation, popular missions in all their forms, the running of parishes especially in mission areas216, the concrete practice of charity217, and the care of the Second and Third Order218. Our Constitutions also point out our charism of prolonging Christ in “families, in education, in the media, in men of thought and in every other legitimate manifestation of the life of man”219.

153. Analogously, the plurality of our Institutes –the First, Second and Third Order, with their active, contemplative and lay branches in their three levels of consecration– harmonizes perfectly with our specific end, inasmuch as they constitute different calls, according to the vocation of each one, to collaborate in the evangelization of culture, which is a work of all the members of the Church.

a) Specific cultural contribution of consecrated life

154. More specifically, it must be affirmed that consecrated life itself, when it is lived authentically, can offer an original contribution to the challenges of inculturation. The evangelical style of life is an important source for proposing a new cultural model220.

155. [Witness of charity] This original contribution is specified, in the first place, in the sphere of the promotion of charity through the preferential option for those who find themselves in a situation of greater weakness and, therefore, of more serious need. That is, it is specified in the sphere of charity toward the poor, which leads one to live like them221; in the care of the sick and of those who suffer, contributing significantly to the mission of the Church222.

156. For this reason, in the variety of apostolates of our Institutes a preferential place must be reserved for charitable work, which is an essential component of the evangelizing mission of the Church and an indispensable element for the evangelization of culture. “Likewise, priority must be given to the care of the poor, the sick and the needy of every kind: the love of Christ urges us (2 Cor 5:14), concretely practicing charity as a witness […] that charity is indispensable for evangelizing the culture, as the end of the one who acts and as the end of the work. Otherwise, ‘the civilization of love’ will not be attained”223. For this reason too, “those who devote themselves to the works of mercy are the key pieces of the apostolic endeavor of our Institute”224. Benedict XVI describes the path of evangelization in the first centuries, saying: “The strong union between faith that is friendly to the intelligence and a practice of life characterized by mutual love and attentive care for the poor and suffering made possible the first great missionary expansion of Christianity in the Hellenistic-Roman world”; and this “remains the royal road for evangelization”225.

157. [Witness to the primacy of God] In the second place, this cultural contribution of consecrated life is specified in its prophetic witness to the primacy of God and of Gospel values226.

More concretely, the profession of chastity, poverty and obedience, while affirming the value of created goods, relativizes them, presenting God as the absolute good. In this way, they present a spiritual therapy for humanity, for they reject the idolatry of creatures and make the living God visible227. In this there are clearly manifested both the eminently cultural character and contribution that consecrated life offers and the consistency of its response to present-day cultural challenges. For the transcendent dimension of human life, openness to and approach toward the mystery of God, constitutes the “heart” of every culture. In this way, it is also understood why the consecrated person is always “on mission.”

  1. This witness to the primacy of God as the only absolute good, through an authentic religious life faithful to its commitments, constitutes the principal apostolate of religious, also and in a particular way in the task of inculturation. Our Directory of Spirituality says: “we want to base our spirituality on the absoluteness of God, before whom everything is as nothing. […] We want in everything and through everything to give primacy to the spiritual and to give ourselves in holy abandonment to the will of God's good pleasure”228.
  2. In this sense, the branches of contemplative life give eloquent witness to this principal dimension of human culture, a witness therefore particularly necessary in the work of the evangelization of cultures, as can be verified historically, for example, in the decisive collaboration the Benedictine monks offered in the building of European culture. St. Paul VI pointed out that “the Church and the world… need… a small ideal society where there reign, as their end, love, obedience, innocence, freedom from things and the art of using them well, the prevalence of the spirit, peace, in a word, the Gospel”229.

b) Need for ongoing formation

160. On the other hand, since the witness to the primacy of God and of charity, through an authentic consecrated life, constitutes the first apostolate of consecrated persons and their principal contribution to the evangelization of cultures, it is fundamental to preserve the quality of this witness, giving the greatest importance to the periods of formation and to ongoing formation in its different dimensions. Our Directory of Spirituality says: “No less weight for our spirituality has the example of Jesus in His hidden life. Hence every time of preparation must be very important for us […] the future of our brothers in religious life and, therefore, of the Institute depends fundamentally on the formation given to them in the times of preparation: postulancy, novitiate, seminary, subsequent further training, ongoing formation”230. Our Directory also emphasizes that our apostolic commitment to evangelize the culture requires of us a spirituality with particular nuances, which consists, as St. John Paul II teaches, in “a faith enlightened by continual reflection that measures itself against the sources of the Church's message, and a constant spiritual discernment pursued in prayer”231.

That is, it requires ongoing spiritual and cultural formation, capable of sustaining the quality of the witness of religious life, and also a pastoral care of culture that can respond positively and convincingly to the great challenges of present-day culture.

161. In this sense, consecrated life, as St. John Paul II emphasizes, needs within itself a renewed love for cultural commitment. A decline in concern for study can have serious consequences in the apostolate as well, generating a sense of marginalization and inferiority, or fostering superficiality and rashness in initiatives232. This cultural commitment appears even more urgent and necessary if one considers our specific end of the evangelization of culture.

162. Furthermore, the task of inculturation also requires constant formation in the spiritual dimension, for it is precisely the imitation of Christ's attitude of humility and love in the mystery of the Incarnation that fosters in consecrated souls the best dispositions for the difficult and urgent task of inculturation: “Relying on the charism of their founders and foundresses, many consecrated persons have been able to approach different cultures with the attitude of Jesus, who emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant (Phil 2:7), and, with a bold and patient effort of dialogue, they have established fruitful contacts with the most diverse peoples, proclaiming to all the way of salvation. […] A genuine inculturation requires an attitude like that of the Lord, who when He became incarnate came among us with love and humility. In this sense consecrated life prepares persons to face the complex and arduous task of inculturation, because it accustoms them to detachment from things, including many aspects of their own culture. Applying themselves with these attitudes to the study and understanding of cultures, consecrated persons can better discern in them the authentic values and the way in which they can be accepted and perfected, with the help of their own charism”233.

c) Key pieces in our apostolic endeavor

163. We must also mention those members of our Institutes –“the sick and the elderly –together with the members of the contemplative branches–, the coadjutor brothers and those who devote themselves to the works of mercy”– who “are the key pieces of the apostolic endeavor of our Institute”234, precisely because of their greater participation in and imitation of the radical self-emptying of the Word of God, who out of love for man became a servant obedient unto death on the Cross in order to give His life for many (cf. Mt 20:28), and in this way transfigure the world.

164. [Laity] Finally, since “inculturation must involve the whole people of God, and not just a few experts”235, we point out the specific contribution of the lay branches to the evangelization of cultures.

The laity, whose proper and particular characteristic is their secular character, which is rooted in the mystery of the Incarnate Word, evangelize the culture through their own vocation “to seek the Kingdom of God by engaging in temporal affairs and ordering them according to the plan of God”236. They live “in each and in all of the secular professions and occupations, and in the ordinary circumstances of family and social life, being led by the spirit of the Gospel in all of this so that, from within, like leaven, they may contribute to the sanctification of the world, and in this way, radiating faith, hope and charity, above all by the witness of their life, they may make Christ known to others”237. Therefore: “in their existence there cannot be two parallel lives: on the one hand, the so-called ‘spiritual’ life, with its values and demands; and on the other, the so-called ‘secular’ life, that is, life in a family, at work, in social relationships, in the responsibilities of public life and in culture. Every activity, every situation, every concrete endeavor –as, for example, skill and solidarity in work, love and dedication in the family and the education of children, service to society and public life, and the promotion of truth in the area of culture– are the occasions ordained by Providence for a ‘continuous exercise of faith, hope and charity’”238.

165. In a particular way, they will collaborate, in a common work, with the apostolate carried out by the priests and the men and women religious of the Family of the Incarnate Word.

166. Let us remember that in order to carry out our apostolate “we wish to achieve the most filial and fraternal relationship with the Pontifical Council for Culture, so as to achieve a greater and better concrete realization of the purpose of the Institute”239.

2. Turning points of culture

167. We have already pointed out the characteristic plurality and variety of our apostolates, in conformity with our specific end of evangelizing the culture. But, at the same time, one must emphasize the need to make a selection of ministries, working principally at the turning points of culture.

168. In order to offer our contribution to the Church's mission of inculturating the Gospel, one can never insist enough on the priority of the life of the spirit and of the qualified witness of religious life, as well as on the concrete practice of charity and on the need to carry out our proper apostolates with discernment and competence, working especially in the places of greatest cultural influence. Our Constitutions say: “The Institute of the Incarnate Word will take on the apostolates most conducive to the inculturation of the Gospel”240.

169. St. Thomas teaches that “acts that have as their object the spiritual good of the soul are more useful to one's neighbor than those ordered to bodily good, and are a greater service of God, which is why no sacrifice is more pleasing than zeal for souls”241.

170. All the more so when that spiritual good reaches many: “it is better to teach sacred doctrine, and more meritorious, if it is done with a good intention, than to attend to the particular care of the salvation of this or that person… the same reason shows that it is better to teach the things that pertain to salvation to those who can profit both for themselves and for others than to the simple, who can profit only for themselves”242. For this reason, “those who devote themselves to theological, philosophical, scientific, cultural research, etc., must be very clear that, although it may seem more remote, this intellectual work is not only for the greater glory of God, but also for the greater good of souls, and falls fully within the charism of our Institute”243. In this field of the intellectual apostolate, emphasis must be placed above all on “publications, since what is written endures and spreads further”244.

171. Concretely, one must “work on the turning points of culture, namely: families, education –especially seminary, university and tertiary education–, the means of social communication and men of thought or ‘intellectuals’”245.

172. The turning points of culture are the milieux and places existing in society in which culture has its proximate source –where culture is “created”– and through which culture is transmitted in a privileged way, whether in an integral and profound manner, for example in the family and in the field of education –especially university education and research settings–, or in a massive manner, as for example in the modern means of communication.

173. It is these turning points that we must give priority to in our work of incarnating the Gospel in modern societies, since “concretely, it is only from within and through culture that the Christian faith becomes a part of history and the creator of history. Therefore, it is necessary to intensify the presence of the Church and of the laity in the privileged places of culture, such as the world of schools and universities, the settings of scientific and technical research, the places of artistic creativity and of humanistic reflection. Also in the instruments of social communication, which today constitute the privileged path for the creation and transmission of culture”246. We now go on, then, to consider the nature and importance of the turning points of culture.

a) The family

174. God is the creator of the family –the original cell of society–, to which He has given its proper goods, ordering it to the mutual and fruitful love of the spouses, a fruitfulness begun through procreation and prolonged in the integral and Christian education of the children247.

The family constitutes the “fundamental cell of society” and the “primary place of humanization” of the person and of societies; therefore, “as experience shows, the civilization and cohesion of peoples depend above all on the human quality of their families. […] The Church is profoundly convinced of this, knowing perfectly well that ‘the future of humanity passes by way of the family’”248.

175. [The family and education] Parents are the first and principal educators of their children, in such a way that their education is the foundation of all other education and exerts a decisive influence. The Document Gravissimum educationis teaches that parents, since they have given life to their children, “are bound by the most serious obligation to educate their offspring and therefore must be recognized as the primary and principal educators. This role in education is so important that only with difficulty can it be supplied where it is lacking. Parents are the ones who must create a family atmosphere animated by love and respect for God and man, in which the well-rounded personal and social education of children is fostered. Hence the family is the first school of the social virtues that every society needs”249. In the family, “from infancy one can learn moral values, one begins to honor God and to make good use of freedom”250.

176. The family has an analogous efficacy in the transmission of Gospel values. The family, as a domestic church, must be “a place where the Gospel is transmitted and from which the Gospel radiates. This transmission takes place from parents to children and vice versa: the parents not only communicate the Gospel to their children, but can in turn receive from them this same Gospel as deeply lived by them; and it is also transmitted beyond the sphere of one's own family, for such a family becomes the evangelizer of many other families and of the neighborhood of which it forms part”251. In this way, through Christian families, the Church finds “her cradle and the place where she can bring about her own entry into the human generations, and where these in their turn can enter the Church”252. In places where de-Christianization, secularism and even anti-religious legislation prevail, “the domestic church remains the one place where children and young people can receive an authentic catechesis”253.

In summary, “Christian marriage and the Christian family build up the Church: for in the family the human person is not only brought into being and progressively introduced by means of education into the human community, but by means of the rebirth of Baptism and education in the faith the child is also introduced into God's family, which is the Church”254.

177. Therefore, this evangelization in families is also in some way irreplaceable and decisive: “family catechesis has a unique character and is in a sense irreplaceable; and it is exercised principally by an everyday life lived in accordance with the Gospel. This transmission of the faith in the family setting will often allow it to leave a decisive mark on children for their whole lives”255.

178. Consequently, because of its fundamental and decisive influence on the human and evangelical growth of human generations, we must give great importance to every kind of apostolic endeavor directed toward promoting, strengthening and multiplying Christian families in their various goods and ends. St. John Paul II, speaking of inculturation, affirmed that “study… and pastoral effort must be continued so that this ‘inculturation’ of the Christian faith may be carried out ever more extensively, also in the area of marriage and the family”256.

179. [The family and the culture of life] In the second place, in relation to the challenges arising from present-day culture in the field of respect for human life, we can point out the essential relationship between the family and the culture of life257. Man has been created in the image of God, who is a mystery of communion among the divine persons. God created the human being in His image and likeness, and created him male and female, to live the communion of persons and to become complementarily a mutual gift, as husband and wife –in a mutual and total, unique and indissoluble relationship–, and in the depth of that mutual love to become father and mother. In this sense, the family is organically related to the civilization of love; it is its heart and center.

180. However, the crisis of truth leads to a civilization of production and of use, a civilization of things and not of persons, in which persons are used in the same way as things. It leads to a consumerist civilization with an anti-birth mentality. The civilization of love, on the contrary, evokes the joy of life, the joy that a human being comes into this world and that spouses become parents. If the family is so important for building the civilization of love, it is because of its particular closeness and the intensity of the bonds that exist between persons and generations within it258.

That is to say, the family, founded on marriage, is a community of life and love whose mission is “to guard, reveal and communicate love”259. Parents are the co-workers and interpreters of God’s own love in the transmission of life and in its education. In the family each person is recognized, respected and honored because he is a person. The family is the setting in which life can be welcomed and protected, and can develop according to the demands of authentic human growth. For this reason, the role of the family in building the culture of life is decisive and irreplaceable260.

181. Therefore, the pastoral care of the family is very necessary, all the more so at a moment in history that is witnessing a widespread and radical crisis of this fundamental institution. The family is being attacked as never before and is undergoing the most acute crisis in all of history261.

It is urgent that families offer a convincing witness to the possibility of a marriage lived in a way fully in accordance with God’s plan and with the true demands of the human person: of the spouses and, above all, of the more fragile persons, the children262.

182. In a particular way, a cultural change in favor of life must be promoted beginning from families, first of all within the Christian communities themselves263. For this, what is needed is the formation of the moral conscience regarding the inviolable value of human life and the rediscovery of the constitutive link between freedom and truth264. Furthermore, educational work is indispensable so that young people may understand and live sexuality and love according to their true meaning and in their intimate interrelation265. And the formation of spouses for responsible procreation is required266.

183. In summary, cultural change demands of everyone the courage to adopt a new lifestyle within the framework of the primacy of being over having, of the person over things. This new lifestyle also implies passing from indifference to concern for the other, and from rejection to acceptance. In this task everyone has an important role to play: teachers and educators; intellectuals; those responsible for the social communications media267; women, who are called to bear witness to the meaning of authentic love, that gift of oneself and that acceptance of the other, since the experience of motherhood fosters a keen sensitivity toward other persons268.

b) Education

184. The most grave obligation of education, which belongs first and foremost to parents, requires the collaboration of society, and, furthermore, this duty of educating also belongs to the Church. “Above all, because the Church has the duty of proclaiming to all men the way of salvation, of communicating the life of Christ to believers, and of helping them with unceasing concern so that they may attain the fullness of this life”269.

185. All men, since they are endowed with the dignity of persons, have the inalienable right to an education “that corresponds to their own end, their own character, their different sex, and that is adapted to the culture and traditions of their country and, at the same time, open to fraternal relations with other peoples”; and ordered to man’s ultimate end and to the common good of society, for “true education aims at the formation of the human person in view of his ultimate end and of the good of societies”270.

186. Specifically, education must help children and adolescents “to develop harmoniously their physical, moral and intellectual endowments, so that they may gradually acquire a more perfect sense of responsibility in the proper development of their own lives, through continual effort and the acquisition of true freedom; likewise, that they be encouraged to value moral values with an upright conscience and to give them their personal adherence, and also that they be prompted to know and love God more”271. That is to say, the intellect must be formed, but without neglecting the formation of freedom, of the courage to make definitive decisions, and of the capacity to love through the sincere and selfless gift of oneself to others.

187. For their part, Christians, by reason of their condition as new creatures –born of water and the Holy Spirit– and as children of God, have a right to a Christian education, which “does not merely pursue the maturity of the human person, but seeks that the baptized… be disposed to live according to the new man in justice and holiness of truth (Eph 4:22-24), and so attain to the perfect man, to the measure of the fullness of Christ (cf. Eph 4:13), and collaborate in the growth of the Mystical Body. Helping, moreover, toward the Christian shaping of the world”272.

188. Given the importance of education in the human and Christian transformation of men and of societies, the Church exhorts her children “to give their help generously in the whole field of education”273; and she “reminds pastors of souls of their most grave obligation to arrange everything so that the faithful may enjoy a Christian education, and above all the young, who are the hope of the Church”274. St. John Paul II reminded religious of the need for a renewed commitment in the field of education, taking up the educational mission with renewed dedication, with schools of every kind and level, with universities and institutes of higher learning275.

Catholic schools

189. Among all the means of education, “the school has a special importance. For, while it cultivates the intellectual faculties with unremitting care, it develops the capacity for right judgment, introduces students to the cultural heritage won by past generations, promotes a sense of values, prepares for professional life, fosters friendly relations among students of diverse character and background, and contributes to mutual understanding”276.

190. The presence of the Church in the field of schooling is shown above all through Catholic schools; these, in addition to fostering, to no lesser degree, all the goods mentioned concerning the cultural and human formation of students, have as their “distinctive note” to help “adolescents, in the development of their own personality, to grow at the same time according to the new creature they have been made by Baptism, and, finally, to order all human culture to the message of salvation, so that the knowledge the students are gradually acquiring of the world, of life and of man may be illumined by faith, educating their students to pursue the good of the earthly city and… to serve the spread of the Kingdom of God”277.

191. Hence the Catholic school retains “its transcendent importance also at the present time”278, and the work of educators is of “the utmost importance”279.

192. In accordance with the right of the Church and in order to carry out our specific end, we must strive “to establish and freely direct schools of every order and grade”280, favoring “primary and secondary schools, which constitute the foundation of education, and also those required by present conditions: such as professional and technical schools, institutes for adult education, for social assistance, for the handicapped, and those in which teachers are prepared for religious education and for other forms of education”281.

193. Furthermore, bearing in mind that education must aim at the integral perfection of the human person, that is, at “cultivating the mind in such a way as to promote the capacity for wonder, for intuition, for contemplation and for forming a personal judgment, as well as for cultivating a religious, moral and social sense”282, and not only at providing practical training in the sciences and in the various technical skills, it is very fitting that, wherever possible, our Institute establish and develop Catholic humanistic schools.

Finally, in the sphere of educational work in schools as a turning point of culture, and without losing sight of its ultimate purpose of building the civilization of love, we can never forget “to attend to the needs of the poor, of those who are deprived of the help and affection of a family, or who do not share in the gift of faith”283.

Seminaries

194. Our Constitutions say that we wish to work in “education –especially in seminaries, universities and tertiary education–”284.

195. We must devote a great part of our best efforts and concerns to the formation of the seminarians of our Institute and also, to the extent of our possibilities, collaborate in other seminaries that ask for our help. For “the religious and moral condition of peoples depends in great part on the priest”285, and likewise “the longed-for renewal of the whole Church depends in great part on the ministry of priests”286; in turn, having priests who effectively change and transform societies with the power of the Gospel “depends on the formation received in the seminary”287. Hence the Second Vatican Council “proclaims the very great importance of priestly formation”288, which is indispensable to such a degree that Pius XI exhorted the bishops of the whole world: “give your colleges the best priests; do not regret withdrawing them from tasks that are apparently more important, but which cannot be compared with this capital and irreplaceable work”289. It is our desire “that God might give us the gift of being able to discover and guide so many vocations that we could fill all the good seminaries and novitiates of the whole world”290.

This demands vocational ministry, into which the best energies must be invested, with appropriate dedication to youth ministry291. That is to say, priestly and religious vocations must be promoted, above all through the faithful and joyful witness of consecrated life, while at the same time carrying out with competence and generosity our proper apostolates: Spiritual Exercises, oratories, formation courses, spiritual direction, etc.; and working in fraternal collaboration.

Faculties and universities

196. In order that the Gospel may become incarnate in the heart of cultures, the presence of the Church in the world of the universities, that is, of thought and research in their various fields, is “essential.” That is why the Church takes the greatest care of institutions of higher learning, above all of universities and faculties292.

Since “the destiny of society and of the Church herself is intimately connected with the progress of young people dedicated to higher studies,” the Second Vatican Council exhorts pastors to be solicitous in caring for university students –both of Catholic universities and also of non-Catholic ones–, providing “permanent spiritual and intellectual help to university youth”293.

197. The Second Vatican Council clearly indicated the principal objective of every university and faculty that depends on the Church, an objective that shows all its timeliness and urgency: “that each discipline be cultivated according to its own principles, its own methods and the freedom proper to scientific research, in such a way that the understanding of these disciplines may become ever deeper, and that, by considering with all attention the problems and discoveries of recent times, it may be seen more exactly how faith and reason move harmoniously toward the truth, which is one, following the teachings of the Doctors of the Church, above all of St. Thomas Aquinas”294.

198. In this way, “the presence of Christian thought must become, as it were, public, stable and universal in the endeavor to promote higher culture, and the students of these institutes must be formed as men outstanding for their learning, prepared to carry out the most important functions in society and to be witnesses of the faith in the world”295.

199. In this regard, St. John Paul II teaches in the Encyclical Letter Fides et ratio that the principal task of the university is the effort to achieve a new synthesis between reason and faith, and to recover Philosophy as a unified knowledge.

The separation between faith and reason impoverishes and weakens both. Faith, deprived of reason, has stressed feeling and experience, and runs the risk of being reduced to “myth” or superstition. Reason, deprived of the contribution of revelation, runs the danger of losing sight of its final goal. A reason that does not have an adult faith before it is not motivated to turn its gaze toward “the newness and radicality of being. Hence the pressing need to recover the profound unity between faith and Philosophy while respecting their reciprocal autonomy296.

200. Connected with this, the conviction that man is capable of attaining a unified and organic vision of knowledge is one of the tasks that Christian thought will have to take up in the course of the next millennium of the Christian era. The fragmentation of knowledge, insofar as it implies a partial approach to the truth, hinders the interior unity of contemporary man297.

201. In a special way, starting from the metaphysics of being, a convincing response must be given to the challenges coming from the various philosophical currents, which deserve appropriate attention; for example, eclecticism298; historicism, which establishes the truth of a Philosophy on the basis of its suitability to a particular period and a particular historical purpose, implicitly denying the perennial validity of truth299; scientism, which does not admit as valid any forms of knowledge other than those proper to the positive sciences, relegating religious and theological knowledge and ethical and aesthetic understanding to the borders of the imagination, while at the same time turning values into mere products of emotion and setting aside the notion of being in order to make room for the pure and simple fact300; pragmatism, as the mental attitude of one who, in making his choices, excludes recourse to theoretical reflection or to judgments based on ethical principles301. In summary, special attention must be paid to the cultural challenge of the loss of the sense of being, which constitutes the common horizon of many philosophies and which leads to a nihilistic attitude302.

202. Furthermore, we must continue to deepen our understanding of the meaning and universal value of the natural law, of the relationship between the positive sciences and faith, between freedom and truth, between bioethics and ethics, etc.; above all in the contemporary context, which seems to grant primacy to an artificial intelligence ever more subject to experimental technique, forgetting that all science must safeguard the human being and promote his inclination toward authentic good303.

203. Given the importance of Catholic and ecclesiastical universities and faculties in the field of education and evangelization, the Institutes that direct them must be very conscious of their responsibility, ensuring that in them, while engaging in active dialogue with present-day culture, the Catholic character that is proper to them is preserved, in full fidelity to the Magisterium of the Church304. In this last sense, it is necessary to remain on guard against the constant temptation to “be in fashion” and to seek the consensus of common opinion. On the contrary, a permanent disposition of obedience to the truth must be cultivated305.

204. Finally, “the Church also expects much from the Faculties of sacred sciences. For she entrusts to them the most serious task of forming their students not only for the priestly ministry but, above all, for teaching in centers of higher ecclesiastical studies, for scientific research, or for carrying out the most arduous functions of the intellectual apostolate”306.

205. [Dialogue between faith and reason]307 It seems fitting to us to examine this question more deeply, since it is also central to the task of inculturation, following the main points of the magnificent address that Benedict XVI gave at the University of Regensburg on the theme of faith and reason308.

In the first place, Benedict XVI affirms the intrinsic necessity of the encounter between biblical faith and Greek thought, since there is an essential relationship between the two. The Holy Father quotes a sentence that the Byzantine emperor Manuel II Palaeologus addressed to his Persian interlocutor: “not to act according to reason is contrary to the nature of God.” In this conviction of the emperor “the profound harmony between what is Greek in its best sense and what is faith in God according to the Bible becomes manifest.” That is to say, “God acts ‘σὺν λόγω’, with Logos.” Therefore, “the encounter between the biblical message and Greek thought was not a mere coincidence. St. Paul’s vision: ‘Come over to Macedonia and help us’ (cf. Acts 16:6-10), can be interpreted as a condensed expression of the intrinsic necessity of a rapprochement between biblical faith and Greek philosophizing.” A rapprochement that had begun long before: “the mysterious name of God uttered from the burning bush… which affirms of Him simply ‘I am,’ His being, stands in contrast to myth, and bears a close analogy to Socrates’ attempt to defeat and overcome myth itself.” “At bottom, it is a matter of the encounter between faith and reason, between authentic enlightenment and religion”309.

Hence the faith of the Church “has always held to the conviction that between God and us, between His eternal creating Spirit and our created reason, there exists a true analogy, in which certainly –as the Fourth Lateran Council said in 1215– the dissimilarities are infinitely greater than the similarities, yet not to the point of abolishing analogy and its language”310.

206. On the contrary, for Muslim doctrine “God is absolutely transcendent. His will is not bound to any of our categories, not even that of rationality.” Within the Church it is necessary to mention certain theological tendencies that break this synthesis: “In contrast to so-called Augustinian and Thomist intellectualism, John Duns Scotus introduced a voluntarist approach which, after successive developments, finally led to the claim that we know of God only the voluntas ordinata. Beyond this there would be the Freedom of God, by virtue of which He could have created and done even the opposite of everything He has actually done. […] Here positions emerge which… could even lead to an image of a capricious God, who is not bound even to truth and goodness. God’s transcendence and otherness are emphasized in so exaggerated a way that even our reason, our sense of the true and the good, cease to be an authentic mirror of God, whose abysmal possibilities remain eternally inaccessible to us and hidden behind His actual decisions”311.

Therefore, it is precisely the encounter between faith and reason, and the analogy between Being by essence and being by participation, that opens access to the possibility of knowing and expressing truly, though with limits, the biblical God, the God of faith; and at the same time of overcoming the irrational and absurd notion of a capricious God who is not bound even to truth and goodness. And it is also this encounter that makes it possible to ground truth and goodness metaphysically, overcoming the contradiction of affirming a God who is not bound to truth and goodness and who could, if He wished, make it lawful even to practice idolatry.

207. In fact, the historical process of separation between faith and reason, or the process of the dehellenization of Christianity –“which has increasingly dominated theological discussions since the beginning of the modern age”–, already in its first stage confined and reduced the sphere of faith “to practical reason”: “Dehellenization first emerges in connection with the postulates of the Reformation of the sixteenth century. […] Thus faith no longer appeared as a living historical word but as one element inserted into the structure of a philosophical system. The principle of sola Scriptura, by contrast, seeks the pure primordial form of faith, as it is originally found in the biblical Word. Metaphysics appears as a presupposition derived from another source, from which faith must be freed so that it may once again be entirely itself. Kant, with his assertion that he had had to give up thinking in order to make room for faith, carried this program forward with a radicalism the Reformers had not foreseen. In this way he anchored faith exclusively in practical reason, denying it access to reality as a whole”312.

208. Then comes the second stage of dehellenization, with liberal theology, which reduced the figure of Jesus to a mere man with a moral message: “The liberal theology of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries represented a second stage in the program of dehellenization, whose most prominent representative is Adolf von Harnack. […] Harnack’s central idea was simply to return to the man Jesus and to his simple message, prior to all the elaborations of theology and, precisely, also of hellenization. […] In the end, Jesus was presented as the father of a humanitarian moral message”313.

We may point out that this doctrine continues to exert a wide influence on certain present-day theologies of religious pluralism, which even go so far as to present the figure of Jesus Christ as a “myth” or as a –“gnostic”– manifestation, one among many, of the “inexpressible and unknowable” divinity.

209. Continuing with Benedict XVI’s address, it is very important to point out that this process of dehellenization –of separation between faith and reason– “coincides” with and adapts itself to the process of modern reason; and that in fact it not only substantially impoverishes the theological field of faith, but at the same time reduces the proper sphere of reason, which progressively loses –dangerously for man– all interest in the most properly human questions that belong to the ethical and transcendent dimension of the human being.

“At bottom, Harnack’s aim was to bring Christianity into harmony with modern reason, freeing it precisely from seemingly philosophical and theological elements, such as faith in the Divinity of Christ and in the Trinity of God. In this sense, historical-critical exegesis of the New Testament, in his view, restored to Theology its place in the cosmos of the university: for Harnack, Theology is something essentially historical and therefore strictly scientific. […] Underlying all this is the modern self-limitation of reason, classically expressed in Kant’s ‘critiques,’ but further radicalized in the meantime by the thinking of the natural sciences. This modern concept of reason is based, to put it briefly, on a synthesis between Platonism (Cartesianism) and empiricism, a synthesis confirmed by the success of technology. […] This implies two fundamental orientations, decisive for our question. Only the kind of certainty that derives from the synergy of mathematics and the empirical method can be considered scientific. Anything that claims to be science must conform to this criterion. The human sciences too, such as History, Psychology, Sociology and Philosophy, have attempted to approximate this canon of scientific value. Furthermore, it is important for our reflections to note that this method as such excludes the question of God, presenting it as an unscientific or pre-scientific question. But in this way we find ourselves facing a reduction of the sphere of science and of reason that must be called into question. I will return to this argument later. For the moment it suffices to bear in mind that, from this perspective, any attempt to maintain Theology as a ‘scientific’ discipline would leave of Christianity only a tiny fragment. But we must add more: if science as a whole is only this, then man himself would suffer a reduction, for the specifically human questions, that is, where he comes from and where he is going, the questions of religion and of ethics, cannot find a place within the space of common reason described by ‘science’ understood in this way, and must be relegated to the realm of the subjective. […] The situation thus created is dangerous for humanity, as can be seen in the pathologies that threaten religion and reason, pathologies that necessarily erupt when reason is reduced to the point that the questions of religion and ethics no longer concern it”314.

210. Finally, Benedict XVI refers to the third stage of dehellenization, which is currently spreading: “in view of the encounter among many cultures, it is often said today that the synthesis with Hellenism in the early Church was a first inculturation, which ought not to be binding on other cultures. These, it is said, should have the right to go back to the point prior to that inculturation, in order to discover the pure message of the New Testament and inculturate it anew in their respective settings. […] However, the fundamental decisions that concern precisely the relationship between faith and the search of human reason are part of the faith itself, and are a development consonant with its own nature”315.

211. In conclusion, Benedict XVI proposes to acknowledge “what is positive in the modern development of the spirit” and “to broaden our concept of reason and of its use.” Because we also see the dangers that arise from the new possibilities, it is necessary to ask how to avoid them: “We will succeed in doing so only if reason and faith come together in a new way, if we overcome the self-imposed limitation of reason to what can be verified by experiment, and once again open up its horizon in all its breadth. In this sense, Theology, not only as a historical discipline and human science, but as genuine Theology, that is, as a science that inquires into the reason of faith, must find a place in the university and in the wide dialogue of the sciences. […] The West has long been threatened by this aversion to the fundamental questions of its reason, and can only suffer great loss as a result. The courage to open oneself to the breadth of reason, and not the denial of its grandeur, is the program with which a Theology committed to reflection on biblical faith enters the debate of our time. ‘Not to act according to reason, not to act with logos, is contrary to the nature of God,’ said Manuel II, starting from his Christian image of God, in answer to his Persian interlocutor. In the dialogue of cultures we invite our interlocutors to this great logos, to this breadth of reason. To rediscover it constantly for ourselves is the great task of the university”316.

c) Intellectuals

212. What we may call the intellectual class deserves a chapter of its own. Among the turning points of culture are “men of thought, or intellectuals,” whom we must help “with regard to the initiation and calling, development, discernment, formation, consolidation, accompaniment and subsequent exercise of the vocation to the intellectual apostolate”317.

213. St. John Paul II, speaking “of the privileged places of culture,” besides mentioning the “world of the school and the university,” also refers explicitly to “the settings of scientific and technical research, the places of artistic creation and of humanistic reflection”318. Here we find ourselves in the sphere par excellence of the “creation” of culture. Intellectuals, who devote themselves to research and reflection in the various branches of human knowledge, are the principal “creators” of culture, since they have an incisive influence on the behavior and cultural models of men and of societies. It is they, above all, who present to society “the criteria of judgment, the determining values, the points of interest, the lines of thought, the sources of inspiration, the models of life of humanity”319.

214. As St. Thomas teaches –referring specifically to the Church–, intellectuals are –analogously– what architects are in the construction of a building: “the doctors of Theology are as principal craftsmen, who investigate and teach how others ought to procure salvation”320.

215. Therefore, those who have greater intellectual capacity should, as far as possible, be provided with the means and time necessary to devote themselves to scientific research in their respective disciplines.

216. As the Second Vatican Council counsels: “young men of greater talent… who show aptitude for teaching and research must be carefully prepared and incorporated into teaching”321.

217. Moreover, the level and future of our seminaries and also of our universities depend particularly on having professors who devote themselves seriously and with distinction to scientific research.

218. Finally, in the sphere of the intellectual apostolate and of scientific research, priority must be given to “publications, since what is written endures and spreads further,” and therefore “special emphasis will be placed on the spread of the Gospel through articles in scholarly or popular journals, monographs, books and other levels of publication”322.

d) The culture of “making”

219. God has entrusted to man, created in His image and likeness, the task of having dominion over the earth, first of all “by shaping the wonderful ‘material’ of his own humanity and then by exercising creative dominion over the universe that surrounds him”323. This second aspect represents the proper sphere of the culture of “making.”

Human work

220. In this sense, we have, in the first place, everything that concerns human work and technology and, in the second place, artistic activity. Although this activity directly perfects things exterior to man –making them useful or beautiful–, nevertheless man and his dignity are always the principle that must illumine and guide the sphere of “making.” The human person is and must always be the subject, the center and the end of all human activity and of every social institution. Consequently, the man who works is much more important than the product of his work, since this product derives from man –through the actualization of the skills and capacities inscribed in his nature– and is destined for his benefit and for the promotion of his dignity and of his good considered integrally.

221. We may also point out, in relation to the dimension of human work, but within the broader sphere of the social and political order whose soul is justice, the task of the Church to proclaim the social doctrine of the Church. That is, the task of purifying reason and of ethical formation, so that the demands of justice may be understood and put into practice324. For the Church, with her social doctrine, which argues on the basis of what is in accordance with the nature of every human being, helps to make it possible for what is just to be effectively recognized and then also carried out. In this sense, we may highlight the effort of the Magisterium, above all in the twentieth century, to read social reality in the light of the Gospel and to offer its own contribution to the solution of the social question325.

222. The Gospel must also penetrate, purify and transform the economic, social and political order. That is why “the new evangelization, which the modern world urgently needs and on which I have insisted on more than one occasion –said St. John Paul II–, must include among its essential elements the proclamation of the social doctrine of the Church”326.

“Today special attention and extraordinary commitment are required by the great challenges in which vast sectors of the human family are in greatest danger: wars and terrorism, hunger and thirst, and certain terrible epidemics. But it is also necessary to confront, with the same determination and clarity of purpose, the danger of political and legislative choices that contradict fundamental values and anthropological and ethical principles rooted in the nature of the human being, in particular with regard to the defense of human life at all its stages, from conception to natural death, and to the promotion of the family founded on marriage, avoiding the introduction into public law of other forms of union which would contribute to destabilizing it, obscuring its particular character and its irreplaceable social role”327.

223. This is the sphere of the laity, whose proper vocation is to seek the Kingdom of God by engaging in temporal affairs, ordering them according to God and informing them with the spirit of the Gospel328. The ethical and social aspect is an indispensable dimension of Christian life and witness: “The temptation to a hidden and individualistic spirituality must be rejected, one that has little to do with the demands of charity, or with the logic of the Incarnation and, in the end, with the very eschatological tension of Christianity”329.

Artistic activity

224. In the culture of making we have, in the second place, the sphere of artistic activity. This consists in giving aesthetic form to the ideas conceived in the mind. Just as in the dimension of work, in this task of producing “beautiful works” it is man who has primacy, not only because he is their author but also, in particular, because “in shaping a work the artist expresses himself to the point that his production is a unique reflection of his very being, of what he is and of how he is”330.

Artistic activity occupies an important place in human culture, in particular because of its proper and peculiar relationship with beauty, “which is, in a certain sense, the visible expression of the good, just as the good is the metaphysical condition of beauty”331. For this reason, the artistic vocation is a social service for the benefit of the common good, which “in its own way contributes to the life and the rebirth of a people”332.

225. Moreover, the historical development of beauty has found profound inspiration in the mystery of the Son of God made visible. “Indeed, the Son of God, in becoming man… has also revealed a new dimension of beauty, of which the Gospel message is full. […] The biblical Word has countless times become image, music or poetry, evoking in the language of art the mystery of the ‘Word made flesh’”333.

226. If we try to specify the cultural contribution of artistic creation in its proper relationship with beauty, we can say that it consists in the concrete effort to express and interpret “beautifully” the mystery hidden in reality. “Every authentic form of art is, in its own way, a path of access to the deepest reality of man and of the world”334. In this sense, art has an intimate affinity with the world of faith. “As a search for beauty, the fruit of an imagination that goes beyond the everyday, it is by its nature a kind of call to mystery”335. For this reason, the activity of artists constitutes a “noble ministry,” “when their works are able to reflect in some way the infinite beauty of God and to direct the thoughts of men toward Him. They are, in their own way, true theological ‘places’”336. In this sense, St. Paul VI pointed out that “Theology and Philosophy have another relationship with beauty, which consists in this: beauty, lending to doctrine its vesture and ornament, with the sweetness of song and the visibility of figurative and plastic art, opens the way for its precious teachings to be taught to many. Lofty disquisitions and subtle reasoning are inaccessible to many; yet they are able to grasp, to feel and to appreciate the influence of beauty, and more easily, by means of it, truth shows itself to them in splendor and nourishes them”337.

227. Consequently, the Church needs art in order to transmit the Gospel message, that is, to make the world of the spirit, of the invisible and of God “perceptible” and, as far as possible, “fascinating.” For art “possesses that particular capacity to reflect one or another aspect of the message, translating it into colors, forms or sounds that assist the intuition of the one who contemplates and listens. All this without depriving the message itself of its transcendent value and its halo of mystery. The Church, in particular, needs literary and figurative art, musicians, architects, etc.”338. In turn, artists, in their search for the hidden meaning of things and in their longing to express the world of the ineffable, find abundant inspiration in religious themes, which in fact are those most treated by artists of all ages. Above all in Christianity, since it, “by virtue of the central dogma of the Incarnation of the Word of God, offers the artist a particularly rich horizon of motifs of inspiration. How impoverished art would be if it abandoned the inexhaustible vein of the Gospel!”339. “Beauty is a key to mystery and a call to the transcendent. It is an invitation to savor life and to dream of the future”340.

e) The social communications media

228. Finally, we have, as a most important turning point of culture, the social communications media, which we must also evangelize.

229. They are instruments, “technical inventions which are directed principally to the human spirit and which have opened new ways of communicating with extraordinary ease news, ideas and doctrines of every kind”341. In our age, thanks to the progress of technology, they have been perfected in an admirable way.

230. Among them stand out “those media which by their nature can reach and move not only individual men but also the multitudes and the whole of human society, such as the press, cinema, radio, television and the like, which therefore can rightly be called the media of social communication”342.

231. Since they communicate news and ideas with great ease and speed, and reach the multitudes –the whole world–, their influence on present-day culture is evident; they in fact exercise an “influence, at once planetary and capillary, on the formation of mentalities and customs”343.

232. These instruments, if they are used rightly, “provide valuable help to the human race, since they contribute effectively to the relaxation and cultivation of minds and to the spread and strengthening of the Kingdom of God”344.

233. These media constitute “at present the privileged path for the creation and transmission of culture, and present themselves as a new frontier for the mission of the Church”345.

234. The Areopagus of Athens, to which St. Paul addressed himself, represented the center of the culture of the Athenian people; today “the first Areopagus of the modern age is the world of communications, which is unifying humanity and turning it –as the saying goes– into ‘a global village.’” These media have come to have such importance that “for many they are the chief means of information and formation, of guidance and inspiration for individual, family and social behavior. The new generations above all grow up in a world conditioned by these media”346.

235. The evangelization of the social communications media is therefore urgent; otherwise the Church and the Gospel will remain completely foreign to what constitutes “the first Areopagus of the modern age,” with grave losses for the task of inculturation, since, as St. John Paul II affirmed, “the very evangelization of modern culture depends in great part on their influence”347 . For this reason the Church exhorts that “they be used, without the least delay and with the utmost effort, in the most varied forms of apostolate, as the reality and the circumstances of our time demand. This task is urgent for the lay faithful and also for the members of the clergy: let the sacred pastors, therefore, hasten to fulfill their mission in this field, which is intimately linked with their ordinary duty of preaching”348.

236. Pastoral work in the media must have as its objective not only “to multiply the proclamation” and “to spread the Christian message,” but especially “to integrate the message itself into this ‘new culture’ created by modern communications”349.

237. That is to say, through these instruments of social communication human and Gospel values must be transmitted in a new synthesis. The Church must take up pastoral work in the media in a much broader and more incisive way in order to collaborate in the purification and transformation of this first Areopagus of modern man, a privileged place for the creation and transmission of cultural models.

Above all bearing in mind that St. Paul VI’s statement about the split between culture and the Gospel as the drama of our time “is fully confirmed” in the field of present-day communications350. For this reason, “in the use and reception of the instruments of communication there is urgent need both for an educational work of critical sense, animated by a passion for the truth, and for a work of defense of freedom, of respect for personal dignity, of the elevation of the authentic culture of peoples, through the firm and courageous rejection of every form of monopolization and manipulation.” However, “the apostolic responsibility of the lay faithful does not end with this work of defense either. On all the roads of the world, including the main roads of the press, cinema, radio, television and theater, the Gospel that saves must be proclaimed”351.

238. With regard to the internet, many Christians are already exploring its potential for evangelization, education, internal communication, administration and governance. On the other hand, Catholic newspapers and magazines, various publications, television and radio remain indispensable within the complete panorama of ecclesial communication352.

In summary, we can say that the Church takes up the providential opportunities offered to her by the social communications media to make the proclamation more incisive. However, these media can be used to reduce the Gospel to silence in the hearts of men353. For this reason it is necessary to educate in the responsible and critical use of the media of communication354. The various Institutes must be ready to cooperate also in the religious formation of those responsible for social communications, in order to avoid a corrupted use of the media and to promote a better quality of broadcasts, with messages respectful of the moral law and rich in human and Christian values355.

239. “On all the roads of the world, including the main roads of the press, cinema, radio, television and theater, the Gospel that saves must be proclaimed”356.

Conclusion

240. To the challenges that come from the shadows and imbalances of present-day culture –the rejection of every possibility of foundation and of truth, ethical disorientation and loss of meaning, the affirmation of the ephemeral and of a totally autonomous conscience, etc.– we must respond positively with an incisive and long-term pastoral care of culture. Starting from an authentically metaphysical vision, the effort must be made to pass from the phenomenon to the foundation, grounding and bearing witness to the certainty of the truth about man and his freedom, and about the global and transcendent meaning of human existence.

241. Against the spread of the “culture of death,” of contempt for human dignity and life, we must set the culture of respect and of the loving and generous acceptance of all human life, from its conception to its natural end, defending “with the utmost determination the right to life as the first and fundamental right, the condition of all the other rights of the person”357, and promoting the civilization of love and of life.

To the thought that shuts itself up within the limits of its own immanence and abandons the search for the transcendent and absolute, with all the tragic consequences this entails for the life of man, we must respond that authentic respect for personal dignity, which entails the defense and promotion of human rights, “demands the recognition of the religious dimension of man,” since “the relationship with God is a constitutive element of the very ‘being’ and ‘existing’ of man: it is in God that we live, and move, and have our being (Acts 17:28)”358. The glory of God is the life of man359.

In the face of the progressive estrangement and even opposition between reason and faith, which impoverishes both, we must attempt a new encounter and synthesis that enriches faith and gives “breadth” to reason. To the parrhesia of faith must correspond the boldness of reason360.

In the face of religious and cultural pluralism –in particular that of Asia–, which also contains within its own perspective different forms of relativism, we must respond with a patient dialogue that takes as its starting point the elements of truth and goodness present in the various cultures and religious traditions. That is to say, we must attempt to create new syntheses between faith and cultures, syntheses such as Europe created in its time.

The riches of the Gospel must be offered generously and abundantly to every man of good will, so that he may freely welcome the truth that saves and makes man truly free.

242. A renewed pastoral care of culture must be carried forward, since culture constitutes the privileged place of encounter with the message of Christ. For “a faith that does not become culture is a faith not fully received, not thought through in its entirety, not faithfully lived”361.

This is a task not only for specialists but for everyone, and it must be concentrated, in particular, in the settings of families, lay associations and parishes and, above all, in centers of education –especially seminaries and universities– and of scientific research, and in the social communications media.

243. In all these settings, there must ultimately be proposed and promoted an authentic pastoral care of holiness362, which emphasizes the primacy of grace and has its center in the Sunday Eucharist363. Holiness is the most perfect form of the vital synthesis between faith and culture. The Sunday Eucharist is its source and its center, the creator and fundamental expression of Christian culture. Pastoral work must tirelessly propose Jesus Christ, the fullness of all authentically human life and culture. “For the Gospel leads culture to its perfection, and authentic culture is open to the Gospel. […] The Gospel, far from endangering or impoverishing cultures, gives them an added measure of joy and beauty, of freedom and meaning, of truth and goodness”364.

St. Thomas teaches that “all other operations seem to be ordered to this one (contemplation) as to their end. For perfect contemplation requires bodily soundness, to which all the artificial goods necessary for life are ordered. It also requires calm from the disturbances of the passions, which is attained through the moral virtues and prudence, and quiet from external disturbances, to which the whole ordering of civil life is directed. So that, if things are rightly considered, all human offices seem to be ordered to the service of those who contemplate the truth” (Summa contra gentiles, III, 37).

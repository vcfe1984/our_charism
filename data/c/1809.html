Homily on the occasion of the celebration in honor of St. John Paul II

[Exordium]

Dear all in the Incarnate Word, we are gathered here in this magnificent Basilica of St. Peter, beneath whose vaults lie the sacred relics of St. Peter, the first Pope; those of some of the apostles; those of glorious martyrs and those of an admirable succession of Supreme Pontiffs. Here too, in this majestic reliquary, rest the remains (and this is particularly significant for us, being the reason that has brought us together here today) of one who was among the most illustrious Pontiffs of the Church: our dear St. John Paul II.

These very walls –a symbol of the Catholic Church and of the Primacy of Peter– which rise up as silent witnesses of the countless demonstrations and teachings of holiness of the successor of St. Peter whom we celebrate today, give us the fitting setting in which to contemplate the eloquent example of love and self-giving to the Church that God was pleased to offer us in the person of St. John Paul II.

That is why I would like to reflect with you, -among the many aspects of his fruitful life that could be mentioned- on the holy Pope’s love for the Church, referring to three notes which, although common to many Roman Pontiffs, in some way characterized and distinguished the Papacy of John Paul II from all the others, whether by their heroism or by their magnitude.

– I refer in the first place, as the first note, to the universal missionary spirit with which he exercised his Petrine ministry;

– in the second place, as the second note, his tireless dedication to preserving and increasing the unity of the Church, the priority task of every successor of Peter;

– and finally, in the third place, as the third note, the unconquerable fortitude and courage with which he himself carried out these tasks, giving us a magnanimous and joyful witness and example of love for the Cross of the Lord. For love for the Church implies love for Jesus, and love for His Cross is an inescapable condition of love for Jesus. It was this witness that encouraged all of us and so many others -and continues to encourage us- to act in the same way… to try to imitate him… to have him as a model of what we are and what we do.

All these elements are, also, fundamental principles of our spirituality and of our mission as consecrated persons, members of the Religious Family of the Incarnate Word.

1. Universal Missionary

John Paul II understood from the very first moment that the call to be Successor of Peter was a call to a universal mission, simply because the church is universal. He said so himself on a day like today, 39 years ago now, when in the homily for the inauguration of his pontificate he stated: “Today, a new Bishop ascends the Roman Chair of Peter, a Bishop full of trembling, conscious of his unworthiness. And how could one not tremble before the greatness of such a call and before the universal mission of this Roman See!”1.

St. John Paul II recognized as implicit in his call to be Universal Shepherd the call to “act in the name of Christ and in harmony with him in the whole vast human area in which Jesus willed that his Gospel be preached and the saving truth proclaimed, [that is, in] the whole world.”2 And that is exactly what he did. He was a tireless evangelizer… a tenacious missionary… an indefatigable lover and spreader of the truth.

His universal solicitude embraces not only geography, but also his immense Magisterium. We could say that there is hardly any aspect of theology and of human, ecclesial and social life that St. John Paul II did not address in his almost 27 years of pontificate with his 14 Encyclicals, 15 Apostolic Exhortations, 45 Apostolic Letters, with the promulgation of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, with the reform of the Code of Canon Law and of the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches, with his countless speeches, homilies, letters and books.

He was, without a doubt, a planetary missionary. His 104 apostolic journeys outside Italy, and the 146 within this country3, and his visits to almost all the parishes of the diocese of Rome bear witness to his immense desire to reach –in imitation, we say it analogically, of the Incarnate Word Himself– “all Christians and the whole world”4, fulfilling so fully the mission entrusted by Christ to the Apostles: make disciples of all nations5.

He recognized himself as a “sign of the presence of Christ in the world. And this is a presence –he wrote– that goes out to meet people wherever they may be; it calls them by name, encourages them, comforts them with the proclamation of the Good News and gathers them around the same Table”6. And wherever he went, that is precisely what he did. It is always very striking, as we remember him, to see the vivid awareness he had of the universal mission… to the ends of the earth.

John Paul II showed us and taught us a tireless missionary spirit, for he exercised his Petrine ministry -as he liked to repeat- moved by the sollicitudo omnium Ecclesiarum and devoting all his energies “to being a sign of the great Friend (with a capital F) of all of us”7, as he once told priests and religious. “The Church must be tireless in this mission received from Christ, -these are his words- she must be humble and courageous, like Christ himself and like his Apostles. She cannot lose heart even in the face of dissent, protests or any kind of accusation […] The Church cannot cease to proclaim the Gospel”8.

How magnificently his example rises before us, missionary religious, the example of this man who knew no limits in loving and serving Christ… because Christ himself loved us to the end9! And Christ himself told us: I have given you an example, so that you also may do as I have done to you10. All his wealth consisted in giving himself to the Word11 in his “Mystical Body, which is the Church”12.

In this resolve he showed himself to be well grounded, giving us a supreme example. John Paul II insistently repeated that evangelization requires holiness, and holiness requires nourishing the spiritual life13. That is why every day he devoted a long time to prayer. And so, both in Rome and during his apostolic journeys, he prayed a great deal: besides Mass, he recited the breviary, prayed the Rosary, made his hour of adoration -on Thursdays he made an additional hour of adoration-, and prayed the Way of the Cross on Fridays. He often spent long hours during the night in the shelter of the Heart of Christ in the Eucharist. Thus he would present himself in prayer “before Mary and Joseph and ask them for help [to] build, together with them and with all those whom God entrusted to him, the house for the Son of God: his holy Church”14, as he so beautifully confessed in one of his books.

Many bear witness, and many of us have seen and verified it, to how prayer was the driving force of John Paul II’s existence. Those who were his closest collaborators state that John Paul II “prayed for the whole Church. But not in an abstract way. He used to speak of the ‘geography of prayer’ and he had an atlas of the world on his desk; every day he would turn the pages and choose a country or a diocese to pray for. This universal solicitude was also manifested in the numerous meetings with bishops from all over the world, whom he would invite to lunch when they passed through Rome, so as to be able to talk with them about the situation of the Church in each place. After many years of pontificate, –those close to him recount– he knew very well both the strengths and the weaknesses not only of the dioceses but also of their pastors”15 and he prayed with greater insistence.

In short, his example as a “universal missionary,” which we wish to evoke today, is without a doubt for us, who want “to be as another humanity of Christ, in order to carry out with greater perfection the service of God and of men”16, a beacon of light, a guiding star, a constant stimulus for our religious and missionary life.

2. The unity of the Church

The second characteristic note of John Paul II’s self-giving and love for the Church is, in my view, the great zeal with which he devoted himself to promoting her unity. He knew how to awaken in so many places a lively sense of communion with him and, through his person, with all the believers of the Church spread throughout the whole world17. He was a man with a great capacity for inclusion18, Pope Benedict XVI wrote of him.

He considered it his own duty to fight for the cause of the unity of the Church, and with great courage he carried out the charge that Christ himself entrusted to Peter19, and he encouraged others to join in the endeavor to achieve the union willed and explicitly asked for by Christ. “Christ calls all his disciples to unity!”20, he said forcefully. “Division ‘openly and clearly contradicts the will of Christ, is a scandal to the world and harms the most holy cause of preaching the Gospel to every creature’”21, he wrote in his most beautiful Encyclical Letter Ut Unum Sint.

A unity that is built -he said- above all on the truth. A unity that he fostered not only among Christians of other churches and ecclesial communities, but also by strengthening the bonds of unity within the Catholic Church herself.

In this regard, I would like to read you a paragraph from a message he addressed to priests and religious in 1982. It read: “the communion of minds easily becomes union of hearts, a convergence of efforts for the same cause. A kingdom divided against itself cannot stand. A divided apostolate annihilates itself. And we know that it will be divided if it yields to the temptation of an exclusivism contrary to the rightful diversity of gifts and charisms, or to the temptation of isolation, losing interest in or closing itself off from the work of others, without fitting into common pastoral programs or plans. […] The union of forces of the workers of evangelization requires understanding; and this in turn will only be achieved through authentic dialogue”22.

John Paul II taught us that the essential identity that Christ has given to his Church is precisely the Eucharist, which is the ‘memorial’ of the Lord, and this of course is stronger than all the divisions introduced by men. The most genuine essence of the Church, that is, what is proper to her, is not division but unity23, and that unity is caused as its proper effect by the Eucharist. That is why for us too the cause of the unity of the Church is our own cause, which begins with the firm union of each one of us to the Incarnate Word and among ourselves through mutual love24.

3. Fortitude

Finally, the Holy Father, whom we so often saw clinging to the cross, gave us proof of his immeasurable love for the Church through his sufferings which, united to the tribulation of Christ, he bore with heroic fortitude. St. John Paul II was without a doubt a man of suffering, but of a suffering borne with dignity, with heroism and with joy. He himself said -he said it repeatedly- that he had placed his life under the sign of the cross25.

Certainly, over the centuries there were many Pontiffs who served the Church with unfailing fortitude in the faith. Nevertheless, among all of them, the Polish pope shone in a particular way.

And this is seen in “the primacy he gave to faith throughout his whole life and in all his activity, to a courageous and fearless faith, a faith refined in difficulties, ready to respond generously to every call of God”26. For he knew that the “Follow me which he had accepted, hearing the voice of Christ in the call of the Church, also carried with it the Whoever seeks to keep his life will lose it; and whoever loses it will keep it alive”27.

We know well, as he himself stated, that engraved upon his soul were the words that Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński addressed to him at his episcopal ordination: “you have the duty to act not only through the word and liturgical service, but also through the offering of suffering. […] Lack of fortitude is the beginning of defeat. Can [one] go on being an apostle? For an apostle, the witness given to the Truth is essential! And that always requires fortitude. […] The greatest fault of the apostle is fear. Lack of faith in the power of the Master awakens fear; and fear oppresses the heart and tightens the throat. The apostle then stops professing his faith. Is he still an apostle? The disciples who abandoned the Master increased the courage of the executioners.”

How firmly these words remained engraved in the Polish Pope! So much so that already in his first homily he told the whole world: “Do not be afraid to welcome Christ and to accept his power! … Do not be afraid of Christ!”

John Paul II was convinced that “one cannot turn one’s back on the truth, cease to proclaim it, hide it, even if it is a difficult truth whose revelation brings great pain with it: You will know the truth and the truth will set you free (Jn 8:32). This is our task and, at the same time, our support! -he said-. […] One must bear witness to the truth, even at the price of being persecuted, even at the cost of one’s blood, as Christ himself did”28.

And so, without denying the difficulties and the crosses that the proclamation of and fidelity to the Incarnate Word entail, he exhorted us: “Go everywhere with confidence […] know how to speak the word that gives light, because it comes from God with the power of the Spirit of Jesus. Never lose heart in the face of difficulties. Humbly take your share of the Church’s toil”29.

And with an extraordinary spirit of faith and long experience in the crucible of tribulations, this most worthy successor of Peter, even though he himself was immersed in sufferings, knew how to instill courage in everyone. And so he told us: “Surely we will encounter difficulties [in our life and in our apostolate]. There is nothing extraordinary about that. They are part of the life of faith. Sometimes the trials are light, at other times very difficult and even dramatic. In the trial we may feel alone, but divine grace, the grace of a victorious faith, never abandons us. That is why we can hope to overcome victoriously any trial, even the most difficult”30.

This was his message, this was his example, and this continues to be the hope he sought to awaken in everyone: “The presence of evil is always accompanied by the presence of good, of grace. […] There is no evil from which God cannot draw a greater good. There is no suffering that cannot be turned into a path that leads to Him. […] All suffering exists in the world to awaken in us love, which is the gift of oneself in generous and disinterested service of others. In love, which has its source in the Heart of Jesus, lies the hope for the future of the world”31.

[Peroratio]

Dear all: I would like to conclude by recalling the words that John Paul II himself wrote in a poem dedicated to St. Stanislaus, and this place, this basilica, would seem to be the most fitting place to do so: “The Church is always outliving me, she is the ground of my life, and its summit; the Church […] is the Sacrament of my existence unfolded in God, who is my Father.” May his example of love and unconditional self-giving in the service of the Church transform us, as it did him, into “other Christs.”

With heartfelt supplication we commend today to our St. John Paul II, in a very special way, our dear Religious Family, our missions and our religious so that, moved by the magnanimous and heroic example of this Holy Pope, we may go forth with generous zeal and resolute self-giving to serve our Mother the Church without limits of horizons, being builders of unity and persevering with unshakable firmness in the faith, giving everywhere consistent witness to the truth.

And through his intercession we also ask this grace of the Mother of the Incarnate Word, of whom the Great Pope was a tender and devoted son and slave.

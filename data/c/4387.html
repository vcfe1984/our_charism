There was a tightrope walker named Charles Blondin1, of French origin. He was able to perform incredible tricks on the tightrope. All over Paris he performed acrobatic acts at great heights which both entertained and terrified the spectators. He gave one performance after another, for example, doing acrobatics blindfolded on the tightrope, or like the time when, blindfolded and pushing a wheelbarrow, he crossed the tightrope from end to end at a great height. An American showman, William Niblo, read about him in the newspapers and wrote Blondin a letter saying: “Mr. Tightrope Walker, I don’t believe you can do it, but I am willing to make you an offer. For a considerable sum of money, plus travel expenses, I would like to challenge you to perform your acrobatic act over Niagara Falls.” To which Blondin replied: “Sir, although I have never been to America nor seen the falls, I would love to come.” Well then, after a great deal of promotion on Niblo’s part and the corresponding preparations over the falls, a huge crowd came to see the show. The tightrope walker was to start from the Canadian side of the falls and walk toward the American side. The drums began to roll, and he advanced along the 340-meter-long tightrope, suspended over a somewhat dangerous part of the falls 49 meters above the water, and blindfolded! Blondin crossed to the other side without any problem on June 30, 1859. The crowd went wild; then the tightrope walker went up to the American showman and asked him: “So?… Now do you believe I can do it?” “Of course I believe it! I just saw it!” “No,” Blondin says to him, “do you really believe I can do it?” “Yes! Of course I believe it, you just did it.” “No, no, no,” the tightrope walker went on, “do you truly believe I can do it?” “Yes,” said the showman, “I believe you can do it.” “Well then,” answered Blondin, “get into the wheelbarrow.”

In Greek, to believe means “that by which one lives”; that is, it must be the operative driving force that directs our actions, our decisions and, in short, our conduct as a whole and in its smallest details, because faith without works is dead2, to the point of being able to say that faith is not only “that by which one lives” but also “that for which one dies.”

Nevertheless, the reality is evident –and we are not exempt from it– that “the split between the faith which many profess and their daily lives deserves to be counted among the more serious errors of our age,”3 as the Second Vatican Council rightly pointed out in its time.

How many of those who say they profess no formal religion can say what it is they do not believe in? The question arises because some years ago many people who said they had no faith knew what they did not believe in; today, those who have no faith do not even know what they do not believe in. They have abandoned all certainties; they do not even have parameters or principles or standards by which to judge their own agnosticism.

Nowadays, with the pandemic, the conflicts between different countries, the scandals and the resulting insecurity, these people have begun to question their own doubts. The words progress, evolution and science, which once motivated and inflamed them, giving them the illusion of faith, now leave them cold.

“Faith is not, as many think,” says Ven. Fulton Sheen, “an emotional trust,” like that of the American showman in the example cited above. “It is not the belief that something is going to happen to you, nor is it even the will to believe in spite of difficulties. Rather, faith is the acceptance of a truth on the authority of God who reveals it. And therefore it presupposes reason.”4 In this sense our Proper Law points out that “faith itself cannot do without reason or without the effort to think through its contents.”5

Hence the condition for converting to Catholicism and persevering in it is total, complete and absolute submission to the authority of Christ and to His prolongation in the Church. Therefore, “a Catholic can be defined as one who has made the incredible discovery that God knows more than he does” 6.

Faith is related to reason as the telescope is to the eye: it does not destroy vision, but opens up to it new worlds that would otherwise be veiled from it. And since faith is a virtue, it is therefore a habit –not acquired, as swimming might be, but a habit infused by God at the moment of Baptism– and it grows with practice; that is, God makes it grow through our practice, which disposes us for the increase of faith… for although faith is a gift of God, it does not practice itself: we have to move our will freely in each act of faith. And our ideal is to reach a point in its practice where, like our Lord on the Cross, we bear witness to God even in the midst of the abandonment and agony of a crucifixion. That “is the spirit of faith and love with which all the members must live the mystery of the Incarnate Word.”7

“We are called to be the men of faith who must lead and strengthen their brothers in the faith.”8 That is what is ours. That is why it seemed good to us to dedicate these pages to the crucial theme of the faith of the priest and of the religious, a faith not seldom put into the crucible of arduous trials, and which just as often finds itself shaken, weakened, questioned, and runs the risk of being confined to ritual, of becoming “stiff” or –God forbid– of being cracked. Faith is essential for being saints and for evangelizing the culture, and without it missionary work, however praiseworthy it may be, remains just that: a great work, but lacking a soul, merits and firm roots.

1. Faith

The Catechism of the Catholic Church defines the virtue of faith as follows: “Faith is the theological virtue by which we believe in God and believe all that He has said and revealed to us, and that Holy Church proposes for our belief, because He is truth itself. By faith ‘man freely commits his entire self to God’9. For this reason the believer seeks to know and do God’s will.”10 Now, the virtue of faith in the priest takes on a particular specificity.

In today’s world there is a worrying decline of faith. Fulton Sheen, decades ago already and speaking of his own country (but it seems to us something that applies to many countries), said: “The principal cause of the decline of religion in America is the irrational and unfounded character of belief. Unless the foundation is solid, the superstructure will soon totter and fall. Try this experiment: ask those who call themselves Christians why they believe, and most of them will find themselves unable to give a reason.”11 That is why it is almost instinctive to recall the words of the Incarnate Word: When the Son of Man comes, will He find faith on earth?12. Of course, this is not something only of today. Already in the 19th century Kierkegaard could write: “For a long time now there has not been enough faith in the world, and that is the reason why faith does not move the world.”

Now, if the life of faith is far from vigorous in our contemporaries, it ought at least to shine forth in priests, called as we are “to live, to believe and to communicate the Christian faith,”13 that is, called “to be witnesses,”14 to be masters in not losing sight of “the eminently supernatural character of faith and of the Christian life.”15

That is why St. Paul said: Men must see in us ministers of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God. What is sought in stewards is that they be faithful16. To be “faithful” means to be a man of faith. What people expect of us is “fidelity.” We will only be in a position to offer such a witness if we are men of faith. Some years ago now, Cardinal Ratzinger stated: “The world thirsts to know, not our ecclesial problems, but the fire that Jesus brought to the earth.”17

As members of the Institute, which prides itself on having, as a ‘non-negotiable’ element attached to the charism, a providential view of life, we must not limit ourselves to perceiving errors and dangers. Certainly the negative or problematic aspects cannot be ignored either, but we must immediately strive to discover paths of faith and hope.

“In the heart of a changing world –said St. John Paul II– in which unheard-of injustices and sufferings persist and grow worse, you are called to make a Christian reading [and, therefore, a reading of faith] of historical and cultural events and phenomena. In particular you must be bearers of light and hope in today’s society. Do not let yourselves be deceived by naive optimism; on the contrary, continue to be faithful witnesses of a God who certainly loves humanity and offers it the grace necessary so that it may work effectively in building a better world, more just and more respectful of the dignity of every human being. The challenge that contemporary culture poses to faith is precisely this: to abandon the easy inclination to paint dark and negative scenarios, in order to trace possible paths, not illusory ones, of redemption, liberation and hope.”18

▪ The faith of the priest

Although faith belongs to every baptized person, priestly faith is distinguished from the common faith of Christians.

All of you know that by virtue of the sacramental grace that the priest receives together with the character, there is a kind of modification of habitual grace, by which the newly ordained is enabled to behave as “another Christ”; that is, he is “enabled” for moral configuration with Christ through the imitation of the virtues and sentiments that He had19.

It is perfectly clear that the priest, whoever he may be, and whether he exercises his ministry in the jungle of an island, in the silence of the cloister, bedridden by illness, or traveling through rural villages or great cities, needs a faith far above the ordinary in order to be equal to his lofty mission. The faith of the priest, which is grafted onto that of the simple Christian faithful, is directed with predilection to the mysteries he celebrates, and is united to the personal gift of himself to the Lord, who is pleased to make use of him for His glory and the sanctification of men.

Archbishop Adolfo Tortolo once stated: “As priests we were born of a mystery of faith, we are a mystery of faith, and we must be perennial witnesses of the faith. Faith envelops us, penetrates us, nourishes us. A priestly life without faith is a real contradiction. A priest who did not live by faith would be incomprehensible, even to himself. And, in addition, he would be thoroughly unhappy in his vocation, of which he would experience only the sensible privations without tasting, in return, the immense supernatural riches it brings.”20

St. John Paul II referred to such men in 1985 in Luxembourg when addressing the clergy: “It must not be denied that many brothers feel insecure in their priesthood21. The evolution of numerous aspects of people’s mentality and of social structures causes many of you to ask whether you are in the right place or whether your priesthood still has meaning. This insecurity is accentuated even more by the terrible question of who will continue the work when priestly vocations are so scarce; a concern for the future that weighs on all of us. One can react to these interior tensions, for example, by adopting exaggerated positions of rigidity or permissiveness, by becoming disillusioned or resigned, by limiting oneself to the bare minimum in the ritual performance of the priestly mission.

From a human perspective, these ways of behaving may be understandable. But the response that befits our priesthood is a different one: faith in the Lord, who is present in our days as well; trust in His word: Cast the nets, a word that has a special value for us priests. It is also important that we be men of firm faith. Let us remember what Jesus said to Peter shortly before setting out for the Mount of Olives: Simon… I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail; and you, once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers22. This is not a function proper only to Peter; it is also the function of each priest toward his brothers in the priesthood and toward the faithful to whom he has been sent. Faith strengthened in the fire of trials and reconciled in forgiveness is a force that is capable, today too, of moving mountains. That faith encourages us to go again and again, with a missionary spirit, after the lost sheep. With such a faith, our life follows the path of Christ, dead and risen: from Him alone does our priestly existence draw its meaning and its mission.”23

Faith is everything for a priest; it rules his life; it gives him the principles that serve as his guide when the moment comes to act, to decide, to enlighten; it puts him in living contact with that indissoluble union that exists between him and the Incarnate Word, as the Magisterium of the Church teaches: there is “a specific ontological bond which unites the priest to Christ, the High Priest and Good Shepherd.”24

That is why our priesthood can only develop and endure if it is rooted in the paschal mystery. The determining element of our priesthood is not earthly success, but the law of the grain of wheat that must fall into the ground and die in order to bear fruit25. That is why the mystery of redemption must be the great theme of our lives. The message of salvation, which the priest is charged with proclaiming so that all who hear it may believe in it, must be the favorite subject of his preaching. And for this, it is necessary first of all that he himself believe intensely in that message.

Priestly faith is directed before all else to the Person of Jesus Christ. That is why our Constitutions say that, in order to bring the effects of the redemption to bear upon souls, “it is absolutely indispensable to unite oneself to His Person.”26 Hence the devotion that the priest must have to Christ should be centered on the Incarnate Word as God and as Savior and sole Mediator of salvation. As a minister of Christ, called to become like Him both in his interior life and in his exterior ministry, and to live intimately united with Him, he must possess in his heart a living faith in the mystery of the Incarnate Word and Redeemer. Faith will make his encounter with Christ permanent, and the encounter with Christ will necessarily transform him. In other words: faith moves us to live in “holy familiarity with the Word made flesh.”27

2. The vicissitudes of the priest’s faith

“By faith man freely commits his entire self to God,”28 we said a few paragraphs above, and our Proper Law points out the same. Now, this self-giving is continuous, ever renewed, progressive, and it implies a supernatural contact that can be unceasingly intensified.

It is enough simply to recall the calling of the apostles. From their first calling they were won over by Jesus. We have found the Messiah29, they said, full of joy. That was already an act of faith. However, that faith was incipient and kept growing in step with their intimacy with Christ, and only after passing through the scandal of the cross did it culminate in full configuration through martyrdom.

So too the faith of the priest will normally have to grow with the passing of the years.

Now, in this process of the growth and intensification of faith, the battles are not few; battles which come down to fighting to safeguard not only the deposit of faith, always keeping that deposit whole in our thoughts despite the many fashionable contradictions, but also to remain faithful to the commitments we have taken on. That is, fidelity to the priestly character, fidelity to our mission, fidelity to the Truth, fidelity to our priestly function in the Church, fidelity to the charism of the Institute in which we have consecrated ourselves to God.

One need not search very hard to realize that maintaining this fidelity is no easy task. The enemy of our souls, the spirit of the world and our own misery are always striving to weaken it, to lull it to sleep and to deal it the death blow. One of the most common obstacles to the intensification of our faith, especially when some years of priesthood have already gone by, is routine, which becomes like a moth eating away at priestly faith. Routine in the ministry is what most often paralyzes or lulls our faith to sleep. That is why we must always ask –as our Proper Law beautifully teaches– for “spiritual fervor, the joy of evangelizing, even when we have to sow in tears.”30 And not only must we ask for this grace, but we must dispose ourselves for it by moving against routine. Let us not forget that, as St. John Paul II used to repeat, “faith is strengthened when it is given!” and one must have initiative and inventiveness so that this faith may reach as many souls as possible, as much and as well as possible. And for that, one must sacrifice oneself.

Likewise, it is of capital importance to nourish oneself continually with the riches of the faith so that its peculiar joy is not compromised in moments of difficulty, aridity and suffering. Our faith is not a sort of deposit that one possesses and preserves without effort, independently of the will, or that is immune to adverse circumstances or to the environment in which we live. It is rather a treasure that must be protected, defended and shared with all.

In this struggle to remain firm in the faith31 trials must not frighten us, for they are part of the program. Not seldom those trials –which God, as a loving Father, not only permits to happen to us but Himself wills for us– put us in a crisis; they leave us facing the dilemma of the American showman in the anecdote: either we run away or we make the act of faith.

That, on the one hand. On the other hand, we would like to warn about one of the obstacles that may come upon us from the enemy of our soul, an obstacle that tends to be quite effective in our pilgrimage of faith, and that is discouragement.

The Magisterium of the Church itself points this out: “This Sacred Council, while keeping in mind the joys of priestly life, cannot forget the difficulties which priests experience in the present circumstances of life today. […] For the new obstacles that stand in the way of faith, the apparent sterility of the work done, and the bitter loneliness they feel can put them in danger of losing heart.”32

Discouragement is a powerful weapon in the hands of the devil33. Discouragement, if it manages to invade us and stays in us insistently, can halt or paralyze us on our way to God. Christ Himself warned St. Faustina of this: “you must know that the greatest obstacle to holiness is discouragement and unjustified anxiety, which take away from you the possibility of exercising yourself in the virtues.”34

This strategy of the evil one is particularly insidious because sometimes the hand of the devil is not easily perceptible, just as it is not evident either that we are drifting away from God. There are many situations, states of soul, particular circumstances and snares of the devil that can lead a soul to discouragement. To some souls he shows all their deficiencies and ineptitudes so as to leave them completely paralyzed: to these he insinuates that since the soul is not perfectly ordered in its thoughts, in its inclinations, in its affections, there is therefore no spiritual progress. Indeed, the devil denies the pilgrim character of our lives. He falsely lays down: total progress or no progress at all. The message of the evil one in this case is that there is no future and no potential on this path. Consequently, he paralyzes us, immobilizes us, and we can succumb to the most crushing discouragement.

In others, discouragement follows upon weariness. The element of temptation enters into weariness when the devil seizes on that weariness and uses it to tell us that we ought to stop doing what we are called to do: sitting down to hear confessions before Mass, studying as part of ongoing formation, persevering in prayer despite great aridity, putting grit into the apostolate, supporting good initiatives… In other words, the devil seeks to push the soul toward such disheartenment and such a state of discouragement that from there to abandoning its commitments (religious vows, activities proper to the ministry) and the mission entrusted to it there is only one step.

We must realize that the devil likes to exploit the weariness caused by the length of the trial as if it were a mine of discouragement, of sorrow, of frustration, seeking to stall initiatives, filling the path with uncertainties, sowing days and nights with temptations about the future, while in the depths of the soul there echoes the cry: how long, Lord, how long?

Another way in which discouragement works in our lives is through feeling overwhelmed. The soul experiences a kind of powerlessness, for example, when a certain combination of circumstances (apostolate, health, family, community, lack of time, whatever it may be) seems to conspire to confuse us and keep us down. Of course, all these things create the perfect breeding ground for discouragement. The devil knows this and of course wants to lead us to ‘disengage’ from all of it by making us feel powerless and overwhelmed before the demands and requirements of our priestly ministry.

Discouragement can also come through intimidation, that is, as the fruit of persecution, hostility, ecclesial scandals and all those circumstances that can be intimidating and leave us reeling. Notice that the devil works in both, persecutors and persecuted. He encourages the persecutors to persecute; the persecuted he discourages. What he wants is that our faith not be proclaimed, that Christ not be praised to the ends of the earth: he wants to draw the missionaries away from the sublime work of the salvation of souls, he wants to cause division and scatter them.

And lastly, we could mention here that the devil injects discouragement into us because of our ineffectiveness in producing real change. Notice, from what we have been saying, that discouragement especially attacks people who have great ideals and who want to do something good. Precisely because they know what could be and perceive a certain potential future, failure in realizing those aspirations greatly discourages them. It happens to those who care for the poor but see that poverty does not end despite their continuous efforts; consequently, since they are not producing real change, possibility becomes impossibility, and then discouragement arises.

We religious are especially inclined to this kind of discouragement. Because many times the results expected from pastoral work rarely coincide with our expectations. But we must realize that in this one plants the seed, and it may take a long time and require much patience until it bears fruit: it depends on God. Blessed Charles de Foucauld (who will soon be canonized), for example, died in 1916 at the hands of rebels from the very tribe he had gone to serve. He apparently had very few apostolic fruits during his life. But he remained faithful to his commitment. He stayed in his ‘little Nazareth’ in the Sahara desert, serving those whom God had entrusted to him. And of course, he too was tempted to become disillusioned, but pay attention to his answer: “One sees oneself so wretched even after so many blessings −he says−. Nevertheless, one must not grow sad but look above all to our Beloved Lord… Hope is an obligation −because charity hopes all things− hope is nothing other than faith in the goodness of God.”35 And then elsewhere he says: “we must work continually, without becoming discouraged, against ourselves, the world and the devil until the end of time. To work, to pray and to suffer −this is our method.”36 And we could also add being very devoted to the Virgin, without whom the work of evangelization is impossible for us.

Certainly the ways in which our faith can be tested are many and very diverse, because, as our Proper Law wisely points out to us, “only God knows… the secret springs that must be moved to bring us to heaven.”37 There is no need to be frightened. The faith of the apostles too was put to the test on many and diverse occasions, but principally by the spectacle of the cross. Our faith too, even though we are priests, always retains its character of trial. Let us not forget that we are part of the Church militant. The exercise of the priesthood is a continuous exercise of faith: believing in the presence of Christ beyond the poverty of the sacramental signs, believing in the Lordship of Christ beyond the unworthiness of His representatives on earth. Because we are more deeply immersed in the mystery of the Church, we know the misery of her men much better than the faithful in general do, and in this sense our act of faith becomes more heroic, more naked, more independent of human props and, therefore, with more renunciations.

Very conscious of this, St. John Paul II, when he visited Argentina in 1982, said to a Cathedral in Buenos Aires filled with priests and religious: “The guarantee of the ecclesial character of our life is union with the bishop and with the Pope. Such a union, faithful and always renewed, may at times be difficult and even involve renunciations and sacrifices. But do not hesitate to accept both when necessary. It is the ‘price,’ the ‘ransom’38 that the Lord asks of you, for Him and with Him, for the good of the multitude and of yourselves.”39

This trial of faith of which we have been speaking, at times heightened by ecclesial scandals, has existed, exists and will exist until the end of time for all the priests of history. But it is perhaps harder for the priest of our time, who must exercise his ministry in an environment that is often de-Christianized or agnostic. Nowadays, “the ministers of the Church, and often even the Christian faithful, feel like strangers in this world, anxiously seeking suitable means and words to be able to communicate with it.”40 Ideological and practical materialism and hedonism have invaded everything, and they impose themselves on people through social networks and the mass media. Therefore, it should not surprise us that our message of conversion is not always well received or seems to leave no mark on souls. That is why the first thing, the most important thing asked of the priest, is that he remain firm in his faith, that he courageously resist the contagion of worldly criteria. Do not be conformed to this world41, St. Paul already said. It is not the world that must “conform” the priest; on the contrary, it is the priest who must “conform” the world, give it a shape coherent with the spirit of the Gospel, since that is why he is in the world, that is why he is a priest. And, in the second place, we must keep doing all that is in our power to present the Truth, the doctrine of the Church, the Word of God, as effectively as possible. Without ever becoming discouraged! The kingdom of God is like a man who casts seed upon the ground, and whether he sleeps or wakes, night and day, the seed sprouts and grows, he knows not how42. “To spend one’s life in the service of the people of God through the word and the sacraments, this is your great task,” our Spiritual Father stated in a fatherly way, “your glory, and your treasure.”43

In the face of the apparent victory of the world –of the spirit of the world–, the priest will affirm his conviction that through his weak hands passes a divine, supernatural force capable of pulverizing the bulk of the world, of that world which in the final analysis is, despite all appearances, empty and superficial; he will affirm the conviction that he holds in his hands the sling capable of bringing down the giant Goliath. He will have to remember the example of St. Paul, who was never ashamed of the Gospel44 and said I know whom I have believed45. He must repeat the words of the beloved disciple: This is the victory that overcomes the world, our faith46. Before a world that rejects him and seeks to shut him away, to consider him mad, his faith must become heroic, so as not to give up, not to resign, not to diminish, not to yield to the temptation to accommodate himself to the world, to serve two masters. Only thus could he say, when the moment of his death comes: I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith47.

Faith assures us that the last word is the triumph of God.

3. The qualities of the priest’s faith

From what has been said so far, it can be seen how the faith of the priest must be much more perfect than that of the layman. And in our particular case –as priests of our beloved Institute– our Proper Law details for us particular notes of our faith learned from the Virgin Mother of God: living, firm, intrepid, eminent, heroic, convinced, in absolute harmony with the doctrine proposed by the Catholic Church, penetrating, triumphant over the world and evil, a builder of great things, illuminating life, a faith that strengthens, encourages, comforts and excludes fear48.

That is, it is not the world, nor an unmortified sensibility, that dictates his criteria to the priest; rather, he must do what he ought, since he has been called not to please the world but God, so that whatever he does he must do with all his soul, knowing that he does it for the Lord and not for men49.

Fr. Alfredo Sáenz states that three qualities must mark the faith of the priest: it will be solid by its firmness, enlightened by its depth, and operative by its radiance.

▪ Solid

“We priests have been called not only to live in the faith but also to constitute something like points of reference for the faith of our people, ‘being courageous witnesses of the truth taught by the Church, so that Christians may not be carried about by every wind of doctrine’50. Our faith must be pure as a lily, but strong as an oak. On the solidity of our faith will rest the faith of the people entrusted to us.”51 And we must remain united in the firmness of the truth, resting on the cornerstone which is Christ, the ultimate foundation of our faith and the supreme motivation of our life. “We cannot be like those whom the apostle lashed out against, who are always learning and never arrive at the knowledge of the truth52. Before becoming Pope, John Paul I said: ‘Truth is not a hare that one is always chasing without ever catching it.’ A priest who does not live by certainties, but is always as it were feeling his way or mixing his faith with human subtleties, ends up weakening the faith of his faithful.”53

“Nothing is more committing than faith. To believe is to give oneself, without reserve. ‘That is why –writes Archbishop Adolfo Tortolo– half-hearted faith, like superficiality in faith, are states anomalous in themselves, which create conflict, wreck and sterilize.’ The robustness of our faith is closely related to the integrity of our faith. […] John Paul II has said it with his characteristic clarity: ‘Your people, simple and intelligent, expect from you that complete preaching of the Catholic faith, sown with full hands in the fertile soil of a traditional and welcoming faith, of a popular piety which, though it always needs to be evangelized, is already a field furrowed by the Spirit to receive that evangelization and catechesis.

Are not the painful circumstances your countries are going through a demand to intensify that sowing? Do not your people ask for reasons to believe and to hope, motives to love and to build, which can only come from Christ and His Church?

Therefore do not disappoint the Lord’s poor who ask you for the bread of the Gospel, the solid food of the sure and complete Catholic faith, so that they may know how to discern and choose in the face of other preachings and ideologies that are not the message of Jesus Christ and His Church’54. […] ‘The people entrusted to you seek you out to have solid certainties, words that do not pass away, absolute values. God is the only certainty… Dearly beloved, I ask you to defend this faith, to nourish it and strengthen it every day. You are the ‘professionals’ of the faith, the specialists of God. […] Let not the snare of doubt creep into you, nor allow weariness or disillusionment to tarnish that freshness of self-giving which the priestly vocation demands’55.”56

This is what is asked of us: that we be strong in the faith57.

▪ Enlightened

Because it is a learned, theological faith, capable of enlightening. That is why “the image of the Catholic priest would be incomplete […] if we did not highlight another very important requirement that the Church demands of him: knowledge. The Catholic priest is constituted ‘a teacher in Israel,’ for he has received from Christ the office and mission of teaching the truth. Lest we fall under God’s threat: Because you have rejected the knowledge of holy things, I will reject you from my priesthood58.”59 If this is said of every Catholic priest, how much more of us, to whom has been entrusted the work of the evangelization of culture, which of itself “demands a faith enlightened by continual reflection that confronts itself with the sources of the Church’s message, and a constant spiritual discernment sought in prayer.”60

Hence the need we have to form ourselves well in doctrine, never considering the task of intellectual formation finished. Indeed, Presbyterorum Ordinis, n. 19, exhorts us to remember that in the rite of ordination the bishop recommends to priests that they be “mature in knowledge,” because “piety without truth is weak, and truth without piety is arid and dry.”61

It is a fact that sacred studies often remain somewhat on the margins of our interior and personal life. Is it not the case that there are priests who live in constant contact with the mysteries of the faith, but who do not think about them, nor take care to penetrate them ever more deeply? How many spend their lives in the midst of divine realities: at the altar, in the confessional, at the ambo, preaching the Exercises, in permanent contact with the supernatural world, but because their faith is not learned and has no theological roots, they live hungry in the midst of abundance.

▪ Operative

Enlightened faith does not remain idle or sterile. It becomes operative. It is like a burning brazier that radiates light and heat. The faith of a priest becomes operative when it is capable of radiating the truth and expelling error.

In this sense, our first duty will always be the proclamation of the truth. St. John Paul II, in an address to a group of seminarians that might seem to have been addressed to a group of our own, told them: “One must prepare oneself to be able to illuminate in a Christian way the human situations of today, above all in the field of fundamental human rights, of the family, of youth, of the ideological and cultural sectors, etc., until one comes to permeate with the Gospel the nerve centers of our society.”62

The great love for the truth that we must profess brings as its counterpart combating error with all our strength, in the midst of a world that believes that error has the same rights among men as truth63. For this reason it is proper to every priest of the Institute to strive to be “men with their own discernment and abounding in spirit.”64

Hence St. John Paul II recommended to priests: “Sustained by the Word of God, the priest must reveal himself as a man of discernment and an authentic teacher of the faith.

Yes, he must be, above all in our age, a man of discernment. And this because, as we all know, the modern world has made great progress in the field of knowledge and human advancement, but it is also flooded with a great number of ideologies and pseudo-values which, through deceitful language, very often manage to seduce and mislead many of our contemporaries. Not only must one know how not to succumb to them –this is all too evident– but the function of Pastors is also to form the Christian judgment of the faithful65 so that they too may be capable of escaping the deceptive fascination of these new ‘idols.’”66

Likewise, Pope Pius XI had alluded to this subject when he wrote: “It is also the duty of the priest to clear minds of the errors and prejudices heaped up in them by the hatred of adversaries. To the modern soul, which anxiously seeks the truth, he must know how to demonstrate it with serene frankness; to the wavering, shaken by doubt, he must instill courage and confidence, guiding them with imperturbable firmness to the safe harbor of the faith, so that it may be embraced with full knowledge and firm adherence; to the onslaughts of error, perverse and obstinate, he must know how to offer resistance that is courageous and vigorous, and at the same time serene and well-founded.”67

When the faith of the priest is like this, solid, enlightened and operative, then it becomes communicative and conquering, with the overwhelming power that characterizes deep-rooted convictions.

“The good priest,” says St. Manuel González, “knows very well that, as long as he has eyes left to weep, hands with which to mortify himself and a body to afflict, he has no right to say that he has done all he had to do for the souls entrusted to him.”68

******

Especially in the exercise of faith while we walk in this valley of tears, we members of the Institute “must take the Most Holy Virgin as our example.”69 Mary’s earthly existence was a journey of faith, hope and charity, an exemplary path of holiness, which, like ours, knew the impulse of the fiat and the jubilation of the Magnificat, but knew how to maintain its recollection in daily activities and persevered in the deep night of the Passion until it shared in the joy of her Divine Son in the radiant dawn of the Resurrection.

“Live in faith and hope, even though it be in darkness, for in that darkness God protects the soul,”70 is the counsel of St. John of the Cross.

And when the trials against faith assail the soul with all their force, let us fly to the Heart of Jesus, who from the Tabernacle tells us: “Behold, I am with you… Yes, there He is. You already know who He is. He is Jesus Christ, the Son of God and of Mary, living, real, as He is in heaven, with eyes that look at you and smile at you; with a mouth that, without moving, speaks to you; with hands that are raised to bless you and lowered to rest upon your weary head; with arms that open to embrace you and, above all, with a Heart bearing thorns of forgetfulness, of ingratitude, of sacrilege… and flames of love… untiring, eternal…

Well then, all of that Jesus Christ, with His greatness as God and His eyes and His mouth and His hands and His Heart as man, with His virtues as Saint, with His merits as Redeemer, with His promises as Father, with His blood as Victim, is yours! Just like that, without hyperbole or exaggeration of language, He is yours! And that means that when you feel weak before the onslaught of your enemies, you have the right to count on His omnipotence. That when the ingratitude of men or your own sins make you weep, you have the right to prostrate yourself before Him, to embrace His knees and to ask Him, laying His blessed hand on your head, to forgive you and to forgive them. It means that when you find a heart, cold and hard as marble, that does not want to be converted, you have the right to take a little of the fire of His Heart and melt that stone. It means that when you sow and do not reap, when you preach and they do not listen to you, when you bless and they curse you, you have the right to ask Him for miracles of patience, of humility, of charity, of zeal… It means, in sum, that when bitterness chokes you and your hand cannot be raised to bless so many ungrateful people, and the tears dry up in your eyes and your strength fails you, and there is no part of your body left unhurt from so many blows, nor a living fiber in your heart from so much suffering, you have the right to ask Him to take you… to transplant you to heaven to live with Him forever, forever… Tell me, my brother, whoever you may be and whatever you may suffer, will you dare to say that you are alone?”71

For all these reasons we could conclude this writing by asking for the grace expressed in the counsel that St. Ignatius of Antioch, bishop and martyr, wrote to his friend St. Polycarp of Smyrna (also a bishop and martyr), to whom he said the following: “The times require of you that you strive to reach God, together with those entrusted to you, as the pilot longs for favorable winds and the sailor caught in the storm sighs for the harbor. Be sober, as an athlete of God. The prize is incorruption and eternal life, of whose existence you too are convinced… Do not let yourself be intimidated by those who give themselves the airs of men worthy of all credit and teach doctrines foreign to the faith. For your part, stand firm like an anvil struck by the hammer. It is proper to a great athlete to be flayed and yet to conquer. How much more, then, must we endure all things for God, so that He too may endure us! Be even more diligent than you are. Understand the times fully. Wait for Him who is above time, the timeless; the invisible, who for our sake became visible; the impalpable, the impassible, who for our sake became passible; Him who in every possible way suffered for us … Stand firm!”72

May the Mother of the Incarnate Word and our Mother grant us the grace to remain firm in the faith, as hers was at the foot of the Cross. Amen.

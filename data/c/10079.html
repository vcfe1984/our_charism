If God the Father Almighty, Creator of an ordered and good world, takes care of all His creatures, why does evil exist?

It is a question as pressing as it is inevitable, as painful as it is mysterious, to which no simple answer can be given. Only the whole of the Christian faith constitutes the compelling answer to such a question: the goodness of creation, the drama of sin, the patient love of God who comes to meet man with His covenants, with the redemptive Incarnation of His Son, with the gift of the Spirit, with the redemptive Consecration, with the power of the sacraments, with the call to a blessed life which creatures are invited to accept freely, but which, also freely, by a terrible mystery, they can refuse to reject. An author says: There is not a single feature of the Christian message that is not in part an answer to the question of evil.

But why did God not create a world so perfect that no evil could exist in it? St. Thomas teaches that God, in His infinite power, could always create something better.

But, as the Catechism of the Catholic Church says, God in His infinite wisdom and goodness willed to create a world in a state of journeying toward its ultimate perfection. In God’s plan this becoming brings with it, together with the appearance of certain beings, the disappearance of others; together with the more perfect, the less perfect; together with the constructions of nature, also its destructions. That is, together with physical good –as St. Thomas says in the Contra Gentiles– there therefore also exists physical evil.

There is no getting around it: angels and men, intelligent and free creatures, must journey toward their ultimate destiny by free choice and a love of preference. That is why they can go astray, and in fact that is how moral evil entered the world, incomparably more serious than physical evil.

That is why God is in no way, neither directly nor indirectly, the cause of moral evil; nevertheless He permits it, respecting the freedom of His creature, and mysteriously knows how to draw good from it; as St. Augustine repeated so many times: For Almighty God, because He is supremely good, would never allow any evil to exist in His works if He were not powerful and good enough to bring good out of evil itself.

And so it is that, with time, one can discover that God, in His almighty providence, can draw a good from the consequences of an evil, even a moral evil, caused by His creatures: It was not you, says Joseph to his brothers, who sent me here, but God … although you meant to do me harm, God meant it for good, to keep alive a numerous people1. From the greatest moral evil ever committed, the rejection and death of the Son of God, caused by the sins of all men, God, by the superabundance of His grace2, drew the greatest of goods: the glorification of Christ and our redemption.

All things work together for the good of those who love God3, St. Paul teaches the Romans. The testimony of the saints confirms this truth:

– Thus St. Catherine of Siena says to those who are scandalized and rebel against what happens to them: “Everything proceeds from love; everything is ordered to the salvation of man; God does nothing except for this end.”

– And St. Thomas More, shortly before his martyrdom, consoles his daughter: “Nothing can happen to me that God does not will. And everything He wills, however bad it may seem to us, is in reality what is best.”

– And Julian of Norwich4: “I understood, then, by the grace of God, that I had to hold firmly to the faith and believe no less firmly that all things will turn out for good.”

We Christians firmly believe that God is the Lord of the world and of history. But we also confess that the ways of His providence are often unknown to us. And only at the end, when our partial knowledge comes to an end, when we see God face to face, will we fully know the ways by which, even through the dramas of evil and sin, God will have led His creation to the rest of that definitive glory for the sake of which He created heaven and earth.

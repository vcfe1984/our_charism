Homily in a Contemplative Monastery of the Servants

[Exordium]Dear Sisters: First of all, I would like to thank you very much for the invitation to celebrate Holy Mass. It truly gives me great pleasure to be able to do so.

On this occasion I would simply like to reflect with you on one line of our Directory of Spirituality, in number 52. There, speaking of women religious as spouses of Christ, it says that: all her wealth –that of the religious sister– consists in giving herself to the Word.

And I would like to place the emphasis on that giving herself in the sentence. Notice that it is a reflexive verb, that is, it applies to oneself. Therefore, it is not only a matter of giving something exterior or accessory, such as handing over goods or academic degrees, leaving one’s own country, or doing some exterior work without greater commitment, etc. It is rather a matter of giving yourselves. And this giving yourselves is a giving with all the powers of body and soul, with all the finitude of one’s own being, with all the affections that lie in the folds of the heart, with all one’s past, present and future, with all one’s talents, and with all one’s miseries too. And to do this with all one’s strength, with all one’s mind, with all one’s heart, with all enthusiasm, with all trust, with all generosity and promptness, and with a great joy founded on the faith that does not disappoint. And to do this once and for all, without seeking to recover what has been given by looking for compensations or settling down and making a “nest” in things that are not God1.

Even more, one must want to “live permanently” in this attitude, “without diminutions or retractions, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without withdrawals or sluggishness,” as the same Directory says in number 73.

That is, this giving of oneself is emptying oneself into the hands of Christ. Let Him dispose, let Him decide, let Him protect, let Him mold the soul as He pleases, let Him use us as He wishes, because it will always be –beyond all doubt– for the good of our soul.

Put in other words, that giving oneself to the Word is to empty oneself of self in order to be filled with Him. It is to have nothing in order to have everything in Him (as St. John of the Cross teaches). That is, the religious empties herself of her frailty to find in Him all her strength; she empties herself of her weakness to be filled with his omnipotence; she empties herself of her ignorance to be filled with his immeasurable wisdom; she empties herself of her disorders to be filled with his peace; she empties herself of her misery to be filled with his infinite riches; she empties herself of human horizons to be filled with supernatural life; she empties herself of attachments of the heart in order to run without obstacles to the divine union.

And to do it only out of love for Him. Because the religious gives herself in spousal love to the Incarnate Word, to no one else. And without that love, which is the theological virtue of charity and which must permeate all her acts, they are –as St. Paul says– like sounding bronze or a clanging cymbal2.

That giving oneself to the Word is to live in the attitude that the great doctor of the Church, St. Teresa of Jesus, declares in her poem, saying:

Behold here my heart,

I place it in your palm;

my body, my life and soul,

my inmost being and my love.

Sweet spouse and redemption,

since I offered myself as yours,

what do you bid be done with me?

Notice that the Saint says what do you bid be done with me? Because, since love must be placed more in deeds than in words3, as we meditate each year in our Spiritual Exercises, one must give oneself to the Word by giving oneself to others. That is, in the concrete practice of charity, living in permanent availability for whatever God asks.

Because love of God and love of neighbor cannot be separated! The two “merge into each other”4; they are inseparable5.

Our Directory of Works of Mercy has a magnificent paragraph, taken from the “Encyclical Deus Caritas Est”, which reads: “If in life attention to the other is omitted altogether, wishing only to be “pious” and to fulfill one’s “religious duties,” then the relationship with God also withers. It will be merely a “correct” relationship, but without love. Only readiness to help one’s neighbor, to show him love, makes us sensitive to God as well. Only service of neighbor opens our eyes to what God does for us and to how much he loves us”6. And then further on it goes on to say: “Love grows through love.”

Therefore, my dear sisters, to serve one’s brothers and sisters –all of humanity from the cloister, as well as the sister who lives with me or the people who come to the parlor– is to serve Christ himself. And it is by giving yourselves to your brothers and sisters that you give yourselves to Christ himself. One cannot say “I give myself entirely to the Incarnate Word” without giving oneself to others. Rather, out of love for God one must give oneself to one’s brothers and sisters. Which is nothing other than imitating Christ, who came into the world for all men7.

Our Proper Law explicitly commands us “to love in deed and in truth the concrete man who is in need”8. Understand here not only the poor man who comes to the monastery door looking for food, but also those whom we always have with us… look at one another, at those of your own family, at the priests, the seminarians…

How can our “existence be a concrete trace that the Trinity leaves in history”9 without loving and serving those who share history with us? How could we “be like another incarnation of the Word”10 if we do not cherish in our heart his same sentiments of compassion toward our brothers and sisters? How could a Servant say that she loves the Incarnate Word and not be available for generous and disinterested service?

We must love as Christ loved us: that is, to the end11, to the point of giving his life for us12, making himself a servant13; submitting even to death, and death on a cross14; being light15among men (not sowing darkness); praying to the Father for us16; walking the extra mile17 and giving us even what we do not know how to ask for.18 You, who are women –and contemplatives!– make use of the fine sensitivity God has given you to lavish that charity in a thousand delicate gestures and details.

Look, I have always liked very much the example of St. Catherine Labouré. She entered the convent and as a novice –nine days after entering the novitiate– the Blessed Virgin appeared to her, and she spoke with her for two hours kneeling at her feet, resting her hands on the lap of Our Lady, who was seated in the chapel. Then, in another apparition, Our Lady asked her to have made what we know today as the Miraculous Medal. That happened when she was 24 years old. St. Catherine died at 70. During 46 years of religious life no one (except her spiritual director) knew that she had seen Our Lady and that she had been entrusted with having the miraculous medal made, which worked so many wonders. What did she do her whole life? Take care of old people!, mend their clothes, wash them, feed them, supervise their recreation, scold them when they misbehaved, deal with the kitchen… She rose at dawn and was the last to go to bed, only to give herself to the Word, concretely, by giving herself to others without restrictions, without conditions… It is not your lot to take care of old people, but it is your lot to pray and immolate yourselves silently for fallen humanity, and to do so hiddenly and in the face of the incomprehension of so many, only to give yourselves to the Word.

Do you want an even more sublime example? Look at the most beautiful example of the True Servant of the Lord who was our Most Holy Mother. With her heart and soul wholly set on God, she sensed within herself the needs of others and sought with maternal wisdom to attend to them promptly, generously, without trying to be noticed, without seeking herself. She did not limit herself to words of understanding, but committed herself personally to authentic assistance. “Our Lady’s service is marked principally by her humility”19. So too must be the service of every Servant.

The Virgin Mary rendered her service without becoming irritated by fatigue. In fact, on arriving at the house of her cousin Elizabeth after a long journey on foot, the Blessed Virgin intoned the Magnificat! Our Lady rejoices in the service she can render. Therefore, from our Mother, the Model of every Servant, you too must learn to serve with joy, with deep serenity –because in the end the work is God’s–, seeking always to be instruments of peace, of concord, each from the post God has assigned her. Without fear of sacrifice, with total availability, with all “spiritual fervor and joy, even if it falls to you to sow in tears”20, as our Proper Law says. Because the sacrifice of the spouse of a Crucified Christ must be a sacrifice of love without restrictions, without withdrawals, but rather a holocaust.

You who have the immense grace of living in the cloister have concrete, very concrete opportunities to distinguish yourselves by service to others, especially to the Religious Family.

St. Peter Julian Eymard told his religious: “love your brothers above all. But you must love them out of pure love of God, and love them in God, and not for the gratitude and response you expect from them. Render them all the services you can within due order. And whenever you render them, do it for that motive alone. Then you will not complain that they do not respond; since you did not act for men but for God, you have no need of human gratitude. To try to draw personal profit from what was done out of duty is to destroy charity and deprive God of his glory. I am inclined to say -says the saint- that you should take excessive displays of gratitude as an insult. To render a service is to fulfill a duty”21. And if the Saint says this to his religious, who were adorers of the Blessed Sacrament, how much more must it be said of those who bear the name Servants.

Only by giving yourselves to others out of love for Christ will you find all wealth. That is, only thus will you come to be those “chalices full of Christ that pour out their superabundance upon others”22 of which our Constitutions speak. Only thus will you be able to show with your lives that Christ lives23.

[Peroratio] My dear Sisters, I know that you already do so, but I want to entrust to you very especially the task of praying for our beloved Religious Family, for its protection, for its unity, for its fidelity to the very rich patrimony it has received. Pray also that we may be ever more faithful to the vows we have pronounced. That is, that “we may carry out with greater perfection the service of God and of men,” that is, “with an ever more sincere and intense love,” and so “prolong the Incarnation of the Word ‘in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man’”24, for to all this we committed ourselves when we recited our formula of profession before God.

May our Heavenly Mother, the Servant of the Lord par excellence, to whom I entrust you with all my heart, obtain this grace for us.

In the Name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.

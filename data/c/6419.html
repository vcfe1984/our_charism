[Exordium] We have just heard one of the Gospel passages that has most moved the world down through the centuries: the eight beatitudes of the Sermon on the Mount.

Many years ago now, Pope St. Paul VI spoke of this passage, presenting it as “one of the most surprising and most positively revolutionary texts: Who, in the course of history, would have dared to proclaim ‘blessed’ the poor in spirit, the afflicted, the meek, the hungry, those who thirst for justice, the merciful, the pure of heart, the peacemakers, the persecuted, the insulted…? Those words, sown in a society based on force, on power, on wealth, on violence, on abuse, could have been interpreted as a program of baseness and apathy unworthy of man; and yet they were proclamations of a new ‘civilization of love’”1.

The Gospel program of the beatitudes is transcendental for the life of the Christian. It is certainly a lofty and demanding program, but at the same time it is fascinating. This is the program of life followed by all the saints whom we celebrate today. That is the narrow path along which they walked.

Our beloved John Paul II said that “each of the eight beatitudes points to that goal beyond time [holiness, the heaven to which we are called]. But at the same time each of the beatitudes affects man directly and fully in his earthly and temporal existence. All the situations that make up the whole of human destiny and of man’s conduct are concretely included, by their own name, in the beatitudes. These point out and guide in particular the conduct of the disciples of Christ, of his witnesses”2.

That is why today we are going to reflect on the beatitudes of the contemplative life. For whoever has understood the eight beatitudes proposed by the Incarnate Word and resolves to practice them has understood the whole Gospel and can make it a reality; that is, can attain holiness.

The Church has always repeated them and does so now as well, addressing them above all to contemplative souls, of whom the whole world has great need. Listen well.

1. The beatitudes of the contemplative life

Jesus proclaims: Blessed are those who mourn: that is, the afflicted, those who feel physical suffering or moral sorrow, those who go through terrible solitudes and dark nights; for they shall be comforted.

That is why the Monastic Rule of the Contemplative Branch says that contemplatives consecrate themselves in a particular way to penance and consider it a special gift of their vocation to share voluntarily in the redemptive suffering of Christ3. Why? Because “in suffering there is concealed a particular power that draws a person interiorly close to Christ”4. This is the consolation of those who mourn. And so, says St. John of Ávila, “The song and the laughter of the religious woman must be to weep for her betrothed, Jesus Christ. In the choir, in the garden, in the refectory, in the cell and everywhere, she must go about sighing for her Spouse Jesus Christ; and this must be her office and this must be her song, and when she is singing in choir with the sweetest melody, her heart must be sighing with an inner and very deep sigh for her most beloved Spouse, whose memory and desire must never depart from her heart. With this she must fall asleep, and of this she must dream while sleeping, and this must be the first thing to come to her mind on waking; her heart always melting in love for him, and her memory occupied with nothing else but her beloved Spouse”5.

Blessed are the pure of heart. Jesus assures us that those who practice this beatitude shall see God. For “There is no holiness without purity of soul: ‘holiness means purity’6”7 says our Directory of Spirituality. And it is “here −in the contemplative life− that one acquires those eyes whose serene gaze wounds the Spouse with love and whose purity makes it possible to see God”8.

Blessed are the merciful for they shall obtain mercy. St. Edith Stein −that marvelous contemplative− who left the cloister only to be martyred, wrote this beautiful paragraph: “Do you hear the groaning of the wounded on the battlefields of the East and the West? You are not a doctor, nor a nurse, and you cannot bind their wounds. You are enclosed in your cell and cannot reach them. Do you hear the agonized cry of the dying? You would like to be a priest and be at their side. Are you moved by the weeping of widows and orphans? You would like to be a consoling angel and help them. Look at the Crucified One. If you are united to him as a bride in the authentic fulfillment of your holy vows, your blood is his precious Blood. United to him you are omnipresent as he is. You can help like the doctor, the nurse or the priest, here or there. In the power of the Cross you can be on every front, in every place of affliction; your merciful love will carry you everywhere, the love of the divine Heart, which everywhere pours out its most precious Blood, Blood that soothes, sanctifies and saves”9. How important and how necessary is the office of souls like yours, who implore mercy for the other members of the Institute and for the whole world! Never abandon your post… the Lord rewards the merciful with mercy itself, with joy, with peace.

Blessed are the peaceful, the peacemakers. This is an exceptional category of men and women whom Jesus proclaims blessed. And in this contemplatives have a role without equal, since they consecrate their prayer and sacrifice to the great concerns and intentions of the Church, especially for those gifts which no merit, but only prayer and penance, can obtain from God. In their prayers, in their hearts, in their hands −I would say− they carry the lot and the destinies of the Church and of the whole world.

Blessed are the meek. These words that came from the mouth of the “good Word of the good Father”10, who, preaching the kingdom of God, also said to his disciples: Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart11, must resound in the contemplative soul with singular power. This is what John Paul II teaches us when he says: “Love him as befits your spousal condition: taking on his very sentiments; sharing his way of life, made up of humility and meekness, of love and mercy, of service and joyful availability, of tireless zeal for the glory of the Father and the salvation of the human race”12. Such must be the meekness of a contemplative soul, to the point that one should be able to apply to it that paragraph of the Directory of Spirituality which says: “Look at the fools! Stones are thrown at them and they kiss the hand that throws them; people laugh at them and mock them and they laugh too, like children who do not understand; they are beaten, persecuted and martyred, but they give thanks to God who found them worthy”13.

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for justice. A thirst for justice that translates into a thirst for holiness. Jesus has called you to the consecrated life and especially to the contemplative life precisely so that you may be “deiform”14, as St. John of the Cross says. And if that must be the principal work [in which] every Christian [is employed], all the more so for one who glories in being called a monk15. But the personal work of holiness is necessarily joined to the ‘thirst for souls’, which makes the contemplative soul offer itself to God so that through it all the members of the Church may grow in holiness.

Blessed are the poor in spirit. This is the first of the eight beatitudes that Jesus proclaimed in the Sermon on the Mount. “The poverty embraced by Christ increases the freedom of spirit and the princely spirit that the religious must possess by his consecration”16. That is why here I would like to repeat the counsel the Mystical Doctor gives to a Carmelite nun, which says: “God wants the religious to be a religious in such a way that he has done with everything and everything is done with for him; because he himself is the one who wants to be his wealth, consolation and delightful glory”17. The poor in spirit are those who are most open to God and to the “wonders of God.” Hearts open to God are, for that very reason, more open to men. They are ready to help selflessly. Ready to share what they have. And what is the reward of the poor monk? Jesus tells us: to the poor in spirit belongs the kingdom of heaven. This is the reward that Jesus promises them. More cannot be promised.

Together with the first, I now want to cite the last beatitude, the one concerning those who suffer persecution for the sake of justice, those who are persecuted for bearing witness to the faith: these are authentic poor in spirit, and that is why Jesus also says that theirs is the kingdom of heaven. How many monasteries, how many contemplative souls guarded the deposit of faith entrusted to them in the midst of the bloodiest persecutions, and shored up the sound doctrine of the Church in the face of a multitude of errors and confusions18! A task that is not foreign to you. And particularly in these times the need to pray and do penance for the members of the persecuted Church becomes urgent; for the conversion of sinners, above all of consecrated souls and of the persecutors of the Church.

[Peroratio] These eight beatitudes are the precious pearl for which all the saints whom we celebrate today left everything. If you want to be happy in the contemplative life, seek identification with Christ. It is true that the beatitudes are not commandments. But they are certainly all contained in the commandment of love, which is the ‘first’ and the ‘greatest.’ The beatitudes are like the portrait of Christ, a summary of his life, and that is why they are also presented as a “program of life” for each one of us.

Love your vocation very much. And from here, from the silence of the cloister and the hidden life, proclaim the preeminence of the Lord over all reality; make manifest in your life and with your life the full relevance of the beatitudes; be ‘the revolutionaries’ of the civilization of love, as St. Paul VI said.

May the Virgin, Queen of all saints, help you to be very holy, since it is for that end that the Lord has gathered you together, as St. Teresa said19.

We may ask ourselves, as St. Thomas Aquinas does: which is the greatest of the religious vows, the one that far surpasses the others? It is the vow of obedience.

St. Gregory the Great, in his work Moralia, says that the religious vows are equivalent to holocausts, and he adds: “Obedience is rightly preferred to sacrifices, because in sacrifice the flesh of others is offered, whereas in obedience one’s own will is immolated.” In obedience a more perfect holocaust is offered: what is most intimate, what is most our own.

St. Luigi Orione said: “Obedience is the fragrance of sacrifice”1.

For three reasons this vow is the most perfect:

1. Because in it a greater good is offered to God

Our own will is offered, which is superior to one’s own body, offered to God through chastity, and to external goods, offered through the vow of poverty. We have said many times that religious life is a martyrdom, a holocaust; and that holocaust is more perfect when what is most one’s own and the best one has is offered.

This is the reason why what is fulfilled through obedience is more beautiful and more meritorious for the soul than what is done according to one’s own will. It is very important to be clear about this; otherwise, religious life is distorted. If one thinks that being poor is more important than being obedient, one does not understand the core of religious life. The three vows are important, but the principal one—the one that best manifests the holocaust and the sacrifice of the religious oblation—is obedience.

That is why, saving the essence of the other vows (which does not mean that they need not be kept), obedience comes first. One could not, for example, under the pretext of poverty, do something contrary to obedience, such as giving away things belonging to the community. To go against obedience is to go against the very essence of religious life and against all the other vows at the same time.

Such is the admonition of St. Jerome to Rusticus, a monk: “My words are meant to teach you not to trust your own judgment.” And he adds: “Do not do what you want; eat what you have been ordered; accept what has been granted you; wear the habits you have been given.”

St. Thomas Aquinas adds: “Not even fasting is pleasing to God if it is done by one’s own will,” as is evident from the words of the prophet Isaiah, who showed the complaint of the people: “Why do we fast and You do not see it? Why do we mortify ourselves and You do not know it?” (cf. Is 58:3).

2. Because the vow of obedience embraces the other two

The religious, even though bound by special vows to chastity and poverty, lives these under the form of obedience, “which embraces many other things along with it.” Obedience informs and orders the other vows.

3. Because it is ordered directly to the end of religious life

The vow of obedience properly extends to the acts closest to the end of religious life. The more proper a thing is to the end, the better it is. That is why the vow of obedience is the most essential to the religious state.

If someone—should the case arise—observed poverty and chastity, even under vow, and did not observe obedience, let him know that he is not a religious. Obedience is preferred even to virginity, as St. Augustine of Hippo affirms: “No one, as far as I know, has ever dared to prefer virginity to the monastic life of obedience”2.

This doctrine is very important and also very beautiful. Just as among the theological virtues charity is the most excellent, so too among the religious vows that of obedience is the most precious, the one with which—so to speak—we must most fall in love and strive to live well. It must be the watchword of our religious life.

St. Ignatius of Loyola says:

“And although I desire for you all perfection in all the virtues and spiritual graces, it is true—as you will have heard from me at other times—that I desire it more particularly in obedience than in any other… because obedience is a virtue which alone implants the other virtues in the soul and, once they are impressed there, preserves them (St. Gregory); and as long as it flourishes, all the others will be seen to flourish and bear the fruit that I desire in your souls and that is asked by Him who redeemed through obedience the world lost through the lack of it: made obedient unto death, even death on a cross (cf. Phil 2:8).”

“In other religious orders we can bear to be surpassed in fasts, vigils and other austerities; but in the purity and perfection of obedience, with the true resignation of our wills and the abnegation of our judgments, those who serve God our Lord in this Society must distinguish themselves, never looking at the person whom one obeys, but at Christ our Lord, for whose sake one obeys.”

He adds the fundamental principle:

“Not because the Superior is very prudent, nor very good, nor very well endowed with other gifts, but because he holds His place and authority, he must be obeyed, for the eternal Truth says: He who hears you hears me; and he who despises you despises me (cf. Lk 10:16).”

And quoting St. Paul the Apostle:

Servants, obey your masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, in simplicity of heart, as to Christ (Eph 6:5). Whatever you do, do it willingly, as one who serves the Lord and not men (Col 3:23-24).

St. Bernard of Clairvaux also says: “Whether it be God, or man, His vicar, who gives any command, it must be obeyed with equal care, when the man does not command things against God”3.

For his part, St. John Bosco affirmed: “If you practice obedience in the way indicated, I can assure you, in the name of the Lord, that you will spend a peaceful and happy life in the congregation. But from the day that, setting obedience aside, you act only according to your whim, you will begin to feel regret for your state”4.

And St. Francis de Sales said: “Everything is safe in obedience; everything is suspect outside of it”5.

Finally, the Virgin Mary is our perfect model of obedience. She always obeyed with submission of judgment, joy of heart and promptness in execution. She obeyed St. Joseph, whom she saw as the head of the home and the representative of God’s authority. She did not argue with his orders nor contradict his directions. She never considered small the things commanded by obedience. She called herself the handmaid of the Lord (cf. Lk 1:38).

Let whoever has made a vow of obedience see what he has bound himself to. There is no other way of fulfilling it than to live totally surrendered—in freedom, will and one’s own opinion—to legitimate authority. In imitation of Mary, he must not stop to examine the reasons for what is commanded, but know whether it is commanded or not, and act without delay.

Make a careful examination on this point: analyze your obedience, compare it with Mary’s, and deduce from it what you must do.

CHAPTER 1: THE MYSTERY OF THE WORD

Introduction

  1. We want to be anchored in the most holy mystery of the Incarnation, which is “the first and fundamental mystery of Jesus Christ”1, presently at work, and from there to launch ourselves boldly to restore all things in Christ (Eph 1:10). We want to be another Incarnation of the Word in order to incarnate him in all that is human.
  2. Our religion “is a doctrine, but above all it is an event: the event of the Incarnation, Jesus, the God-Man who has recapitulated the Universe in himself (cf. Eph 1:10)”2. “It is impossible to find anything like the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity and of the Incarnation”3.
  3. Well then, from the fact of the redemptive Incarnation we want to draw ever new light and strength, since Jesus Christ is the inexhaustible source of Being, of Truth, of Goodness, of Beauty, of Life, of Love.

    Article 1: Primacy of Jesus Christ
  4. We confess the preeminence of Christ, even as man, over all creation. A primacy that Christ has over the souls and over the bodies of the members of his Mystical Body and also over all men of all times –he is the Head of all– even of those not predestined, who will only cease to be potential members of the Body of Christ when they leave this world. The Son is “the art of the Father”4.
  5. We confess that Christ is Head of the Church5 and of all men6, and that over all he has a threefold primacy: of order, of perfection and of power. He has priority of order, since by his nearness to God his grace is the highest and the first, though not temporally; because all who receive grace receive it in relation to his: Those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, so that he might be the firstborn among many brothers (Rom 8:29). He has priority of perfection, because he possesses the fullness of all graces: We have seen him… full of grace and truth (Jn 1:14). He has priority in power, since he has all power to communicate grace and glory to all the members of his Body: From his fullness we have all received grace upon grace (Jn 1:16).
  6. That is why the heresies of all times have, and will have, a common denominator: to diminish the dignity of Jesus Christ. St. Leo the Great pointed out: “Hardly any (heretic) has been deceived without having abandoned belief in the truth of the two natures joined in the one person of Christ”7; and St. Thomas teaches that “when one considers the errors of the heretics as a whole, it is manifest that their principal aim is to diminish Christ in his dignity”8. Finally, “there is no other mystery apart from Christ”9. And hence St. Philip Neri teaches that “whoever wants something other than Christ does not know what he wants. Whoever asks for something other than Christ does not know what he asks. Whoever works, and not for Christ, does not know what he is doing”10.

    Article 2: Love for the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit
  7. The contemplation of the mystery of the Incarnation nourishes love for the Most Holy Trinity: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who brought about the Incarnation; and it sets us aflame with ardent love for the Word who “for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven”11; and for the Word in his preexistence, his entrance into the world, his earthly life –works and teachings–, his departure from the world, his mystical life and his second Coming.


    Article 3: Preexistence of the Word

  8. a) Eternal Person. The person of the Word exists from all eternity: in the beginning was the Word… and he was in the beginning with God (Jn 1:1-2). In confessing the existence of the Word as prior to the Blessed Virgin and prior to the creation of the world, we want to base our spirituality on the absolute of God, before whom all is as nothing12. The exhortation of St. Cyprian “to put nothing before Christ”13 must always be of capi­tal importance for us, convinced that “God loves Christ more than all things”14, as must the conviction of St. Teresa: “God alone suffices”15. We want in everything and through everything to give primacy to the spiritual and to surrender ourselves in holy abandonment to the will of God’s good pleasure, since, in response to God’s revelation, “man must abandon himself entirely to God”16.

  9. b) Distinct Person. And the Word was with God (Jn 1:1). The Word is the “Word that proceeded from silence”17. The personal distinction of the Word from the Father and the Holy Spirit impels us to let our whole life bear the Trinitarian imprint, which is the greatest mystery of God, is the fullness of man and is “the substance of the New Testament,” in which men, through the Son made flesh, have access in the Holy Spirit to the Father and become partakers of the divine nature (2 Pet 1:4). It must be a badge of honor to confess “the distinction of the persons, the unity of their nature and the equality in majesty”18.
  10. c) Divine Person. And the Word was God (Jn 1:1). We recognize in him the fullness of the divinity and all the attributes of the divine being and acting, and that all things were made through him and without him nothing was made of all that has been made (Jn 1:3). In a particular way, we want to see ourselves and every man in him, and to see ourselves created “in the image and likeness of God”19, and furthermore, “through him and before him to understand that man is unique and unrepeatable; someone eternally conceived and eternally chosen; someone called and named by his own name”20.

    CHAPTER 2: THE MYSTERY OF THE INCARNATE WORD

    Article 1: His first Coming

  11. And the Word became flesh (Jn 1:14). The work of the Incarnation is common to the three divine persons, but it is attributed to the Holy Spirit because: a) its cause is the supreme love of God: God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son (Jn 3:16); b) the human nature was not assumed by its own merit, but by grace alone, which is attributed to the Holy Spirit: There is a diversity of graces, but the Holy Spirit is the same (1 Cor 12:4); c) Jesus Christ is the only Holy One and Son of God: what will be born of you will be Holy, he will be called Son of God (Lk 1:35); and in him we are made children of God: because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying: “Abba! Father!” (Gal 4:6), and we are sanctified, since he is the Spirit of sanctification (Rom 1:4).
  12. Grounded in the mystery of the Incarnation, wrought by the Spirit in the Virgin Mary, we must always sing the mercies of God21 because “through the Incarnation of the Word the immortality of happiness becomes believable”22; we must have a clear awareness that without Jesus Christ we can do nothing23, and we must strive with all our strength always to advance in virtue.
  13. Hence we must act moved by the Holy Spirit, being supremely docile to him. For this, we want to have three things:
  14. a) Attentiveness to the inspirations of the Holy Spirit, like the Virgin, to whom the angel Gabriel could present himself and speak because of her habitual attentiveness to the motions of the Spirit24; for this one must work against habitual dissipation, lack of mortification and disordered affections.
  15. b) Discernment of spirits, in order to accept and second the motions of the Holy Spirit and reject those of the evil spirit; being very prudent, after the example of the Virgin, who wondered what that greeting might mean (Lk 1:29).
  16. c) Docility and promptness in carrying out what the Holy Spirit asks, like the Virgin, who set out in haste (Lk 1:39), because “slow calculations are foreign to the grace of the Holy Spirit”25; always working against the temptation of delay, against the fear of sacrifice and of total self-giving, and against the temptation to take back what we have given by seeking compensations or settling down, making a “nest”26 in things that are not God.
  17. Being attuned to the Holy Spirit must move us to live the evangelical beatitudes, to let ourselves be led by the action of the gifts of the Spirit and to be fruitful in producing his fruits: charity, joy, peace, longanimity, affability, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance (Gal 5:22).
  18. We must also consider the “yes” of the Blessed Virgin: Be it done unto me according to your word (Lk 1:38). The consent of the Mother of God shows us that she was not “a merely passive instrument, but a cooperator in human salvation through free faith and obedience”27. And we too must not be passive instruments.
  19. The Virgin gave her “yes” as a slave: Behold the slave of the Lord (Lk 1:38); and God looked upon the lowliness of his slave (Lk 1:48); and then the Word took the form of a slave, being made like unto men (Phil 2:7) in her most pure womb. That is why our spirituality wants to be marked, with special emphasis, by professing a fourth vow of Marian slavery, according to the spirit of St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, so that our whole life may be Marianized. When speaking of the vows we shall go more deeply into our Marian spirituality.


    a) The divinity of Jesus

  20. From the beginning, the confession of St. Peter: You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God (Mt 16:16), has been central in the Catholic faith, and it must be central in our spirituality. “We have only been saved if Jesus Christ shares the full divine life in his person,” taught St. John Paul II28; and, on another occasion, he said that in confessing that Jesus is Lord (Rom 10:9)29 “we break with everything else that claims to set itself up as absolute, and we destroy the idols of money, of power, of sex, those which hide in ideologies, ‘secular religions’ with totalitarian ambitions”30.

  21. He is the “Way” to go to the Father, and no one goes to the Father except through him31. He has the only name by which we can be saved (Acts 4:12). He is the one who makes the Church a “sacrament, that is, a sign and instrument of intimate union with God and of the unity of the whole human race”32. He is the one who upholds all the dogmas of the Church, since he is “the truth that includes all the others”33. He is the one who shows us the primacy and the weight of eternity over every temporal reality.
  22. Confessing the divinity of Jesus Christ must move us, furthermore, to the practice of the virtues of transcendence: faith, hope and charity, and, from these, to the urgency of unceasing prayer and contemplation, and to the awareness of the need for the active and passive purifications of sense and spirit.
  23. Contemplating that the Incarnate Word is “God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father”34, we want to set aside every stance of pure humanism (humanism without transcendence), which ends up annihilating man, and every false kenotism (self-emptying) which, under the pretext of going down to what is lower, empties itself of what is higher, as do, for example, those who, in order to “be in the world,” give in to the spirit of the world, emptying themselves, and forget that Christians are in the world, but are not of the world (Jn 17:16). For “if God were completely lacking to man, man would cease to exist. The glory of God is that man live, but the life of man is to see God”35.


    b) The humanity of Jesus

  24. St. John the Baptist announced Jesus: After me comes a man (Jn 1:30); St. Peter denied him before the maidservant: I do not know that man (Mt 26:72); and Pilate presented him to the crowd: Ecce homo (Jn 19:5). We confess that Jesus is not only God, but also true man, as the centurion did: Truly this man was the Son of God (Mk 15:39).
  25. Becoming man is “the first and fundamental mystery of Jesus Christ”36. “God was never so close to man –and man was never so close to God– as precisely at that moment: at the instant of the mystery of the Incarnation”37.
  26. The Word, who expresses not only the Father, but also expresses the being and acting of all creatures38 and, in a particular way, of every human being, man or woman, “fully reveals man to man himself and discloses to him the sublimity of his vocation,” to such a degree that “the mystery of man only becomes clear in the mystery of the Incarnate Word”39.
  1. From this derives, at its very root, one of the principal elements of our spirituality. Terence said: “I am a man, and nothing human is alien to me”40. With all the more reason, because of the Incarnation, nothing authentically human is foreign to us. Therefore, we must work to prolong the In­carnation in all of reality, specifically by evangelizing the culture, because “no human activity is foreign to the Gospel”41.
  2. For us, Christ is mysteriously identified with each man, and each man and all men can only be understood in Christ Jesus, knowing that “it was necessary for the human race that God become man in order to show the human race the dignity of human nature”42. He will say on the day of Judgment: for I was hungry and you gave me to eat… (Mt 25:35). The poor are Christ: “they represent the person of the Son of God”43; pilgrims are Christ: “let them be received as Christ himself”44; children are Christ: Whoever receives them receives me (Mt 18:5); in every man there is “Jesus hidden in the depths of his soul”45. That is why St. Augustine said: “in him we are christs and Christ”46.
  3. If every Christian should pass through this earth in imitation of the Incarnate God, with much greater reason must we live this reality as religious of the family “of the Incarnate Word.” And not only must we ourselves live the life of Christ, seeking God in all things, but we must spread the life of Christ in others, and inform with it the cultures of men in order to elevate man: “that man feels inclined to attach himself to things inferior to God, taking them as his end, is because he is ignorant of the dignity of his own nature”47.
  4. This mysterious and joyful reality of being “other Christs” (central in our spirituality) leads St. Paul to invent words to express it: conmortui (2 Tim 2:11), consepulti (Rom 6:14), conresuscitati (Eph 2:6), convivificati (Eph 2:5), complantati (Rom 6:5), convivemus (2 Tim 2:11), consedere (Eph 2:6)… to the point of exclaiming: It is no longer I who live, it is Christ who lives in me (Gal 2:20). St. Elizabeth of the Trinity says very beautifully that we must be “like a new incarnation of the Word,” “like another humanity of his,” so that the Father may see in us “nothing but the beloved Son”48.
  5. We are already, in principle, Christ through Baptism, but it is our task to be so in fullness, dying and living, as St. Paul says: consider yourselves dead to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus (Rom 6:11), and as St. Peter says: He bore our sins in his body upon the wood, so that, dead to sin, we might live for righteousness… (1 Pet 2:24).
  6. Dying:
    – to sin and to the works of the flesh, since in Christ we have Redemption through his blood, the remission of sins according to the riches of his grace (Eph 1:7); those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and concupiscences (Gal 5:24);
    – to the penalty of sin, that is, to the evil world: I have overcome the world (Jn 16:33), because before we lived in bondage under the elements of the world (Gal 4:3); and to hell, because: at the name of Jesus every knee should bend… in hell (Phil 2:10);
    – to the fear of death, since the Son of God became incarnate in order to free those who through fear of death were subject to bondage all their life (Heb 2:15);
    – to the power of the devil: for this the Son of God appeared (became incarnate), to destroy the works of the devil (1 Jn 3:8);
    – to the slavery of the old law: he redeemed us from the curse of the law (Gal 3:13).
  7. Living:
    – the life of grace in fullness, since Christ has come in the flesh to bring us life, and life in abundance49: that life is the grace of God which makes us partakers of the divine nature (2 Pet 1:4); it is the supernatural life of the theological virtues, of the infused moral virtues and of the gifts of the Holy Spirit;
    – the prophetic life, by which we share “in the prophetic office of Christ”50, bearing witness to faith and charity, offering to God the sacrifice of praise, teaching the Word in season and out of season51, whether in preaching, in teaching, in writing or research, in evangelization or in catechesis, etc.;
    – the priestly life, both of the common priesthood derived from Baptism and of the ministerial or hierarchical priesthood (distinct in essence and not merely in degree)52;
  8. – the life of lordship, which connotes a certain aspect of dominion:
    a) Lordship over oneself: to the extent that man triumphs over sin, masters the incentives of the flesh, and governs his soul and his body. The religious, to the extent that he fully submits his soul to God, reaches a state of indifference and detachment from the things of the world, which does not mean impotence but on the contrary, a masterful and free will, capable of dedicating itself to things without letting itself be dominated by them.
  9. b) Lordship over men: to the extent that the religious gives himself generously to the service of Jesus Christ, the only King who deserves to be served, he acquires an effective, though spiritual, kingship over men, even over those who have power and authority, and even over those who abuse it. Because they take upon themselves the burden of their sins and their sorrows, through a humble and serving love that goes as far as the sacrifice of oneself.
  10. c) Lordship over the world, in two ways:
    – One, by collaborating with the world of creation through work and with the world of Redemption through the apostolate. For this kingship to be effective, it will be necessary that, together with dedication to things, there be at the same time a detachment and disengagement from them: It remains that those who have wives live as though they had none; those who weep as though they were not weeping; those who rejoice as though they were not rejoicing; those who buy as though they did not possess, and those who enjoy the world as though they were not enjoying it, for the outward form of this world is passing away (1 Cor 7:29ff.).
    – The other, by rejecting the world, whether out of loyalty to the world itself, which must be held as a means and not as an end, or out of loyalty to God, resisting the concupiscences, temptations and sins of the world; being independent in the face of the maxims, mockeries and persecutions of the world, depending only on our right conscience enlightened by faith; ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God, which constitutes the full and total rejection of the evil world.
  11. The greatest grace that God can grant our tiny Religious Family is persecution, especially that which reaches martyrdom53: “Long live Jesus Christ, who gives us strength to bear all trials for love of him. The works of God have always been opposed, for the greater splendor of the divine magnificence”54. We must always remember that, for persecution to be blessed, it must necessarily meet two requirements: that we be “insulted for the sake of Christ,” and that “what is said against us be false”55; and we must take great care not to turn our troubles over and over, entertaining ourselves with delicate self-satisfaction in them, or falling into “that Luciferian belief that we are something great”56, that we are suffering a great deal. Our pride leads us to “regard blades of grass as beams, pinpricks as wounds, mice as elephants”57.
  12. d) Lordship over the devil: We need religious convinced not only that by the grace of God they have power to resist the devil, but also power to exorcise him.
  13. – Living as risen ones: seek the things that are above, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God; set your minds on the things that are above, not on those of the earth (Col 3:1); in the freedom of the children of God, who do not let themselves be enslaved:
    – neither under the elements of the world (Gal 4:3),
    – nor under the letter that kills58,
    – nor under the spirit of the world59,
    because we must not submit to the yoke of slavery… (otherwise) Christ would profit us nothing60.
  14. We must be so docile to the Spirit that we can say: “My glory is to live as free / as a bird in the sky; / I make no nest upon this ground…”; owing nothing to the flesh, because all flesh is like grass and all its glory like the flower of grass (1 Pet 1:24), and if we live by the Spirit, let us also walk by the Spirit (Gal 5:25), since what is born of the flesh is flesh; but what is born of the Spirit is Spirit (Jn 3:6).
  15. That is to have a princely spirit61: it is to direct the soul toward great acts… it is to be concerned with great things… it is to accomplish great works in every virtue. It is to be noble. And what is it to be noble?… That is felt and not said. It is a man of heart. It is a man who has something for himself and for others. They are those born to command. They are those capable of punishing themselves and of punishing. They are those who have put style into their conduct. They are those who ask not for freedom but for hierarchy. They are those who set laws for themselves and keep them… They are those who feel honor as they feel life. Those who, because they possess themselves, can give themselves. They are those who know at every instant the things for which one must die. Those capable of giving things that no one obliges and of abstaining from things that no one forbids. They are those who always consider themselves beginners: let us always consider ourselves beginners, never ceasing to aspire to a holier and more perfect life, never stopping.
  16. So that we may be firmly resolved to attain holiness. A religious who is not willing to pass through the second and the third conversion, or who does nothing concrete to achieve it, even if he is with us in body, does not belong to our spiritual family. We must have “a great and very resolute determination not to stop until reaching it (holiness), come what may, happen what may, whatever the toil, whoever may murmur, whether one gets there, or dies on the way, or has no heart for the labors along it, even if the world should sink…”62. What matters is to take a step, one more step; it is always the same step beginning again.
  17. Through Baptism, through the practice of the virtues and by reason of religious profession, “which is intimately rooted in the consecration of Baptism and expresses it more fully”63, we want to imitate more closely and represent perpetually in the Church that form of life which the Son of God chose when he became incarnate64.
  18. We want to imitate Jesus Christ as perfectly as possible, since he teaches us: I have given you an example (Jn 13:15), and St. Paul exhorts: Have the same sentiments that were in Christ Jesus… (Phil 2:5), in such a way that we may be the good fragrance of Christ (2 Cor 2:15), ambassadors for Christ (2 Cor 5:20)… of the mystery of the Gospel (Eph 6:19), a letter of Christ (2 Cor 3:3), clothed with Christ (Gal 3:27), firmly convinced that we are predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son (Rom 8:29), reproducing him65, becoming like him66, configuring ourselves to him67, knowing that we reflect the very image (2 Cor 3:18) of the Only Son of God. We want to imitate him until we can truly say to others: Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ (1 Cor 11:1), it is no longer I who live, it is Christ who lives in me (Gal 2:20).
  19. The fact that the Word assumed a human nature must move us to the practice of the virtues of self-emptying: humility, poverty, suffering, obedience, self-denial, mercy and love for all men.
  20. From the very fact of becoming man without ceasing to be God, we must learn to be in the world68 “without being of the world”69. We must go to the world to convert it and not to blend in with it. We must go to the culture and to the cultures of man not to be converted into them, but to heal them and elevate them with the power of the Gospel, doing, analogously, what Christ did: “He suppressed the diabolical, assumed the human and communicated the divine to it”70.
  21. Just as Christ became like us in all things except sin (Heb 4:15), so for us sin, error and all their derivatives cannot be assumed. Before baptizing one must exorcise; without conversion reconciliation is impossible; without renouncing evil there is no Redemption. There can be no unity at the expense of truth. There is no holiness without purity of soul: “holiness means purity”71.
  22. Only what has dignity or necessity can be assumed. Neither the inhuman, nor the anti-human, nor the subhuman can be assumed. The irrational, the absurd and all their derivatives cannot be assumed.
  1. But nothing authentically human must be rejected, since Christ assumed a complete human nature. We must assume everything human, since “what was not assumed was not redeemed either”72, and what is human and is not assumed “sets itself up as a new idol with an old malice”73.
  2. And this assuming of what is human must not be merely apparent, but real. This assumption is only real when it truly transforms what is human in Christ, elevating it, dignifying it, perfecting it. What is left merely on the human level has only apparently been assumed.
  3. What has been said applies, in a particular way, to the evangelization of culture, which demands of us a spirituality with particular nuances: “this calls for a new way of approaching cultures, attitudes and behaviors, in order to enter into deep dialogue with cultural milieus and to make their encounter with the message of Christ fruitful. And on the part of responsible Christians, this work demands a faith enlightened by continual reflection that is confronted with the sources of the Church’s message, and a constant spiritual discernment sought in prayer”74, never forgetting that “true inculturation is from within: it consists, ultimately, in a renewal of life under the influence of grace”75.
  4. Furthermore, we must attend spiritually to religious sisters, because of the very dignity of their consecration, which makes them “spouses of the Word.” In a certain way, religious sisters resemble the human nature of Christ, which:
    – is totally stripped of itself, without even having its own personhood;
    – is united to the Word with an intimate and most perfect union;
    – is a most docile instrument of the Word;
    – all its wealth consists in giving itself to the Word.
  5. The religious sister who truly longs to be a spouse of the Word must, out of love for this divine Spouse76:
    – “abandon everything”: through the three vows she separates herself from everything that might hinder perfect union with Jesus Christ;
    – “in order to unite herself with all her strength to the Word”: through charity and through a stable, firm and constant fidelity to the will of Jesus Christ: “in the Church, in the cloister, in the cell, in the refectory, at recreation, they must go about sighing for Jesus Christ”77;
    – “to live for him and let herself be guided by him”: they must be zealous for the honor and glory of the Word, their Spouse, and must be able to say with St. Paul: it is no longer I who live, it is Christ who lives in me (Gal 2:20);
    – “to conceive of the Word what she must bring forth for the Word”: since through the Holy Spirit all her acts must be supernatural, fruitful and meritorious.


    c) The union of both natures

  6. The whole august mystery of the Incarnate Word must be a motive of constant inspiration for our life: “The Father begot the Son as uttering himself, equal to him in all things; for he would not have expressed himself completely and perfectly if in his Word there were anything more or less than what is in him. There we recognize in the highest degree the yes, yes; no, no (Mt 5:37). And that is why this Word is truly the Truth”78.
  7. The Word, in the Incarnation, anoints with a most holy anointing each and every one of the cells of the body of Jesus, and the whole soul in its essence and in its faculties. There is nothing in Christ that is not thrice holy, and therefore infinitely adorable. Everything in him is transparency, authenticity, sincerity, coherence, truth: I am… the Truth (Jn 14:6). He is the Amen (Rev 3:14). All the promises of God find their Yes in him; and through him we say Amen, to the glory of God (2 Cor 1:20); in him dwells the fullness of the divinity bodily (Col 2:9). In him there is nothing empty, hollow, or not hypostatically assumed. There is nothing of veneer or shell. Nothing simulated or camouflaged. Nothing of lies, falsehood, insecurity, concealment or hypocrisy. He is One Alone79 –the Word– in two distinct natures, both perfect, complete and hypostatically united.
  8. For this very reason Christ is one beyond comparison, and “this is said of Christ in many ways:
    – In the first place, God is one alone because of the divine nature; hence, thus, no one is God but one alone.
    – In the second place, he is one Creator alone because of his infinite power: One is the Most High, Creator of all things (Eccl 1:8).
    – In the third place, he is unique as man, because of the singular eminen­ce of his holiness: there is none who does good (Ps 14:1); it is expedient that one man die for the people (Jn 11:50).
    – In the fourth place, he is one Lord alone, because of his government of preeminence: one Lord, one faith (Eph 4:5). In this way there is one Lord alone, Jesus Christ.
    – In the fifth place, one Teacher alone, because of the infusion of knowledge: call no one teacher, for you have but one Teacher: the Christ (Mt 23:10).
    – In the sixth place, one Father alone, because of the production of all things: one is your Father who is in heaven (Mt 23:9).
    – In the seventh place, one Shepherd alone, because of the common care of the faithful people: there shall be one flock and one Shepherd (Jn 10:16).
    – In the eighth place, one Victim alone, because of the singular price of our Redemption: for by a single offering he has consecrated forever those who are sanctified (Heb 10:14)”80.
  9. We confess our Only Master and Lord Jesus Christ (Jude 4), and that in him a reference to all creatures is contained. He is the point of union and meeting of the comprehensor and the wayfarer; of the Master81 and of the slave82; of this age and of the age to come; of the universe and of the atom; of the from now on of salvation and of the until now of the promises; of the not yet of what we hope for and of the already accomplished; he is the point of union and meeting of God and man; of the eschatological and of the incarnate; of sidereal distances and of the millimeter. That is why nothing escapes Christ: neither space travel, nor the world of electronics and computers, of the most current science or the most advanced technology, nor recent discoveries, nor the family, work, culture, the economic-political sphere, etc. That is why we want to live the exhortation of St. Ignatius of Antioch: “Stop your ears when you hear anyone speak of anything that does not have Christ Jesus as its foundation…”83.
  10. There is one Lord Jesus Christ (1 Cor 8:6); that is why we do not want “to leave anything untried so that the love of Christ may have supreme primacy in the Church and in society”84.
  11. Because Christ is one, we want to work with all our strength to build our life in union with the legitimate Pastors, and most especially with a heartfelt adherence to the Bishop of Rome, thus showing the Church as one:
    – So that all Christians may reach perfect unity in order that the promise and prophecy of the Lord may be fulfilled: There shall be one flock and one Shepherd (Jn 10:16), and that his prayer may bear fruit: Father, that all may be one (Jn 17:21). This is the ecumenical work.
    – So that all men may confess the adorable Name of the Lord Jesus, fulfilling his commandment: Go into the whole world and preach the Gospel to every creature (Mk 16:15). This is the missionary work. Not forgetting that: “it is truth, more than anything else, that builds unity: the communion of minds is easily transformed into a union of hearts…”85.
  12. Only in the truth will we be able to live the ineffable reality that St. John Eudes expresses: “All that is his is yours: the Spirit, the heart, the body, the soul and all his faculties… all that is in you must be grafted onto him… he must be your spirit, your heart, your love, your life and all that is yours”86.
  13. The two natures, complete and perfect, of the Incarnate Word remind us of the twofold reality, supernatural and natural, of created things, and therefore of the real distinction between grace and nature, faith and reason, Church and world, which must not be confused, nor changed, nor mixed, nor absorbed, nor subsumed. One must not mix the human with the divine, which is a kind of mixture that no Christian understanding should wear. They move us, furthermore, to the practice of apparently opposed virtues, respecting their essences and avoiding every false gnostic monism; for example, justice and love, firmness and gentleness, fortitude and meekness, holy anger and patience, purity and great affection, magnanimity and humility, prudence and courage, joy and penance, etc. Lofty holiness is the eminent union of all the virtues, even the most diverse.
  14. Both natures, preserving their properties, are substantially united in the person of the Word; therefore, they leave no room for false dualisms or destructive dialectics that seek to divide, separate and oppose grace and nature, faith and reason, Church and world, realities that must not be destructively opposed, but united in an orderly way. They also move us not to dialecticize either our spiritual life or our pastoral life, not emphasizing one element against another, avoiding every duality, permeating our whole life with the Truth, being an “amen” of the Amen (Rev 3:14).
  15. Care must also be taken never to fall into Manichean views of reality or into Jansenist reductionisms, preventing the legitimate and just autonomy of the temporal and of the spiritual from degenerating into independence from God: “The creature without the Creator vanishes”87. Atheism is “the most serious phenomenon of our time”88.
  16. Certainly it is God who assumes a human nature and not the other way around. And so it must be in our whole life, in which we must always give primacy to God over the world, to grace over nature, to faith over reason: “in the priority of ethics over technology, in the primacy of the person over things, in the superiority of spirit over matter”89. Just as in Christ there is an order between his two natures, so in all of reality there must be a hierarchical subordination between the supernatural and natural orders. He is the one who gives us the law that governs the relations between both orders. It is God who heals and elevates man, not the other way around; it is faith that purifies and perfects reason, not the other way around; it is the Church that heals and saves the world and not the other way around. The inversion of values that affects modern culture is another of the problems of our time. One must give more importance to what has it, and one must seek first what counts first: Seek first the Kingdom and its justice, and all the rest will be given to you besides (Mt 6:33).
  17. Nor is the mystery of the Incarnation a mere union of the divine nature with the human nature; rather, it is a union of assumption90. Not just any mode of union between God and the creature is enough for us to recapitulate all things in Christ (Eph 1:10); rather, analogously to the mystery of the Incarnation, it must be an assumption, that is, there must be an action and a passion that express the becoming and not only the final result, and that bear a distinct reference to each of the terms, of departure and of arrival. Cultures must be “assumed,” purifying them and elevating them starting from Christ and his Gospel, understood “in the Church”91. A mere union is not enough for us, nor is merely putting a nominalist label on reality so that it may truly be made subject to the lordship of Christ. “Being in the world” only makes sense for us when it depends on “not being of the world.” Only thus can one truly be salt of the earth and light of the world (Mt 5:13ff.). Otherwise we would become salt that has lost its savor and a light under the bushel basket.


    d) On the end of the Incarnation

  18. The ultimate and absolute end of the Incarnation is the glory of God; the mediate end is the exaltation and glory of Christ, redeemer of man; and the immediate end is the Redemption of the human race. Hence in everything and through everything we want always to seek the glory of God, the ultimate end of the whole universe; in a particular way, in the search for, investigation, proclamation and celebration of the truth, because we follow the Word who says: I am the Truth (Jn 14:6), since that very thing is “the ultimate end of the universe… the truth”92, and because it was for this that Jesus Christ became incarnate: For this I have come into the world, to bear witness to the truth (Jn 18:37). It is first of all the truth that gives clear knowledge, with praise, of God.
  1. A particular way of giving glory to God is to trust without limit in His Providence, grounded in His plan of salvation, which is manifested in a most eminent way in the Incarnation. We must learn to look upon everything as coming from Him who does not forget even a little sparrow… and who has numbered even the hairs of our head93. That is why St. Paul teaches that all things work together for the good of those who love God (Rom 8:28). In saying all things, he excepts nothing. Therefore everything is included here: all events, prosperous or adverse, whatever concerns the good of the soul, the goods of fortune, reputation, all the conditions of human life (family, studies, talents, etc.), all the interior states we pass through (joys, gladness, privations, dryness, displeasures, weariness, temptations, etc.), even our faults and our very sins. Everything, absolutely everything. In saying work together for the good, it is understood that they cooperate, contribute and come about for our spiritual good. We must have this view, and not that of the carnal or worldly man. We must see everything in the light of the loving designs of God's Providence, which only the spiritual man discovers: the spiritual man judges all things (1 Cor 2:15). We must believe with unshakable firmness that even the most adverse events, those most opposed to our natural outlook, are ordained by God for our good, even though we do not understand His designs and do not know the goal to which He wishes to lead us. But on our part we must fulfill one condition for this to come about, and that is why he adds of those who love God, that is, those whose will is united and submissive to God's, who seek above all the interests and the glory of God, who are ready to sacrifice everything without reserve, persuaded that nothing is so advantageous as abandoning oneself into the hands of God in all that it may please Him to ordain, as Jesus gave us to understand: if anyone serves me, the Father will honor him (Jn 12:26). He alone knows everything, even our soul, our feelings, our character, the secret springs that must be moved to bring us to heaven, the effects that this or that thing will produce in us, and He has all means at His disposal. If we love God, it is impossible that there should be anything in the world that does not concur and contribute to our good.
  2. And we wish to tend toward the sanctification and salvation of men, our brothers. Jesus Christ came into the world "for us men"; therefore "every man, the whole man and all men"94 –without discrimination–, and preferentially the most needy, must be the object of our love and of our service. In the likeness of the Incarnate and crucified Word we must have a "thirst for souls." We must love in deed and in truth the concrete man who is in need –of material or spiritual goods–, and never use him as demagogic propaganda. We follow Him who, inspiring St. John, said of Himself: God is love (1 Jn 4:8).

    Article 2: His Earthly Life
  3. The whole life of Christ must be for us a source of spirituality: "He willed to prefigure us in His body, in His own body"95.


    a) In becoming incarnate

  4. When the Father brings His Firstborn into the world (Heb 1:6), the sublime priestly consecration of Jesus Christ takes place, by which He is marked and anointed forever: God, your God, has anointed you, O God, with the oil of gladness above your companions (Heb 1:9); High and Eternal Priest96, sole Mediator between heaven and earth.
  5. In that instant, His soul contemplated the infinite majesty of the Father and experienced "the incomprehensible immensity of His abundance"97, and at the same time contemplated the immense offense done to God –in a certain way infinite– by the sins of all men of all times, and the insufficiency of the victims offered up to that moment; and He experienced that the Father had made Him Priest and Victim –for which He had to assume matter–, and the impulse of His unspeakable love made a supremely priestly act in giving Himself entirely to His Will98.
  6. That first priestly act of the most holy soul of Christ, in the very instant of His Incarnation and priestly ordination, was never bent, never retracted, and remains forever in His priestly heart. It is present during His infancy, His hidden and public life, at the Supper and on Calvary, in His Resurrection and Ascension, and the five precious wounds recall it to Him permanently in the heavenly Jerusalem. That priestly act was the prelude to His redemptive sacrifice on the Cross and to all the acts of His heavenly priesthood: coming into the world He says: a body you have prepared for me… Here I am!… (Heb 10:5-7). He acknowledges the supreme dominion of the Father; He offers Himself to Him without restriction; He makes an oblation of Himself as victim; He immolates Himself in expiation; He gives Himself to the Father without reserve.
  7. Such must be the priestly attitude of every member of our little Religious Family. Through Baptism all are built up as a spiritual house for a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ (1 Pet 2:5). All, even our lay brothers and sisters, are a royal priesthood (1 Pet 2:9), because "they are united to Him and to His sacrifice"99; so too are those who by religious vows must give themselves totally to God by another title; and so must be those who, in addition, through Holy Orders exercise the ministerial priesthood. All the members of the Institute must perfect themselves by being in Christ "an eternal offering to God"100, "a living and perfect victim for the praise of your glory"101. It is the priestly attitude proper to the "third class of men" of the Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius of Loyola102. It is man's response to God's revelation: "By faith man gives himself entirely and freely to God"103. An attitude that must be lived permanently, without diminutions or retractions, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without retreats or slackness. Both in the undertakings of the inner life and in the high undertakings of history: he who is not capable of setting fire to his ships when he lands is not capable of building empires.
  8. In this sublime instant of the Incarnation He gives us a signal example of the practice of the mortifying virtues to an abysmal degree, since without ceasing to be infinite God, He became finite man, showing us an infinite humility, poverty, obedience and love.
  9. It is an example of humility, since Christ, being in the form of God, did not cling greedily to being equal with God… He humbled Himself… (Phil 2:6-8) in assuming the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men (Phil 2:7); without ceasing to be God, He manifests a supreme lowering –He emptied Himself (2:7)– even as man, an immeasurable abasement, in His inner and outward judgment; His humanity knew that it had everything from God. Seeing such great humility, we must learn to regard one another as superiors, each one seeking not his own interest, but that of others (Phil 2:4)104. We must never cease to consider the whole inexhaustible treasure of virtues of the hypostatic Heart of Jesus. To contemplate that richest of veins and not to want to leave it must be the badge of honor, the distinctive sign, of our Institute.
  10. There, in the Incarnation, we must also take example from the Blessed Virgin. In the first place, from her faith: Blessed is she who believed… (Lk 1:45); we must live by faith105 and have a faith that is living, firm, intrepid, eminent, heroic; a faith convinced that God would not be God if we were capable of encompassing Him with our limited intelligence, if we understood all His judgments and ways; a faith in absolute harmony with the doctrine proposed by the Catholic Church, even in the smallest details, kneaded in the strictest docility to the directives and teachings of the Pope; a faith full of promptness in rejecting error perceived even under the faintest appearances, full of ardent zeal in spreading it, but without bitterness or harshness; a penetrating faith that sees all things in the light of revelation, sub specie aeternitatis, raising the soul to the supernatural plans of God, so that we must consider ourselves "deserving of all afflictions"106; a heroic faith like that of the saints of the Old Testament107, which triumphs over the world and evil108, which builds great things, which illumines life and gives it meaning, which strengthens, encourages, comforts and casts out fear: Do not be afraid! It is I (Mk 6:50). Cowards die many times before their death.
  11. From the Virgin Mary we must take example, moreover, of her humility: she was troubled at hearing these words (Lk 1:29), and she proclaimed herself lowly109: and He has exalted the lowly (Lk 1:52); of her prudence: she pondered what this greeting might mean (Lk 1:29); of her purity: How can this be, since I do not know man? (Lk 1:34); of her abandonment to God: Behold the handmaid of the Lord… (Lk 1:38). In Mary we must find strength to rediscover permanently the Christian and priestly life as fidelity to the religion of mystery and miracle, of martyrdom and mercy, of the Magnificat and of magnanimity. Of mystery, because there the Trinity is revealed: the Lord God… Son of the Most High… the Holy Spirit… (Lk 1:32-35), and the Incarnate Word is revealed: Be it done unto me according to your word (Lk 1:38). Of miracle: a virgin… you will conceive in your womb and bear a son (Lk 1:27.31). Of martyrdom: set for the fall and the rising… and for a sign of contradiction… and a sword will pierce your heart… (Lk 2:34-35). Of mercy: God so loved the world that He gave His only-begotten Son (Jn 3:16); whom you shall name Jesus (Lk 1:31), because He will save His people from their sins (Mt 1:21), and His mercy is poured out from generation to generation… forever (Lk 1:50-51). Of the Magnificat: My soul proclaims the greatness of the Lord (Lk 1:46), and the angels praise God saying: Glory to God in the highest… (Lk 2:14). Of magnanimity: the Mighty One has done great things for me (Lk 1:49).
  12. We could say that our spirituality must be that of the "Hail Mary," that of the "Angelus" and that of the hymn of the kenosis110, that of the "Magnificat" and that of the "Gloria."


    b) In the womb of the Virgin

  13. We must learn to see ourselves enclosed with Christ in the womb of Mary. There, at the moment of His becoming incarnate, we were included in Him: Christ assumes, morally, the flesh of all. In Mary's womb He took physically His own flesh and morally the flesh of all of us, in such a way that we were conceived in the most pure womb of Mary, and that is why she is our true and proper spiritual Mother: "We have been born from the womb of the Virgin, in the manner of a body united to its head"111.
  14. We will never insist enough on this mysterious and ineffable reality of our incorporation into Christ by the principle of koinonia, solidarity or communion, by which we are recapitulated and as it were concentrated in Him, since He took upon Himself the representation, the responsibility and the destiny of all men. By this intimate interpenetration and identification of Christ with each one of us, at the moment of the Incarnation we are made –in root– children of God, forming with the Incarnate Word a single whole, a compact block: we are sons in the Son, filii in Filio. "The Savior… willed to become Son of man so that we might become sons of God"112. "Together with Him they form one single mystical person"113.
  15. And not only men. In the Word made flesh all things converge, gather, join hands, interlock, are summed up and are epitomized… recapitulating all things… in the Alpha and the Omega114.
  16. For nine months the Virgin was the tabernacle that sheltered Jesus, just as for almost two thousand years she has been the monstrance that shows Him to the world, and the one task is no less important than the other.
  17. There God was taking human form; that is why St. Augustine calls the Virgin "forma Dei"115, and in the likeness of Jesus we, consecrating ourselves as slaves of the Virgin, wish "to enter into our Mother's womb and be born again"116. To consecrate ourselves to Jesus through Mary is to follow the path that He followed, that He continues to use and that He will use to come into the world117.
  1. There Jesus taught us to depend totally and in every way on God through Mary. There He taught us the evangelical ministry of visiting. Like Mary, "carrying Him who carried her"118, He taught us to found all our apostolic enthusiasm on Him. To carry out the things of God quickly119. To serve our neighbor, even, like Mary, in the simplest tasks. To praise and give thanks, to sing and rejoice in God who is Mighty, Holy, Merciful, Savior and Faithful, like Mary, because He "looked upon" her and did "great things" in her (the Incarnation), displaying His power, scattering the proud, casting down the mighty, filling the hungry, exalting the lowly, protecting His servants, forever.


    c) Infancy

  2. The birth of the Incarnate Word urges us, among other things, to live holy poverty strictly, for he who has God is extremely rich. And to live in joy, fruit of the Holy Spirit and consequence of the Incarnation, as the angel announced to the shepherds: I bring you good news, a great joy that is for all the people (Lk 2:10); it is the joy of the Virgin Mary, "cause of our joy": Rejoice, Mary… (Lk 1:28), the angel Gabriel said to her, and she, most docile, sang: my spirit exults with joy in God, my Savior (Lk 1:47), and that is why all generations have called her and will call her blessed (Lk 1:48), happy in the highest degree.
  3. It also urges us to work in the most difficult places (those where no one wants to go), and when it is no longer possible to continue there, after praying over it for a night before Jesus in the Blessed Sacrament, to ask the bishop to send us to a worse place.
  4. From the Epiphany we must draw light to commit our whole life to manifesting Christ to the world, so that He may be recognized not only by the poor but also by the rich, not only by the ignorant but also by the wise, not only by those of our own homeland but also by those of other peoples. We must not get trapped in false reductionist dialectics in which others are excluded (for example, the rich, intellectuals, foreigners or outsiders, etc.), in which one is concerned exclusively with some people or some place –narcissistic localism–, because the Spirit blows where it wills (Jn 3:8). The field of our action has no limits of horizon; it is the wide world: go into the whole world… (Mk 16:15), said Jesus.
  5. From the loss and finding of Jesus we wish to learn to be firm and faithful to the call, to our vocation, above any other claim of this earth: I must be about my Father's business (Lk 2:49). And to fulfill the will of God ever better, that is, in a manner:
    – more universal in its object: in all the forms in which it can be manifested, whether when God commands –commandments, precepts, laws, etc.–, or when He counsels –inspirations, vocation, evangelical counsels, etc.–, or when He manifests Himself through events –signified will–;
    – more exact in its execution, not contenting ourselves only with what is substantial but also attending to the details (God has refinements of love, and we must respond to those refinements);
    – more supernatural in its motives, with our intention being purer, doing things only because God wills them;
    – more perfect in the dispositions with which we fulfill it, not doing the will of God out of worldly fear or out of selfish or temporal interest, but out of pure love, showing Him in this way that we love Him and that we want nothing but to please Him.
  6. His example also moves us to have love for theological science, to have the true sense of the dialogue of salvation: "Apostolic dialogue starts from faith and presupposes a firm Christian identity"120, to be occupied with the Father and to have a profound spirit of piety.


    d) Hidden life

  7. No less weight for our spirituality has the example of Jesus in His hidden life. Hence every time of preparation must be very important for us; it is not lost time when it is put to good use, it is not a time to be gotten through as quickly as possible, it is not a necessary evil; the future of our brothers in religious life and, therefore, of the Institute, depends fundamentally on the formation given to them in the times of preparation: postulancy, novitiate, seminary, subsequent further training, ongoing formation. For this, good doctrine alone is not enough; in addition, "masters" are needed who have much spirit and a clear intention of making a most rigorous selection; this is capital for maintaining the good spirit. Knowing that in selecting it is better to err by excess than by defect.
  8. In the likeness of Jesus, particularly in the times of formation, there must stand out in the candidates the yearning to grow, love for fruitful silence, a dauntless dedication to intellectual and manual work (fulfilling the duty of one's state perfectly to the end), being subject121 with responsibility, promptness and maturity, and living in a climate of festive122 joy.
  9. Silence is a need of the soul, to express in the most profound way that, in the presence of God, there is nothing more to say. It is a means for attaining union with God and, therefore, must lead to the summit of prayer. He who seeks to keep silent will do so in order to converse only with God, who leads the soul into the desert to speak to its heart123.
  10. Also in the likeness of the Word, who willed to live in the silence of Nazareth for thirty years, some of our members consecrate themselves to Him in the state of contemplative life. They wish to dedicate themselves to the one thing necessary; they have chosen the better part124. "How precious is the contemplative life in the eyes of God and of the Church! It constitutes one of the fundamental structures of holy Church; it has been present in all the phases of her two-thousand-year history, always fruitful in solid virtues, always endowed with a mysterious and powerful attraction over the most elevated and noble souls"125. For that very reason, these members are in the vanguard of all the apostolic works of the Institute, since by their life of prayer and penance they obtain from the Lord the graces necessary for the salvation of many souls: "Institutes of contemplative life have the greatest importance in the conversion of souls by their prayers, works of penance and tribulations, because it is God who, through prayer, sends laborers into His harvest, opens the souls of non-Christians to hear the Gospel and makes the word of salvation fruitful in their hearts"126. To them apply these words of St. Teresa: "For this (the salvation of souls) He has gathered you here; this is your vocation and your desire, this is the reason for your tears and your prayers… On the day when your prayers, disciplines, longings and fasts are not dedicated to this that I have told you, you will not attain –know it well– the end for which the Lord has gathered us here"127. Our monasteries of contemplative life must be magnets of God's grace and lightning rods of His wrath. Would that we could multiply them throughout the whole world! "The Church and the world… need… a small ideal society where there reign, as its end, love, obedience, innocence, freedom from things and the art of using them well, the prevalence of the spirit, peace, in a word, the Gospel"128.


    e) Public life

  11. It is impossible to summarize all that Jesus Christ teaches us by His life and His doctrine, which is the source of all Christian spirituality. By way of example –not as an exhaustive list– we wish to point out some aspects of the spirituality of our little Religious Family inspired by the signal examples of our Savior and Redeemer.


    The Baptism

  12. Christ was baptized by John in order to consecrate Baptism129; hence the whole theological depth of our baptismal birth and of our religious profession, which "is intimately rooted in the consecration of Baptism and expresses it more fully"130, can only be grasped in the light of the Baptism of the Lord.
  13. It reminds us that we must give ourselves unceasingly to the Father, who in the Son has said to us: This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased (Mt 3:17); to the Son, whose disciples we are made and from whom we are called Christians131; and to the Holy Spirit, who descended upon us in the Son –in bodily form, like a dove upon Him (Lk 3:22)–; and all this was worked in us sacramentally on the day of our Baptism.
  14. It reminds us of the grave obligation to remain faithful to the promises of holy Baptism, by which we commit ourselves to renounce the devil and to confess the holy Catholic faith, and to the promises of religious profession.
  15. It must also be a spur to live the virtue of humility in its fullness, since Christ was not afraid to pass for one more sinner, because He wished "to purify the waters so that, cleansed by contact with the flesh of Christ, which knew no sin, they might have the power to baptize"132, and "to leave them sanctified for those who were afterwards to be baptized"133; likewise, He left us an example of the exercise of the virtue of justice: it is fitting that we fulfill all justice (Mt 3:15).


    The fast

  16. The practice of penance is essential to the Christian life, and therefore it must be so in our spirituality: Unless you do penance, you will all likewise perish (Lk 13:3). Above all interior penance, metanoia134, that is, the intimate and total change and renewal of the whole man in all his feeling, judging and disposing.
  17. In Christ we recognize the holiness of God and the gravity of sin135; by His word we are invited to conversion and to receive the forgiveness of sins, gifts which we receive in their fullness in Baptism and which we must "incarnate" by denying ourselves, taking up our own cross and sharing in the sufferings of Christ.
  18. For our tiny Religious Family, the holy sacrament of Reconciliation or Penance occupies a most important place in the spiritual life, so much so that we consider it advisable to receive it weekly. We must have devotion to frequent confession, since many are the fruits that follow from it: "genuine self-knowledge increases, Christian humility grows, bad habits are uprooted, spiritual tepidity and indolence are resisted, the conscience is purified, the will is strengthened, the salutary direction of consciences is carried out, and grace increases by virtue of the sacrament"136.
  19. But we must also hold exterior penance in the highest esteem: regarding eating and drinking, regarding the manner of sleeping, "giving sensible pain"137 to the flesh by means of hair shirts138, disciplines, etc. The preferentially interior and religious character of penance does not in any way exclude or lessen the external practice of this virtue; on the contrary, it demands its necessity with special urgency. The norms given by St. Ignatius must regulate its practice139.
  20. We must always give priority to the penitential seasons –Advent and Lent– and penitential days, according to the precept of our Holy Mother Church140, in which we must dedicate ourselves in a special way to prayer, to the practice of charity and piety, to self-denial, to fulfilling better the obligations of our state, etc.
  21. The example of our Lord in withdrawing for forty days must lead us to value to the highest degree the practice of the Spiritual Exercises, especially according to the method of St. Ignatius of Loyola, and the customary thirty-day Exercises toward the end of the novitiate and every ten years. Likewise, making annual eight-day Exercises is altogether praiseworthy. We also believe that the monthly retreat is very important.
  22. This clear awareness of the irreplaceable value of the Spiritual Exercises for the renewal of the Christian life, teaching one to conquer oneself and to order one's own life according to God, must lead us to know them in depth, to prepare ourselves to preach them fruitfully, and to have the necessary availability so as not to let any opportunity of preaching them pass by, as occasion offers.


    The temptations

  1. The example of the Lord in undergoing the assaults of the devil in the desert will always be an inexhaustible source of encouragement for religious. For He willed to give us strength against temptations: He conquered "our temptations by His own"141; so that no one, however holy he may be, should consider himself free from being tempted: My son, if you come to serve God, prepare your soul for temptation (Eccl 2:1); to teach us with what promptness and firmness, and with what justice, the temptations of the devil must be overcome: "the Devil is not to be conquered by force, but by justice"142; so that we may trust more in His mercy: For we do not have a High Priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who was tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin (Heb 4:15). Above all, because just as in the three temptations is found "the matter of all sins"143, religious, by the three vows, are diametrically opposed to all sins and to all the temptations that push toward sin and that can, in some way, be reduced to the three that our Lord underwent. And by the fourth vow we oppose sin by yet another title, since by being slaves of the Virgin we clearly take sides in that enmity created by God: I will put perpetual enmity between you (the devil) and the woman, and between your seed and hers; he shall crush your head… (Gen 3:15).
  2. It is not a good sign to be frightened at having great and grave temptations, and to give them excessive importance, for two reasons: a) "The Whole Christ was tempted by the devil, since in Him you were tempted… Recognize yourself tempted in Him and recognize yourself also victorious in Him… our progress is accomplished by means of temptation, and no one can know himself unless he is tempted, nor can he be crowned unless he has conquered, nor can he conquer unless he has fought, nor can he fight if he lacks an enemy and temptations"144; b) No temptation has overtaken you that is not human, and God is faithful, who will not let you be tempted beyond your strength; rather, with the temptation He will also provide the way out, so that you may be able to resist it (1 Cor 10:13).
  3. Worthy of note are some of the most common temptations that we observe in ourselves, damaging our spiritual and pastoral life. Among these states are the self-centered priest, who seeks human glory, that is, who seeks to please men rather than God145, cares only about what affects him personally, and is hyper-jealous, above all if the younger priests have more gifts than he; the functionary, with the spirit of a bureaucratic civil servant, with multiplied demands on the people, whom he sees as clients, who thinks himself "obedient" because he fulfills the formalities but neither burns others nor is burned with the fire of the Holy Spirit, who knows nothing of venturing out for Christ, is not creative, does not undertake new apostolates, does not "make the road by walking," does not realize that the priest must be a poet, a craftsman; the one who sows sparingly, doing as little as possible on the excuse of not falling into activism, or because the times are bad, or because the family does not form as it used to, or because of the unhealthy influence of the mass media…; he only knows how to lament: "nothing can be done here"; the inconstant one, who begins many things but never finishes any, who lacks follow-through; the boring one: some are as orthodox as they are boring; they have the rare ability to make the truth tedious; deep down they do not love: "The soul that walks in love neither tires others nor grows tired"146; the improviser, who does everything without foreseeing anything, does not prepare his sermons, does not set goals and, if he does, does not know how to use the means, does not think about how a thing ought to be done, is incapable of planning pastoral work, is disorderly, is not master of himself, ends up being irresponsible, does not fulfill what he promises, does not know how to choose his ministries; the frivolous one, who trivializes everything; everything is superficiality; he thinks himself clever, does not respect essences, handles everything carelessly, lacks nobility of bearing and ignores hierarchies, delights in being "smooth," especially with young women, and is incurably childish and superficial; the uptight one, all seriousness, always distant, inaccessible, tense; he does not know how to laugh, every healthy recreation seems to him levity, an ill-sounding word is for him a grave sin; for such men it seems that eutrapelia is not a virtue; in looking at the tree they forget the existence of the forest; they tend to make storms in teacups; the mediocre one, who takes shelter under a false balance, considers himself "middle of the road"; all magnanimity seems to him pride, all heroism seems to him extremism, all generosity seems to him exaggeration; he makes everything mediocre: retreats, camps, popular missions, catechesis, his own spiritual life, etc.; the localist, who cares only about parish-pump interests, lives engrossed in his little work, has a ghetto spirit; it seems as if the Church were exhausted in his parish, city, province or country; if he is of the diocesan clergy it seems to him a sin if some young man he knows wants to be a religious; if he is a religious, he suffers if someone wants to enter a congregation other than his own; he does not understand what it means that the Church is "missionary by her very nature"147; his problems are exhausted in the assistant pastor, or some little old lady, or the neighboring pastor, or his own bishop, and he does not see as problems either the "invasion of the sects"148, or militant atheism, or the de-Christianization of culture…; the affected one, all elegant and clean, a perfect little gem, but all outward shine; he stays in the details and that is his world; the sacrum of the liturgy slips through his fingers; he lacks grit, fortitude and worldly experience in the non-pejorative sense of the word; the avaricious one, who chooses apostolates according to their financial yield, more interested in stipends than in the salvation of souls; the expenses of popular missions, Spiritual Exercises, etc., seem to him an unpardonable waste; he does not invest in pastoral work but in finance companies; all the poor who knock at his door are freeloaders; he does not understand that we must make for ourselves purses that do not wear out (Lk 12:33); the ill-humored one, always impatient, sullen, approachable only when he is in a good mood; he is sterile, sees everything as black, is not a celibate but an old bachelor, and in practice ignores that one milligram of grace weighs more than all the sins of the world, even these raised to the nth power; the laicized one, who is lay in his customs, life and spirituality; he seems not to have the sacrament of Holy Orders; he is known by his lay manner of dressing, by his way of dealing with people, by the number of hours he spends watching television, by his worldly criteria, etc.; the fearful one, worried about what people will say, who mistrusts everything, interprets the simplest gesture as if it were a grave disqualification, makes up stories that feed his phobias; he lacks the intrepidity of the Holy Spirit; the timid one is a milder variant of the previous one; he thinks only that he is not worthy, that he does not know how to preach, that people make fun of him; he does not deal with cultured people, is faint-hearted, falls into false humility, and does little or nothing to conquer himself; the climber, who sidles up to authority, flattering it in order to rise; he sees the priesthood as a career ladder, takes great care of his image, tends to be of an effeminate psychology and therefore a schemer; he is incapable "of blooming where he has been planted"; he wants to be in flowerpots set out on balconies; the loner, who acts according to his own spirit, is incapable of joining others for a common task, does not integrate, and ends up that way; the ubiquitous one, who is everywhere except where he has to be, who goes up, comes down, comes and goes, confuses the pilgrim Church with a nomadic Church, does not know how "to restrain vain steps"149, and therefore does little and wastes much time. In short, this is enough as a sample and to point out that what is needed are holy priests and religious who are poets, metaphysicians and soldiers, who sing, contemplate and fight.


    Teaching and works

  4. Jesus did and taught (Acts 1:1). Therefore, in our spirituality, we must never dialectically separate teaching from doing, nor doing from teaching. We must always unite integrity of doctrine with uprightness of life, orthodoxy and orthopraxis. In all of revelation, word and deed, dabar and bara, are inseparably united.
  5. The lines of the spirituality of teaching must also be inspired by the examples of our blessed Savior. Above all in this time of revitalization of evangelization and catechesis. We think that the fundamental characteristics of the eloquence of Jesus are: originality, authority, serenity and universality. The members of our Institute dedicated to teaching –and in some way all must be– must grasp these characteristics very deeply in prayer so as then to be able to transmit them to others.
  6. We must have the firmest conviction that only in the strictest fidelity to the doctrine of Jesus as understood in the Church is the strongest, most living, freshest and most graceful originality to be found: No man ever spoke like this man (Jn 7:46), His hearers said of Jesus. Everything that departs from Jesus is outdated, grows old quickly and, sadly, will be a boring refrain. Here that ancient counsel also applies: "do not love singularities"150.
  7. Being convinced of the truth contemplated makes one teach with conviction and –according to its degree of theological certainty– with an authority, which is nothing other than a participation in the authority of Christ, such that it astonishes: They were astonished at His teaching, for He taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes (Mk 1:21). To teach one's own doctrines, to demand undue assent, to emphasize the accidental, to have a doubtful attitude about everything, to remain in insinuations or approximations, to problematize the simplest things, to obscure what is clear and not clarify what is obscure, to seek oneself in teaching, to prefer chaos to order, to be padded instead of sober, opaque instead of transparent, confused instead of precise, verbose instead of concise, to stir up the waters so that others may think they are deep… this is not to have the spirit of Christ, who said: Let your word be yes, yes; no, no; anything beyond this comes from the Evil One (Mt 5:37).
  8. Serenity and gentleness are another characteristic of evangelical teaching, which are not at odds with, but rather required even in, the lively reply, intelligent polemic, effective refutation, academic or apologetic discussion. One must never lose spiritual peace, moderation, affability, unction.
  9. Likewise, one must know how to let oneself be penetrated by the universality of Christ's teaching. Intellectual "sectarianism" is neither evangelical nor intellectual. Those who are incapable of making themselves understood by simple people, because they are compartmentalized, do not grasp the universality of the message of Jesus. Only the contemplative and the authentic intellectual do not forget that "there exists a fundamental unity that is prior to all pluralism, and that is the only thing that allows pluralism to be not only legitimate but desirable and fruitful"151.
  10. We must be impatient to preach the Word in every way, following the counsel of St. Paul: preach the word, be insistent in season and out of season, reprove, rebuke, exhort… (2 Tim 4:2). We must not be dumb dogs, unable to bark (Is 56:10). We must seek out the sheep, use the method of dialogue, of witness and of solidarity152, correct sinners, teach doctrine: faith comes through hearing (Rom 10:17), visit the sick, bring souls to the sacrament of Reconciliation. "Show by the depth of your convictions and by the coherence of your conduct that Jesus Christ is our contemporary"153.


    Works

  11. Our Lord not only spoke, but "did"; in particular, He became flesh (Jn 1:14) in the Incarnation, and He became sin154 in dying on the Cross in sacrifice for our sins. So numerous are the things that Jesus "did" that St. John says that if they were written one by one, I think this world could not contain the books that would be written (Jn 21:25).
  12. What is common to all the works of Christ is that He did nothing of Himself, but only what He sees the Father doing; for whatever He does, the Son does likewise (Jn 5:19), and this is what constitutes the essential work of Jesus: My food is to do the will of Him who sent me (Jn 4:34), so much so that He says of Himself: I can do nothing of myself… I do not seek my own will, but the will of Him who sent me (Jn 5:30). And here lies the true greatness of the Christian and religious vocation: union with God.
  1. The will of the Father has a first fundamental concrete form: the Kingdom… He has made us a Kingdom (Rev 1:6). Hence it must be our first concern: Seek first the Kingdom… (Mt 6:33). To build the Kingdom He called men in order to make them disciples: He was making more disciples… (Jn 4:1), and He formed them by teaching them, above all by example, so that they would do the same as He: For I have given you an example, that you also should do as I have done (Jn 13:15). In a similar way, as an integral element of our spirituality, we must know how to call, teach, direct, accompany and select priestly, diaconal, religious, missionary and secular vocations. Knowing that "the vocation is present in germ in the majority of Christians"155, we must fulfill the Pope's mandate: "with passion and discretion, be awakeners of vocations"156, knowing how to detect the action of the Holy Spirit in the soul, which drives it to irresistible demands of sacrifice for love of Christ, which is at the same time an act of integral faith and total self-giving, with the formal decision not to make pacts, not to compromise, not to capitulate, not to negotiate, not to make concessions, nor to come to terms with the spirit of the world.
  2. The letter of this Directory will be a dead letter if we do not know how to form young men of great spirit who know how to transmit to the new generations of our Religious Family the charism that the Holy Spirit granted to the Founder.
  3. The Incarnate Word also taught us to work for purification and renewal: making a whip of cords, He drove them all out of the temple (Jn 2:15); for reconciliation: He made the two peoples one (Eph 2:14); for not losing sight of the eminently supernatural character of the faith and of the Christian life, for which He did works that no one else has done (Jn 15:24); for attaining the oblative and Eucharistic attitude, imitating our Lord who did this once for all (Heb 7:27); for trying to do things as well as possible and not just any old way, since Jesus has done all things well (Mk 7:37), and as the good thief confessed: He has done nothing wrong (Lk 23:41).
  4. The miracles worked by our Lord are irrefutable proof of the credibility of our holy religion and a most eloquent manifestation of the divinity of our Lord Jesus Christ, God from God and Light from Light. He worked miracles over all the beings of creation: over the angels (good and bad), over the heavenly bodies, over men and over irrational creatures; as proof and argument of the faith He taught, that He freed men from the power of the demons, that He was the one who saved men, that He was the Master and Lord of all creation. In this way He raised up the Kingdom on solid and indestructible foundations. And on that solidity our spirituality must be founded, which must be steeled by trials, refined by tribulations, perfected by persecutions, unshakable before all the unleashed furies of hell, even should it fall to us to live in the times of the Antichrist… we follow Him who today, as yesterday, has all power, and therefore there is no room for any fear, and nothing can move us to renounce revealed truth and the love of Christ157.
  5. The miracle of the Transfiguration, by which He showed His disciples the glory and beauty of His body, must not only encourage us, through the brief contemplation of eternal joy, to bear difficulties, but must also remind us of the specific end of our very little Religious Family: to evangelize the culture, that is, to transfigure it in Christ. Therefore, on August 6, the feast of the Transfiguration of the Lord, we will always commemorate with solemnity that which constitutes us and must distinguish us among the various religious families of the Church.


    f) The priesthood of Christ

  6. By reason of the union of the Word with human nature and of the grace of headship, Jesus Christ is constituted true High and Eternal Priest. No one is so truly Priest as He; the others are in His image. No one is more High Priest than He, in whom the others participate by derivation. No one is Eternal Priest as He is, who will perpetuate His sacrifice on the altars until the end of the ages and will consummate His priesthood in heaven: You are a priest forever… (Ps 110:4), High Priest (Heb 4:14), Priest forever (Heb 6:20).
  7. He was, at the same time, priest and victim of His own sacrifice: He gave Himself up for us to God as an offering and sacrifice… (Eph 5:2).
  8. His priesthood has superabundant power to expiate all the sins of men: by His wounds we have been healed (Is 53:5).
  9. He is the most perfect mediator between God and men: For there is one God, and one mediator also between God and men… (1 Tim 2:5); He is mediator of a more excellent covenant (Heb 8:6).
  10. In fact, Jesus Christ communicates His own priesthood to His disciples, in diverse degrees, making them participate in it truly and really. It is in the liturgy that the Mystical Body of Christ, that is, "the Head and the members, exercises the whole of public worship. Consequently… it is a sacred action par excellence, whose efficacy, by the same title and to the same degree, is equaled by no other action of the Church"158.
  11. In the liturgy, the Incarnate Word prays for us –He is our Priest–, He prays in us –He is our Head–, and we pray to Him –He is our God–. We must recognize our voice in Him and His voice in us. We must pray with Him, since He prays with us; pray in Him, since He prays in us159.
  12. This participation in the priesthood of Christ is the essence of the sacramental character, which begins with Baptism, is perfected with Confirmation, and reaches its maximum fullness in Holy Orders.
  13. a) The baptismal character empowers us to receive the other sacraments, and empowers the ordinary faithful to act as the proper ministers in the sacrament of Matrimony (this is the highest realization of the common priesthood). How we ought to nourish our soul with the reality of our Baptism! How we ought to teach this reality to the lay Christian faithful!
  14. b) The baptismal character empowers every baptized person to offer the Eucharistic Sacrifice. We must teach this grand and impressive prerogative insistently to all the members of our Institutes who are not ministerial priests. The baptized participate in the oblation of the Victim of the altar to the Father –without themselves performing the liturgical rite– in two ways: first, they offer the sacrifice through the visible priest, since he offers in the name of all the members when acting in the person of Christ as Head and Shepherd; and second, together with the visible priest, by uniting their prayers of praise, of petition and of expiation.
  15. c) The character of Confirmation empowers one to confess the faith of Christ with courage and fortitude, by giving strengthening or corroborating grace, defending the faith against its adversaries with greater ease and as if by office.
  16. d) The character of Holy Orders configures one to Christ the Head, giving the ministerial priest power over the physical Body of Christ and over His Mystical Body. It enables him to act "in persona Christi"160, his principal act being the immolation and oblation of the sacrifice of the Mass161, and, in addition, blessing, presiding, preaching, baptizing, hearing confessions, etc. The members of our Institute who are ministerial priests must return again and again to this ineffable reality, which produced in them an ontological change by making them like Christ the Head, and no lay spirituality should diminish their priestly spirituality.

    Article 3: His Departure from this world


    a) Passion

  17. According to the plan of the Most Holy Trinity it was necessary that Jesus Christ suffer in order to save all men: Just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, so that everyone who believes in Him may not perish, but have eternal life (Jn 3:14-15); Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things and enter into His glory? (Lk 24:26); He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things… and be killed and rise again after three days (Mk 8:31). If in the present economy of salvation the Passion of Christ was necessary, our suffering will also be necessary. If there were another way to go to heaven, Jesus Christ would have followed it and, what is more, would have taught it. But it is not so: Christ went by the royal road of the holy Cross and taught us to go by it: If anyone wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross daily and follow me (Lk 9:23). St. Cyril of Jerusalem teaches: "Jesus, who had in no way sinned, was crucified for you; and you, will you not crucify yourself for Him, who was nailed to the Cross for love of you?"162.


    The cross in our life

  18. Therefore we must love the living cross of labors, humiliations, affronts, torments, pains, persecutions, misunderstandings, setbacks, disgraces, slights, insults, calumnies, death… and be able to say with St. Paul: I die every day (1 Cor 15:31), so as to nail in our heart Him who for us was nailed to the Cross163.
  19. We must vehemently desire the cross: "that I may die for love of your love, since for love of my love you deigned to die"164. It is a grace that must be asked for in prayer: God has given you the grace… of suffering for Christ (Phil 1:29). In a special way we must ask for the grace of the science of the cross and of the joy of the cross, which are attained only in the school of Jesus Christ.


    Love of the cross

  20. That is why we must have a very great devotion to the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ: "Everything is in the Passion. It is there that the science of the saints is learned"165. The love that is not born of the Cross of Christ is weak: "from whatever side we look at it, whether from the side of the person who suffers, or of the things He suffers, or of the end for which He suffers them, it is the highest and most divine and secret thing that has happened in the world since God created it, nor will anything like it happen until its end"166. The Cross "was the chair from which He taught, the altar on which He immolated Himself, the temple of His prayer, the arena where He fought, and as it were the forge from which so many wonders came forth"167. This devotion must take concrete form in knowledge and love of the Gospel accounts of the Passion (Mt 26-27; Mk 14-15; Lk 22-23; Jn 18-19), in the theology of the Passion and Redemption, in the contemplation of the holy places of Jerusalem, of crucifixes, of the Way of the Cross, of the beautiful texts of the Imitation of Christ on this subject168, of the Eucharist as the perpetuation of the Passion and Cross and second act of the one drama of the Redemption, of the cross in our life so well taught in the Circular Letter to the Friends of the Cross of St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, and finally, in fervor to bring the grace of the Redemption to all of reality: to man, to the whole man and to all men, to marriage and the family, to culture, to political, economic and social life, to the international life of peoples with special reference to the question of peace, that is, to all the great contemporary problems analyzed by the Pastoral Constitution Gaudium et Spes in its second part169.
  21. In Sacred Scripture we are taught that many men conduct themselves as enemies of the cross of Christ (Phil 3:18). This is because they reject it: He who does not take up his cross and follow me is not worthy of me (Mt 10:38); they cut it down170, they lower it: let him come down from the cross and we will believe in Him (Mt 27:42); they avoid being persecuted on account of the cross of Christ (Gal 6:12); they do not preach the whole message: if I still preach circumcision… then the scandal of the cross is done away with! (Gal 5:11).
  22. The Incarnate Word Himself teaches us to love the cross: deny yourself, take up your cross every day and follow me (Lk 9:23). Of this the saints give us an example, who bore in their bodies the sufferings of Jesus171, completing in us what is lacking in the Passion of Christ172.
  23. We must not wish to know anything except Jesus Christ and Jesus Christ crucified (1 Cor 2:2). This doctrine of the cross173 must be what we preach: the folly of preaching… of Jesus Christ crucified, a scandal to the Jews and folly to the Greeks (1 Cor 1:21-23). It is in this cross that we must glory, in imitation of the Apostle: I will glory only in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom the world is crucified to me, as I am to the world (Gal 6:14).
  1. This cross prepares for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure (2 Cor 4:17), for the sufferings of the present time are nothing in comparison with the glory that is to be revealed in us (Rom 8:18). Hence the Lord himself encourages us: Blessed will you be when they insult you and persecute you and falsely say every kind of evil against you because of me. Rejoice and be glad, for great will be your reward in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were be­fore you (Mt 5:11-12).
  2. There is no other school but the Cross, in which Jesus Christ teaches his disciples how they must be: It is “for us the supreme Chair of the truth of God and of man”174. Hence “in the school of the Incarnate Word we understand that it is divine wisdom to accept his cross with love: the cross of the humility of reason before the mystery; the cross of the will in the faithful fulfillment of the whole moral law, natural and revealed; the cross of one’s own duty, at times arduous and unrewarding; the cross of patience in sickness and in the difficulties of every day; the cross of tireless effort to respond to one’s own vocation; and the cross of the struggle against the passions and the snares of evil”175. And it is a Chair because in It “the glory of the Love that is ready for everything has been revealed”176. The cross is the only way of life, the sign of the predestined, the scepter of the kingdom of holiness; “it is the source of every blessing, the origin of every grace; through It, believers receive strength from weakness, glory from shame, and life from death”177.
  3. The saints of all times, true words of God178, show us the necessity of the cross in our lives: “Rightly is he glorified with Christ who, suffering for Him, truly suffers with Him”179. “Whoever wishes to lead a perfect life need do nothing else but despise what Christ despised on the Cross and love what Christ loved on it”180. “Jesus is loved and served on the Cross and crucified with Him, and in no other way”181.
  4. They themselves burned with desire for the cross: “If the Head is crowned with thorns, shall the members be crowned with roses? If the Head is mocked and covered with mud on the way to Calvary, shall the members wish to live perfumed upon a throne of glory?”182. “Those who relish the Cross of Christ our Lord find rest living in these labors, and die when they flee from them or find themselves without them”183. “To suffer or to die”184. “I want and choose poverty with Christ poor rather than riches, insults with Christ laden with them rather than honors, and I desire more to be esteemed as worthless and a fool for Christ, who was first held to be such, than as wise or prudent in this world”185. “I know well what is good for me… Let fire, the cross, packs of wild beasts, lacerations, amputations, dislocation of bones, severing of limbs, the crushing of my whole body, all the cruel torments of the devil come upon me, provided only that this may serve me to attain to Chris­t”186. “Allow me to imitate the Passion of my God”187. In a word, “neither Jesus without the Cross, nor the Cross without Jesus”188.
  5. And the saints remind us of the joy that is the fruit of this cross: “I have reached the point of not being able to suffer, for all suffering is sweet to me”189. We must hope in such a way that every sorrow gives us consolation. It “is the fulcrum, upon which leverage is applied to serve man, as well as to transmit to so very many others the immense joy of being Christians”190.
  6. The Cross of Christ demands of us a generous response: “(the Passion and the Cross)… are a persevering and unbending aspiration and a cry: an immense cry of hearts”191. “Jesus, who had sinned in nothing, was crucified for you; and will you not crucify yourself for Him, who was nailed to the cross out of love for you?”192. Hence it is also –because of the imitation of Christ that it implies– “a crown, not a disgrace”193. This is the resounding idea: to sacrifice oneself. Thus is history directed, even silently and hiddenly.


    Fittingness

  7. Of the many advantages that Christ’s redeeming us by his Passion has brought us, we must discover:
  8. a) How much God loves us and, therefore, how much we must love Him: God proved his love for us in that, while we were sinners, Christ died for us (Rom 5:8); I live by faith in the Son of God who loved me and gave himself up for me (Gal 2:20).
  9. b) The example he gave us of so many virtues necessary for human salvation, such as obedience, humility, constancy, justice, contempt for worldly things… Christ suffered for you, leaving you an example that you should follow in his steps (1 Pet 2:21).
  10. c) Not only how he redeemed us from sin, but the greatness of sanctifying grace and of the eternal glory that he merited for us.
  11. d) The greater need to keep ourselves free from sin: You have been bought at a great price! (1 Cor 6:20).
  12. e) The greater dignity of man, for although he was conquered by the devil, the devil was conquered by man: Thanks be to God who gave us the victory through Christ! (1 Cor 15:57).
  13. In short, we find a thousand mysteries in the Passion of the Lord, so that its abundance is inexhaustible.
  14. We must also consider attentively that Christ freely willed to suffer all the bitter sorrows of his Passion and Death; he did not prevent them though he could have prevented them: I lay down my life… No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down and power to take it up again (Jn 10:17-18). And showing that he kept all the vigor of his bodily nature until the last moment, crying out with a loud cry, in a loud voice he said: Father, into your hands I commend my spirit. Having said this he breathed his last (Lk 23:46).
  15. And he freely subjected himself to obedience in order to fulfill his Father’s command: By the obedience of one many will be made righteous (Rom 5:19). It is true that obedience is better than sacrifice (1 Sam 15:22), but sacrifice offered out of obedience is the highest perfection. Hence we must learn to live the vow of obedience freely and never fall into the destructive dialectic of opposing freedom to obedience or freedom to authority, and vice versa.
  16. So that, everything well considered, in the Passion is manifested the severity of God, who did not wish to forgive without fitting satisfaction: he did not spare his own Son; and his infinite goodness: he delivered him up for us all; from which there must spring up in us a boundless confidence in the overflowing generosity of the Father: How will he not give us all things with Him? (Rom 8:32).


    Example

  17. This is his second great abasement: the kenosis of the Passion. His humility shines forth in it and we must imitate it: in humility regard one another as superior (Phil 2:3). In this regard St. Thomas Aquinas teaches that in man two things can be considered: what there is of God in him, that is, everything that pertains to salvation and perfection; and what is proper to man, that is, everything that pertains to defect. And since humility properly refers to the reverence by which man subjects himself to God, it follows that every man, according to what is his own, must subject himself to every neighbor, as regards what is of God in him. In this subjection humility shines forth194.


    Modes

  18. Contemplating the different ways in which the Passion of the Lord attains its end, that is, the Salvation of men, leads us to admire his infinite Wisdom, who has done all things well (Mk 7:37); the delicacy of his love: he loved them to the end (Jn 13:1); the magnanimity and generosity of his action: where sin abounded grace superabounded (Rom 5:20). This so marvelous and exuberant work of art of the Savior gives rise to the most absolute certainty in the Salvation brought about by Him, by way of merit, of satisfaction, of sacrifice, of redemption and of efficacy. These five modes of causality of the redemptive work, far from dismembering it, make us see it with greater force and greater unity, like the fingers that perfect the hand.
  19. Thus Christ merited for all men of all times all the graces that they receive or will receive, without any exception whatsoever; a merit that is universal: He is the propitiation for our sins. And not only for ours, but for those of the whole world (1 Jn 2:2), superabundant, infinite and of strict justice.
  20. Thus Christ made satisfaction for the sins of all men by offering the Father a universal, superabundant reparation in strict justice. He bore the sins of all upon himself (Is 53:12); him who knew no sin, [God] made sin for us so that we might become the righteousness of God in Him (2 Cor 5:21); he became a curse for us (Gal 3:13), paying for all: Christ Jesus… gave himself as a Redemption for all (1 Tim 2:6).
  21. Thus Christ saves us by the sacrifice of the Cross, which is a unique oblation: we are sanctified by the offering of the Body of Christ, made once for all (Heb 10:10); it is a definitive sacrifice: instituted… according to the power of an indestructible life (Heb 7:16); it is an eternal sacrifice, since he holds a perpetual priesthood because he remains forever (Heb 7:24), and the Victim is perfect: and he is able to save perfectly (Heb 7:25), since he was at once a host for sin, a peace offering and a holocaust: he loved us and gave himself up for us as an offering and sacrifice of fragrant and pleasing odor (Eph 5:2). Should we not recall here that those are called religious who consecrate themselves totally to the service of God, offering themselves to Him as a holocaust?195.
  22. Thus Christ redeems us from all slaveries, since he came to give his life as a Redemption for many (Mt 20:28), as a Redemption for all (1 Tim 2:6); he gave himself for us to ransom us from all iniquity (Titus 2:14), freeing us from all the slaveries of sin, of the punishment of sin, of death, of the power of the devil and of the Mosaic law.
  23. Thus Christ is the efficient cause both of the objective Redemption, accomplished by Him on Calvary, and of the application to us of the fruits of the Redemption –the so-called subjective Redemption– through faith and the sacraments. He is the most efficient cause of our salvation because he is God, the Word of God, and because his humanity is the conjoined instrument of the divinity; hence all the actions and sufferings of Christ work, instrumentally, the salvation of the human race.


    Effects

  24. And if it is an abyss to consider the modes by which Jesus Christ saves us, the effects of his Passion are an abyss as well:

    a) it frees us from sin: he washed us from our sins in his blood (Rev 1:5);
    b) it frees us from the power of the devil: now the prince of this world will be cast out (Jn 12:31);
    c) it frees us from the punishment of sin: he took upon himself our infirmities and bore our sorrows (Is 53:4);
    d) it reconciled us with God: we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son (Rom 5:10);
    e) it opened to us the gates of heaven: having firm confidence to enter the sanctuary by virtue of the blood of Jesus (Heb 10:19);
    f) it grants him exaltation: therefore God exalted him (Phil 2:9).

  25. We must still treat of an aspect already hinted at but which we now wish to make explicit: I complete in my flesh what is lacking in the Passion of Christ (Col 1:24), teaches St. Paul. Certainly we add nothing substantial to the sacrifice of Christ, but in the order of operation we complete it in some way. In his sacrifice, Christ the Head offered to the Father his own sufferings and those of all the members of his Mystical Body; he took upon himself not only the sins of all men but all their sorrows, the sufferings that his members would have to suffer for love of him, since they would be persecuted because they served Him196. Christ “suffered as our Head, and continues to suffer in his members, that is, in us”197. That is why “Christ will be in agony until the end of the world,” since “the total measure of the sufferings of all men will not be filled up until the end of the world”198.


    Suffering

  1. Suffering is something precious and of incalculable value, since it was chosen by God to redeem us, when it is borne with patience, accepted as coming from God and sanctified by uniting it to that of Christ. Here is the deepest reason for our apostolate with the sick, which will always be a verification of our Christian authenticity. They must always be our favorites!
  2. All our sufferings had entered into the heart of Christ on Calvary. Those sufferings, offered beforehand by Christ, Priest and Victim, remained to be fulfilled in our flesh. The sacrifice of Christ, complete in his prophetic thought, will be totally fulfilled in reality when the number of the elect is complete. We are co-redeemers!
  3. All the co-redemptive efficacy of our sufferings depends on their union with the Cross, and in the measure and degree of that union. We live from the sacrifice of Christ: no act of virtue has value if it was not offered by our Head on the Cross; no sin is forgiven if Christ did not plead for mercy for it on Golgotha; no suffering is redemptive if it is not united to the Passion of Christ. If we do not learn to be victims with the Victim, all our sufferings are useless.
  4. We must learn to complete what is lacking in the Passion of Christ199 with a reparation that is affective –through prayer and love–, effective –the fulfillment of the duties of one’s state, apostolate, etc.–, and afflictive –sanctified suffering–, for our own benefit and that of the whole Mystical Body.
  5. That is why the great benefactors of our Institutes are their sick members, whom we must consider privileged members, for whom we must be ready to sell “the chalices”200 if it were necessary for their good.


    b) Death on the Cross and Burial

  6. Christ by his death kills death: Death has been swallowed up in victory; where, O death, is your victory? (cf. 1 Cor 15:54-55); and that is why his death gives life: it is the passage to his resurrection and to ours, both spiritual and bodily.
  7. That is why true Christians consider: if we have died with Him, we shall also live with Him (2 Tim 2:11). In Baptism we die, and that death assures us of the resurrection: If we have been grafted into Him by the likeness of his death, we shall be so also by that of his resurrection (Rom 6:5), offering with Him our daily sufferings, sharing in his sufferings, being conformed to Him in his death (Phil 3:10), until we can exclaim: for me to live is Christ and to die is gain (Phil 1:21).
  8. That is why it is “necessary to live dying”201, or as the poet sings: “He who does not know how to die / while he lives, is vain and foolish: / to die a little every hour / is the way to live […]/ from death I receive / new life, and if I live, / I live from so much dying”. This is the secret of all supernatural fruitfulness! Everything lies in knowing how to die! That is the great science! Unless the grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies it will bear much fruit. Whoever loves his soul loses it; but whoever hates his soul in this world will keep it for eternal life (Jn 12:24-25).
  9. But the Cross offers us another magnificent lesson. Just as all of us men had sinned and perished in solidarity in Adam, since through one man sin entered the world… inasmuch as all had sinned (Rom 5:12), so, in Christ, the new Adam, by that principle of communion (koinonia), we all find salvation and life: those who receive the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness will reign in life through the one man, Jesus Christ (Rom 5:17).
  10. Jesus Christ takes upon himself the representation of all men: “he suffered for me, paid for me and died for me”202. In Him we are recapitulated and, as it were, concentrated. This so intimate and mysterious interpenetration and, as it were, identification of Christ with each one of us is what caused Christ to take on our sins, to make them his own: he became sin203.
  11. Now then, not only did he die representing us, but all of us also died, represented and contained in Him. He and we, we and He, form a single whole, a compact block: you are all one in Christ Jesus (Gal 3:28). That is why we too die on the Cross, by the moral interpenetration or mystical identification of persons. By that mysterious solidarity or ineffable communion of men with Christ, at the root, we have died in Christ.
  12. Because Christ truly died representing all men, it was necessary that all of us, in some way, should die in Him and with Him: if one died for all, then all died (2 Cor 5:14), that is, all of us have died in Him. For all to die because the one dies presupposes that all are in the one and that the one and all are the same: we are all one… We must carry out in fact what was accomplished on the Cross at the root: we must die, in fact, to the old man, to sin, to sinful affections, even to the very appearance of evil.
  13. We must die totally to our own self. There are three moments in perfect self-denial: Christian mortification, the spirit of sacrifice, and total death to one’s own self. It is very difficult to rise to this third moment. It is achieved through perennial work. It is a matter of dying in order to live: you have died and your life is hidden with Christ in God (Col 3:3). The life of Christ was a continual death, whose last act and consummation was the Cross. Through various degrees of death the mystical life of Christ is established in us:
    – death to sins, even the slightest, and to the least imperfections;
    – death to the world and to all exterior things;
    – death to the senses and to immoderate care of one’s own body;
    – death to one’s temperament and natural defects: not speaking or acting according to one’s own mood or whim, always remaining at peace and in possession of oneself;
    – death to one’s own will and one’s own spirit: subjecting the will to reason, not letting oneself be carried away by whim or fancies, not being obstinate in one’s own judgment, knowing how to listen, always being joyful with what God gives us;
    – death to self-esteem and self-love: to amour-propre;
    – death to spiritual consolations, which one day God withdraws completely, and then everything bothers the soul, everything annoys it, everything wearies it; nature cries out, complains, becomes en­raged;
    – death to supports and securities regarding the state of our soul: experiencing abandonment by God…;
    – death to all ownership in what concerns holiness: complete nakedness. One no longer sees one’s gifts or virtues, only one’s sins, one’s own nothingness.
  14. We must learn to discover ourselves, and others, in the Heart and in the wounds of Christ, and thus we will understand what we ask of Him when we say: “within your wounds, hide us”204, since “we have been made Christ”205.


    Folly of the Cross

  15. But we must take into account yet one more thing: the folly of the Cross, which is the folly of God, wiser than the wisdom of men (1 Cor 1:23.25). This folly consists in living in the more and in the beyond, that is, where all balancing acts, all calculation, all “I give so that you may give to me” cease. This folly begins where one no longer counts, nor calculates, nor weighs, nor measures. Do you love only the one who loves you? Do you give only to the one who can give it back to you? Do you do favors only for those who thank you? What importance does all that have? Do not the pagans do the same? (Mt 5:47).
  16. The folly of the cross consists in living the beatitudes. Blessed are the fools for Christ! They will be pushed from here to there, people will laugh at them and consider them clumsy, backward and even feeble-minded: theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven. Blessed are the fools for Christ! For they have stripped themselves and stand before God in all their guilelessness. Blessed are these fools for Christ! No wisdom of the world will ever be able to deceive them. It is the folly of love without limits or measure. It is to bless those who curse us206, it is not to repay evil for evil (Rom 12:17). When the world says to us: Look at the fools! stones are thrown at them and they kiss the hand that throws them; people laugh at them and mock them and they laugh too, like children who do not understand; they are beaten, persecuted and martyred, but they give thanks to God who found them worthy. When the world says that, it is a sign that we are on the right path. Folly of love! But a folly that the folly of the Cross makes wiser than the wisdom of all men (1 Cor 1:25).


    c) The Descent into Hell

  17. St. Peter teaches that Christ went to preach to the spirits who were in prison (1 Pet 3:19), and he adds, the Gospel was proclaimed to the dead (1 Pet 4:6).
  18. The kenosis of the Incarnation of the Word is prolonged in his Passion and Death, and reaches its fullness when he descends into hell, so that, if we ceased to consider this truth of our faith, we would be diminishing somewhat the reality of the Incarnation.
  19. There Christ showed the fruit of his Passion to the saints of the Old Testament, to the Patriarchs and Prophets, to St. Joseph and St. John the Baptist, to the Holy Innocent Martyrs, to the good thief… and he communicated to them glorious beatitude.
  20. He also consoled the souls in purgatory, bringing out from there those who were sufficiently purified. In our preferential option for the poor we must always keep these blessed souls in mind, so as to offer suffrages for them.

    Article 4: His glorious Life

    a) Resurrection

    The Fact

  21. The fact of the Resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ is a Mystery and a double miracle. It is a theological miracle that surpasses the capacity of every created intellect, so that, if Christ has not risen, vain is our preaching, vain also is your faith (1 Cor 15:14). But, in turn, it is an apologetic miracle, since, through the empty tomb and the appearances, the prophecies are ful­filled and the truth of the divinity of Jesus Christ is certified. He is the firstborn of the dead (Col 1:18), the firstfruits of those who die (1 Cor 15:20), that is, the first to rise in a glorious way to an immortal life. He is the principle of the new creation or re-creation of the world, infinitely superior to the first creation.

    Effects
  22. The Resurrection of our Lord brought us two principal benefits: our future bodily resurrection and our present spiritual resurrection. That is why we are taught, on the one hand: as death came through a man, so also through a man came the resurrection of the dead (1 Cor 15:21); and, on the other: Christ was handed over for our sins and was raised for our justification (Rom 4:25); it is evident that justification is the resurrection of souls, spiritually dead through sin, to the life of grace. That is why St. Thomas teaches: “The good of grace of one individual is greater than the good of nature of the whole universe”207.
  23. It is in the Resurrection of the Lord that the importance of grace in the saving economy of the New Covenant is best seen, and why it is the “sole formal cause”208 of our salvation. Sanctifying grace is what makes us partakers of his divine nature (2 Pet 1:4); it is, as theologians say, a physical and formal, though analogous, participation in the divine nature itself. With Him we have been raised! (Eph 2:6), coming “to be by grace what He is by na­ture”209: children of God (cf. Rom 8:16), brothers of Christ, the Firstborn among many brothers (Rom 8:29) and, therefore, co-heirs with Him, heirs of God and co-heirs with Christ (Rom 8:17). Together with grace we are given the infused virtues and the gifts of the Holy Spirit.


    Sending of the Holy Spirit: the New Law

  1. On the very Sunday of his Resurrection, privately, Jesus Christ did what he would later do publicly on the day of Pentecost, sending the Holy Spirit: he breathed on them and said to them: receive the Holy Spirit (Jn 20:22), who constitutes the principal element of the New Law, or New Covenant, or Gospel, that is, the life, and life in abundance (Jn 10:10) that Christ came to bring to the earth. It is the law of Christ (Gal 6:2), it is the law of faith (Rom 3:27), a spiritual law210, the perfect law (Jas 1:25), the law of freedom (Jas 2:12), the law of the Spirit (Rom 8:2), the Gospel of grace (Acts 20:24). It is the infused law –poured into the heart– prophesied by Jeremiah: I will put my law within them and write it on their heart (Jer 31:33). It is “that in which all its power lies… in the very grace of the Holy Spirit, which is given through faith in Christ…”211. Only this law justifies and saves man: the Gospel… is the power of God for the salvation of everyone who believes… (Rom 1:16). Only insofar as it is infused, that is, insofar as it is communicated interiorly, does the new law, the Gospel, save and justify; insofar as it is secondarily written, it does not. That is why St. Paul says that the letter kills, the Spirit gives life (2 Cor 3:6), and by letter is to be understood anything or any writing that is outside of man, even if it concerns spiritual and heavenly things, such as those contained in the Gospels, “whereby even the letter of the Gospel would kill if it did not have the interior grace of faith, which heals”212. If this holds for the Holy Gospels, whose Principal Author is the Holy Spirit, how much more does it hold for this Directory! Without faith in Christ, without the Holy Spirit, without the love of God, without sanctifying grace, all these wri­tings would kill souls, since they would remain merely exterior things.
  2. To live according to the new law is to live in accordance with this reality: The love of God has been poured into our hearts by the power of the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us (Rom 5:5). It is to live according to faith that works through charity (Gal 5:6). And charity will never die! (1 Cor 13:8).


    Freedom

  3. Through the Resurrection and the sending of the Holy Spirit we understand that we have been called to freedom213, since for us to enjoy freedom, Christ has set us free (Gal 5:1).
  4. Freedom implies “building up a communion and a participation… on three inseparable planes:
    – man’s relationship with the world, as lord;
    – with persons, as brother;
    – and with God, as son”214.
  5. “Today the range of abuses of freedom has spread greatly, and this leads to new forms of slavery, very dangerous ones, because they are disguised under the appearance of freedom. This is the paradox, the profound drama of our time: in the name of freedom, slavery is imposed”215.
  6. Against these abuses St. Peter already warned, exhorting the faithful to be truly free and not as one who uses freedom as a cover for wickedness (1 Pet 2:16), and St. Paul, who admonished us not to take freedom as a pretext for serving the flesh216.
  7. Authentic freedom is identified with holiness, with the New Law, with the Christian faith, with charity; it is the freedom… of the children of God (Rom 8:21). It has truth as its foundation, as our Lord showed when he taught us that the truth will make you free (Jn 8:32). It is proper to those who let themselves be guided by the Holy Spirit: The Lord is Spirit, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom (2 Cor 3:17). That is why St. Augustine teaches: “Love and do what you will”217, and St. John of the Cross places at the summit of the mount of perfection: “Here there is no longer any path, for for the just man there is no law”218.
  8. Christian freedom “is being able to avoid sin and to do meritorious works, without anyone having the power to prevent one from attaining the last end”219.
  9. We must be, and must know how to form, men and women who are: “Free… free… free… free… free… free with your freedom… who will go everywhere with… the holy Gospel on their lips and the holy rosary in their hand, to bark like do­gs, to burn like live coals and to illumine the darkness of the world like suns”220.
  10. St. Thomas himself teaches: “He is free who is the cause of his own acting; he is a servant who has his master as the cause of his acting. Therefore, whoever acts by his own decision acts freely; whoever does so moved by another does not act freely. Thus, he who avoids evil, not because it is evil, but because God commands it, is not free; but he who avoids evil because it is evil, he is free. This is done by the Holy Spirit, who interiorly perfects the soul through a good habit, in such a way that it abstains from evil out of love, as if the divine law commanded it; and therefore it is called free, not because it does not submit to the divine law, but because it is inclined by good habits to do what the divine law commands”221.


    The New Man

  11. It is this New Law that brings forth the new man, who, by essence, is Jesus Christ himself: so as to create in himself one new man out of the two (Eph 2:15), and, by participation, all those who are incorporated into Jesus Christ by grace: put on the new man, created according to God in true righteousness and holiness (Eph 4:24). The new man is opposed to the old man, corrupted by seductive desires (Eph 4:22)222, of whom the Apostle prophesied: they will not endure sound doctrine; rather, with itching ears, they will heap up teachers in accordance with their passions (2 Tim 4:3).
  12. The new man is the interior man, powerfully strengthened… by the action of his Spirit (Eph 3:16223). The new man is identified with the spiritual man (1 Cor 2:15). He is the man who sings the new song, “a song… that only new men can learn, renewed from their former condition by the work of grace and already belonging to the New Testament, which is the Kingdom of Heaven. For it all our love sighs, and it sings the new song. But it is our life more than our voice that must sing the new song… Each one asks himself how he will sing to God. Sing to Him, but do it well. He does not accept a song that offends his ears… When will you be able to set yourself to sing with such art and mastery that you in no way displease such perfect ears? But see, He himself suggests to you the way you are to sing to Him: do not worry about the words, as if these were capable of expressing what delights God. Sing with jubilation. This is the song that pleases God, the one that is done with jubilation… (that is), to realize that we cannot express in words what the heart feels… This way of singing is the most fitting when it concerns the ineffable God. For if He is ineffable, He cannot be translated into words. And… on the other hand you are not permitted to remain silent, so the only thing you can do is sing with jubilation. In this way the heart rejoices without words, and the immensity of the joy is not limited by syllables”224.
  13. The supreme and eternal new man is Jesus Christ, who wants us to assimilate his doctrine, imitate his examples, unite ourselves in heart to his Person and frequent his sacraments. Modern immanentist conceptions abhor Him, proposing a “new man” who is nothing but a human wreck, since they deny him the supernatural dimension of grace. The only authentically new thing that there has been, is and will be in the world is Jesus Christ, who “in giving himself, has given newness to all things”225.
  14. The new man who is the Christian intones his own song: “song springs from joy, and, if we look at it carefully, it is born of love: to act is proper to love. He, then, who knows how to love the new life also knows how to sing the new song”226, “because singing and chanting psalms is the business of those who love”227. The Christian pilgrimage must be accompanied by song, by expressions of joy: “let us sing, then, now, brothers; not for the delight of rest but for the relief of our labor. As travelers are accustomed to do, you too sing and walk. Relieve your weariness as a traveler with song. Do not let sloth overcome you; sing and walk. What does walk mean? Always advance in the good. For there is no lack of those who go backward, going from bad to worse, as the Apostle says (2 Tim 3:13). If you progress and go forward, you walk; but progress in the good, progress in faith, do not turn back, do not stop”228.


    Joy

  15. From here too, from the Resurrection of the Lord, there arises an element that must be essential in our spirituality –and in every Christian spirituality–: joy, which, in our case, must be manifested in a special way in the celebration of the Lord’s day, Sunday; in the sense of feast; and in recreation, which we call eutrapelia.
  16. Joy, which is the gigantic secret of the Christian, is spiritual and supernatural, and is born from considering the mystery of the Incarnate Word. Rejoice, be glad, the angel Gabriel said to Mary; She would say later: my spirit exults with jubilation (Lk 1:47), Elizabeth having testified moments before: the child in my womb leaped for joy (Lk 1:44); and the angel had said to the shepherds: I announce to you a great joy, which is for all the people (Lk 2:10). It is born from verifying the mystery of the Resurrection of the Lord: filled… with great joy (Mt 28:­8); like the disciples of Emmaus: Were not our hearts burning within us while he spoke to us on the way? (Lk 24:32); they still did not believe for sheer joy and amazement (Lk 24:41); they returned… with great joy (Lk 24:52); the disciples rejoiced when they saw the Lord (Jn 20:20). That is why St. Paul insists: Rejoice, I say it again, rejoice (Phil 4:4).
  17. That is why the Christian must rejoice always and in everything: the Kingdom of God… is joy in the Holy Spirit (Rom 14:17); may the God of hope fill you with complete joy (Rom 15:13); coming to you with joy (Rom 15:32); rejoice (2 Cor 13:11); rejoice in the Lord (Phil 3:1); be always joyful (1 Thess 5:16).
  18. He must rejoice in the virtues: whoever practices mercy, let him do it with joy (Rom 12:8); joyful in hope (Rom 12:12); strengthened in every virtue according to the might of his glory, in all patience and long-suffering, with joy (Col 1:11).
  19. He must rejoice also in sufferings: I overflow with joy in all our tribulations (2 Cor 7:4); as sorrowful yet always rejoicing (2 Cor 6:10); I rejoice in my sufferings (Col 1:24).
  20. He must rejoice also in the community of the followers of the Risen One: I rejoice over you (Rom 16:19); my joy is also yours (2 Cor 2:3); we wish to contribute to your joy through your firmness in the faith (2 Cor 1:24); always, in all my prayers, I pray with joy for you (Phil 1:4); Brothers… my joy and my crown (Phil 4:1); you are our glory and our joy (1 Thess 2:20).
  21. He must rejoice with Christ proclaimed: that Christ is proclaimed, in this I rejoice and will rejoice (Phil 1:18); I will remain with you for your progress and joy in the faith (Phil 1:25); make my joy complete (Phil 2:2); receiving the word with joy in the Holy Spirit (1 Thess 1:6).
  22. At bottom, joy springs from considering that God is229, that Christ is: Take courage, I am (Mk 6:50); that truth prevails over falsehood, good over evil, beauty over ugliness, love over hatred, peace over war, mercy over vengeance, life over death, grace over sin, and finally, being over nothingness, Our Lady over Satan, Christ over the Antichrist, God over all. “God is infinite joy”230.


    Sunday

  1. From the Paschal Mystery and from the Lord’s day –Sunday–, which is the feast day par excellence, our feasts must be born, since the authentic feast must be born from worship, that is, from the praise rendered to the Creator for the goodness of existence, since on the seventh day God saw that everything was good… and he rested (Gen 1:31; 2:2-3). And, as St. Augustine teaches, worship takes place through “the offering of praise and thanksgiving”231, and since the prin­ci­pal act of worship is sacrifice, sacrifice thus becomes the soul of the feast. How much more meaning this has in the light of the Resurrection of the Lord and of the perpetuation of his Sacrifice on the altars! Indeed, through the Incarnation of the Word and his Paschal Mystery, man is raised to a supereminent plane, the plane of grace, which absolutely exceeds the order of nature. Therefore it is the Paschal Mystery, and Sunday, which is its prolongation, that is the source of the authentic fea­st, where we celebrate both the goodness of this higher existence that the Word brought us, for grace and truth have come to us through Jesus Christ (Jn 1:17), and the hope of being able finally to enter into eternal Beatitude, the true Feast, of which the Paschal Mystery and Sunday are a sign and a foretaste. The very name by which Christian tradition calls the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass refers to this thanksgiving: “Eucharist”. And it is already an entering into the true feast, that of eternity: for us who live here, our feasts are an open access to that life. We go from feast to feast toward the Feast232.


    Feast

  2. Every other feast also takes part in the Resurrection of the Lord and in Sunday, since the motive of a feast is joy: “A feast is joy and nothing more”233. But joy presupposes a foundation, something to rejoice in: it is the response of a lover to whom what he loves has been granted. Someone rejoices because he possesses the good that is fitting for him, whether really, or in hope, or at least in memory. And only he truly rejoices who rejoices in love: “Where charity rejoices, there is festivity”234. That is why there is no greater motive for joy than the Resurrection of the Lord, because his triumph is our triumph, his victory is our victory. The Paschal Mystery is the manifestation par excellence of God’s love for men: he loved them to the end (Jn 13:1). That is why we must rejoice in this mystery, and our joy must be permanent. Hence in our Institutes the great solemnities must be celebrated fittingly, especially the Octave of Easter, Sundays, and the days of the Apostles and of Our Lady.
  3. Likewise there is, as it were, another feast in miniature, which is recreation, in which the virtue of eutrapelia must be practiced. The soul, like the body, grows tired and must rest. The rest of the soul is delight, so that delight must be the remedy against the weariness of the soul, interrupting the tension of the spirit. Hence it is necessary to procure this rest for the soul through games and feasts, whose moderation belongs to the virtue of eutrapelia, practiced in times of recreation.
  4. Whoever does not practice the virtue of eutrapelia gladly will hardly persevere. One must be very wary of those who, under the cloak of virtue, do not wish to practice the virtue of eutrapelia; generally they are carried away by their selfishness, fail in charity toward their neighbor and are harsh in judgment. The worst is that they go against the truth of man, who, because he is intelligent, must know how to be playful.


    Sending and Mission

  5. The risen Christ, as the four evangelists indicate, gives us the mandate of the mission: As the Father has sent me, so I send you (Jn 20:21); in which we must bear witness: You will bear witness to this (Lk 24:48); by the power of the Lord, addressing all nations, of the whole world: Go and teach all nations (Mt 28:19), throughout the whole world (Mk 16:15); to preach the Gospel, teaching them to observe what He commanded us, baptizing in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, so that whoever believes will be saved; Christ accompanying us until the consummation of the world (Mt 28:20).
  6. In order not to shy away from the missionary adventure235, and to move many others to it, one must have something of the poet, since peoples have been moved by none but poets, and what greater poet than Jesus Christ! And what greater poetry than his crying out to us: Put out into the deep! Duc in altum! (Lk 5:4). A profound word, of very profound content, of deep mystical resonances, which impels to great ideals, understood by those who are men of action, of broad vision, of resolute and generous heart, who because of the nobility of their soul smile with joy on knowing that it is Jesus himself who says to them Duc in altum! It is an invitation to carry out great works, extraordi­nary undertakings in which there is much of adventure, of vertigo, of danger, where the waves toss the boat, the salt water splashes the face, the prow cuts its way for the first time, where there are no tracks and the only points of reference are the stars, where the keel is tossed by opposing whirlpools, the unfurled sails receive the fury of the wind, the masts creak… and the soul trembles… Into the deep! far from the shore and from the solid ground of merely human, calculating and cold thoughts… where the water seethes, the heart beats fast, where the soul knows heavenly inebriations and fascinating joys. To put out into the deep is to take seriously, thoroughly, the demands of the Gospel: go, sell all that you have… (Mt 19:21); it is the longing of our restless heart, which yearns to possess the Infinite; it is the impetus of the Saints and the Martyrs, who gave everything for God. It is what is proper to fishermen: humble, hardworking men, who do not fear dangers, watchful, patient in prolonged vigils, constant in repeating their outings to sea, prudent in hauling in the fish, weathered by salt and sun. It is to make oneself ready to die, like the grain of wheat, in order to see Christ in all things. For this reason, we make our own the ardent words of St. Luigi Orione: “Whoever does not want to be an apostle, let him leave the Congregation: today, whoever is not an apostle of Jesus Christ and of the Church is an apostate”236.


    b) Ascension

  7. The Ascension of Christ into heaven, among other things, moves us always to seek the essential things, which are invisible to the eyes of the body, and which are those things that do not pass away and do not die: Aspire to the things above, where Christ is… relish the things above, not those of the earth (Col 3:1-2).
  8. In these times of crude temporalism, in which many contemporaries are infected by secularism, we must give clear witness of being men who are not absorbed in the horizons of this world, not even in the unavoidable commitment to promote justice.
  9. Likewise, the Ascension of the Lord must fill us with unshakable hope, since he assured us: In my Father’s house there are many dwelling places… I am going to prepare a place for you… I will come again and take you with me, so that where I am you also may be (Jn 14:2-3). “It is necessary that the members follow wherever the Head goes before them”237. His Ascension is the cause of ours. “He with us is one single thing”238. All with Christ “form one single Christ”239. We are citizens of heaven! (Phil 3:20).
  10. They were looking at the sky, their gaze fixed on Him… (Acts 1:10). We will always have a preferential place in our Institute for the contemplative branches. Contemplative life, as St. John Paul II teaches, is “the core from which you prepare to give full meaning to your religious life, as ambassadors of Christ240 in the midst of this world”241. And the reason for this lies in the fact that contemplative life implies a permanent dedication to God and to the consideration and love of his mystery and of his plan of salvation for all men, according to that saying of St. Paul: Seek the things above, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God… You have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God (Col 3:1.3). That is why St. John Paul II says: “To dedicate oneself to God is… the noblest and highest form of activity of the human being, inasmuch as he concentrates himself entirely on the adoration of the Infinite Being, who wills the salvation of all humanity… Here, then, is the precise form of collaboration that… you offer to the Church for the good of souls”242. In this sense St. John of the Cross says: “Let those, then, who are very active, who think to encircle the world with their preaching and exterior works, take note here that they would bring much more profit to the Church and be much more pleasing to God if they spent even half the time being with God in prayer. Certainly then they would do more, and with less labor… otherwise it is all hammering away and doing little more than nothing, and at times even harm”243. And likewise St. Thérèse of the Child Jesus: “Only this does Jesus ask of us. He has no need of our works, but only of our love”244. And thanks to the prayer and sacrifice of those who consecrate themselves to God in contemplative life, the Lord raises up preachers, confessors, spiritual directors, etc., who transmit the truth to men, so that it can truly be said that others transmit what they contemplate.


    c) At the Right Hand of the Father

  11. He sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high (Heb 1:3): according to St. John Damascene, this refers to “the glory and honor of the divinity”245, that is, it means that Christ reigns together with the Father and, moreover, has judicial power over the living and the dead. Knowing that the Lord is with the Father must make our confidence in Him grow immeasurably: I can do all things in him who strengthens me (Phil 4:13), in keeping with that other magnificent expression of total confidence: I know in whom I have placed my trust! (2 Tim 1:12).


    d) Kingship of Christ

  12. The Lord is King of all humanity in the strict, literal and proper sense, since he received from the Father authority, power and the Kingdom (Dan 7:13), and that is why he is King of kings and Lord of lords (Rev 19:16). The foundation of this kingship is fourfold: in the first place, it belongs to him because he is God, the Incarnate Word, and thus St. Cyril of Alexandria says: “Christ obtains dominion over all creatures, not seized by force nor taken for any other reason, but by his very essence and nature”246; in the second place, it belongs to him by virtue of the Redemption, by right of conquest, since he bought us with his blood: You were not ransomed… with perishable gold or silver, but with the precious blood of Christ (1 Pet 1:18-19); in the third place, because he is Head of the Church, by the fullness of grace: full of grace and truth (Jn 1:14); in the fourth place, by right of inheritance: whom he appointed heir of all things (Heb 1:2). His royal authority includes the fullness of the threefold power: legislative, judicial and executive.
  13. The sphere of his kingship is twofold, personal and social. He reigns over intellects because he is the Truth, “and it is necessary that men receive the truth from Him with obedience”247; he reigns over wills because he is Goodness, “in such a way that he inflames us toward the noblest things”248; and he reigns over hearts because he is Love. And he also reigns socially, since “there is no difference between individuals and civil society, because individuals, united in society, are not for that reason any less under the power of Christ than each of them is separately. He is the source of private and public well-being”249.
  14. Certainly His is not a temporal or earthly kingdom: my Kingdom is not of this world (Jn 18:36), but rather it is an eternal Kingdom: his Kingdom will have no end (Lk 1:33), and it is universal: to him was given all power in heaven and on earth (Mt 28:18); a Kingdom of truth and life, of holiness and grace, of jus­tice, love and peace250.
  15. All missionary and apostolic work is founded on the conviction that He must reign (1 Cor 15:25).

    Article 5: His mystical Life

    a) The Church, Mystical Body

  1. From his Resurrection and forever, the sacred humanity of Jesus Christ lives a glorious and immortal Life, which since the moment of the Ascension is in heaven and which will return when his second Coming takes place.
  2. But, in the span of the centuries between the Ascension and the Parousia, he lives his mystical Life here on earth. Indeed, just as the Word assumed a human nature to fulfill the design of salvation, so, to prolong that design throughout the ages, he chooses other human natures so that salvation may reach all men of all times. The Church is Jesus Christ continued, diffused and communicated; she is like the prolongation of the redemptive Incarnation, continuing the threefold function: prophetic, priestly and royal; she is the new People of God; “she is in Christ like a sacrament or sign and instrument of intimate union with God and of the unity of the whole human race”251; she is an organically structured community252, “a people gathered by the unity of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit”253. The Church is mystery, communion and mission254. Our belonging to Her, through faith and Baptism, must be our badge of honor. The Church always, in the light of the Word of God, celebrates the mysteries of Christ for the salvation of the world255.


    b) Her “history”

  3. The reality of the Church-mystery precedes the creation of the world, as St. Clement of Rome says: “the Church is not of now, but of before”256, and the Shepherd of Hermas: “she was created… before all things… for her sake the world was set in order”257.
  4. She was, in a mediate way, “prepared in a remarkable way in the history of the people of Israel and in the Old Testament”258; in an immediate way, “established in the last times”259 by Jesus Christ, who gave her doctrine, the sacraments and the hierarchy, in a special way in Peter, through whom her constitution begins when he confesses, and is told: You are Peter and upon this rock I will build my Church (Mt 16:18); her preparation culminates on the summit of Calvary, when blood and water flow from the wounded side of the crucified Jesus Christ, and “as often as the sacrifice of the Cross, in which Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed (1 Cor 5:7), is renewed on the altar, the work of our Redemption is carried out”260; she was publicly “manifested by the outpouring of the Holy Spirit”261, both in the Pentecost of the Jews262 and in the Pentecost of the pagans263; and “she will be gloriously perfected at the end of time”264, when all the just “from Abel the just to the last of the elect”265 will be united before the Father in a Universal Church.
  5. We must always consider this so as not to fall into the temptation of thinking that the Church begins with us. On our shoulders there are not only 2000 years of history, but the past of the world and all its future.


    c) Her “soul”: the Holy Spirit

  6. In the Church the Holy Spirit plays a fundamental, essential role, constitutive of the Church herself. Indeed, it is He who brings about our incorporation into the Mystical Body of Christ, of which we are members by virtue of Baptism, since it is necessary to be reborn of water and the Spirit266: we have been baptized in one Spirit to form one body (1 Cor 12:13).
  7. The Holy Spirit had already been revealed in the Old Testament, although not as a Divine Person, under different aspects related both to the life of the People of Israel and to that of its members. Thus he intervenes in creation267; he is a source of life: I will pour out my Spirit upon your offspring… and they will grow as willows grow among the grass, beside flowing streams of water (Is 44:3-4); his action is directive, both of Israel: The Lord came down in the cloud and spoke to Moses, and taking some of the Spirit that was in him, he poured it upon the seventy men (Num 11:25), and, prophetically, of the Messiah: The Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon Him, a spirit of wisdom and understanding, a spirit of counsel and fortitude, a spirit of knowledge and piety, and he shall be filled with the spirit of the fear of the Lord (Is 11:2-3), upon Him I have poured out my Spirit (Is 42:1); by his action the prophets speak: and as soon as the Spirit rested upon them, they began to prophesy (Num 11:25); his action renews, purifies the heart, sanctifies: do not take your holy spirit from me… create in me, O God, a pure heart… sprinkle me, and I shall be purified; you will wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow (Ps 51:9.12.13).
  8. In the New Testament all these aspects are taken up again and acquire a new meaning in the light of the revelation, made by Jesus Christ, of the Holy Spirit as a Divine Person. And in the very life of the Incarnate Word, the Holy Spirit is present in a most particular way. He is the author of the hypostatic union, which is called the “grace of union”268, so that He brings about the Incarnation: the Holy Spirit will come upon you (Lk 1:35). Hence Christ “is the supreme marvel accomplished by the Holy Spirit”269, and we confess in the Creed that he “was conceived by the work and grace of the Holy Spirit”270, that is, “by the power of the Spirit”271. It is this same Spirit who is the author of the holiness of Jesus: therefore what will be born of you will be Holy (Lk 1:35). Even the response of the Blessed Virgin, from which the Incarnation followed, is the fruit of the action of the Holy Spirit in her soul272, who establishes a most personal, spousal, nuptial relationship with Her, a union that is a model for us273, since the Spirit dwells in you (1 Cor 3:16), for he dwells in every soul in grace. He is also present in the messianic prayer and preaching of Jesus; Christ applies to himself the fulfillment of the prophecy of Is 61:1: the Spirit of the Lord is upon Me because He has anointed me; He has sent me to bring the Good News to the poor, to proclaim liberty to captives and sight to the blind, to set the oppressed free, to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favor (Lk 4:18ff.); he is the source of Christ’s joy: In that hour he thrilled with joy in the Holy Spirit (Lk 10:21); from Him Christ has the power to cast out demons: If it is by the Spirit of God that I cast out demons, it is evident that the kingdom of God has come upon you (Mt 12:28). He is present in his redemptive Sacrifice, which Jesus calls a “Baptism”: Can you drink the cup that I must drink and receive the Baptism that I must receive? (Mk 10:38); I came to bring fire upon the earth, and what am I to desire but that it be kindled! I have a Baptism with which to be baptized, and how distressed I am until it is accomplished! (Lk 12:49). John the Baptist had already announced that he who comes after me… will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire (Mt 3:11), and we are taught that through his eternal Spirit he offered Himself without blemish to God (Heb 9:14). Finally, the Holy Spirit is the author of the Resurrection of Christ: he was put to death in the flesh, but called to life by the Spirit (1 Pet 3:18). And in this he is manifested as the Vivifier, “Lord and giver of life”274, as we profess in the Creed. Precisely, the Gospel, the New Law, consists in the grace of the Holy Spirit, which is why it is the “law of life”: The law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set me free from the law of sin and death (Rom 8:2).
  9. This same Spirit, after the Resurrection and Ascension of Jesus into heaven, is sent to the Church, according to the Lord’s own promise: when the Intercessor comes, whom I will send you from the Father, the Spirit of Truth, who proceeds from the Father, He will bear witness to Me (Jn 15:26). “Once the Lord Jesus had been exalted on the Cross and glorified, he poured out the Spirit he had promised, through whom he called and gathered together in unity of faith, hope and charity the People of the New Testament, which is the Church”275. This sending was fulfilled on the day of Pentecost: they were all filled with the Holy Spirit (Acts 2:1-4). Therefore the Holy Spirit is a gift given by God to the Church: you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit (Acts 2:38), a gift that at the same time gives her life: If you knew the gift of God and who it is that says to you, “give me a drink,” you would have asked Him and He would have given you living water (Jn 4:10), and gives her life to such a degree that he is rightly called the “Soul of the Church,” for “he so vivifies, unifies and moves the whole body that his operation could be compared by the Holy Fathers to the service performed by the principle of life, or soul, in the human body”276. Therefore the Holy Spirit is the source of the unity of the Church: we were baptized in one Spirit, to be one body (1 Cor 12:13), a unity that is based on charity, which is also the fruit of the action of the Spirit: The love of God has been poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit who has been given to us (Rom 5:5). Rightly, then, did St. Augustine call the Holy Spirit the “Kiss, Love, Bond of union between the Father and the Son”277. Consequently the Spirit is also the source of the holiness of the Church, since He himself is Holy: Spirit of holiness (Rom 1:4) and, therefore, works sanctification: God has chosen you… through the sanctification of the Spirit (2 Thess 2:13).
  10. He works this sanctification interiorly in our hearts, since the Spirit of God dwells in you (1 Cor 3:16), and by this renewing presence he transforms us, making us children of God: and because you are sons, God sent into our hearts the Spirit of his Son, who cries “Abba, Father!” So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, also an heir, by the favor of God (Gal 4:6-7); those who are moved by the Spirit of God, these are children of God (Rom 8:14ff.). He renews us, makes us new creatures, built up by God: You are being built together in the Spirit into a dwelling place of God (Eph 2:22), and through this renewal and purification, and in Him, we can have access to the Father: both of us have access to the Father in one Spirit (Eph 2:18). Therefore, he is the pledge of eternal life: the Spirit of the promise, who is the pledge of our inheritance (Eph 1:13-14), and who already here teaches us all things, because he searches everything (cf. 1 Cor 2:10-11); He will teach you all things (Jn 14:26) because he is the Spirit of Truth (Jn 14:17); he helps us, since the Spirit comes to the aid of our weakness, for we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit intercedes for us with inexpressible groanings (Rom 8:26); he enriches the Church with the diversity of his gifts: all these things are worked by one and the same Spirit, distributing to each one as he wills (1 Cor 12:11). We must, then, always ask the Lord to grant us his Holy Spirit, and docility to Him, so that we may belong ever more to Christ, and therefore to his Mystical Body, since if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to Christ (Rom 8:9). God never denies this most precious gift, in which the whole New Law consists, and therefore all our salvation: If you, who are evil, know how to give good things to your children, how much more will the Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him! (Lk 11:13). We must, then, understand that the Kingdom of God is… justice, peace and joy in the Holy Spirit (Rom 14:17).


    The Word of God

  11. The Holy Spirit is the Principal Author of Sacred Scripture, because he willed that we might be nourished abundantly at “the table of the Word”278. “The Holy Spirit made Sacred Scripture fruitful with a truth more abundant than men can comprehend”279. The Bible is closely united to the mystery of the Incarnate Word: “just as the substantial word of God became like us in all things except sin, so the words of God expressed in human languages have become like human language in all things, except error”280.
  12. The richness of this heavenly treasure281 of the Word of God is seen, among other things, in the fact that it is:
    – contained in the Holy Books,
    – celebrated in the Sacred Liturgy,
    – commented on by the Holy Fathers,
    – witnessed to by the Martyrs,
    – deepened by the Doctors,
    – authentically interpreted by the Magisterium of the Church,
    – and lived by the saints of all times.
  1. The Word of God must be “deepened in the Church”282, that is, with the same Spirit with which it was written283, and it is truly the strength of our Institute, “as is its supreme sacramental proclamation in the Eucharistic Sacrifice”284.
  2. Sacred Scripture must be the soul of our soul, of our spirituality, theology, preaching, catechesis and pastoral work. It should be possible to say of us what St. Jerome said of a person he knew: “Through daily reading and meditation of Scripture, he has made of his heart a library of Christ”285, for to us “the Word of God represents nothing less than the Body of Christ”286.


    d) One Church

  3. “This is the one Church of Christ, which in the Creed we confess to be One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic”287.
  4. The Church of Christ is One and unique. The idea of koinonia (communion) adequately expresses the profound core of the Mystery of the Church, since this is the mystery of the personal union of each man with the Most Holy Trinity and with other men, begun by faith and Baptism.
  5. This common union implies the vertical dimension, that is, communion with God, and the horizontal dimension, that is, communion among men. This unity is a gift of God, the fruit of the divine initiative accomplished in the Paschal Mystery.
  6. Ecclesial communion is at the same time invisible and visible. As an invisible reality it is the communion of each man, on the one hand, with the Father through Christ in the Holy Spirit and, on the other, with other men, who also share in sanctifying grace288, in the Passion of Christ289, in the same faith290, in the same Spirit291. All these are realities that bear an intimate relationship with the visible communion founded on the teaching of the Apostles, on the sacraments and on the hierarchical order. This relationship between the visible and the invisible aspects of ecclesial communion is constitutive of the Church as the “universal Sacrament of salvation”292.
  7. This reality, at once hierarchical and mystical, visible and spiritual, earthly and heavenly, canonical and charismatic, human and divine, by a profound analogy “resembles the Mystery of the Incarnate Word”293, since “Christ himself is Incarnate in his Body, the Church”294. Therefore we, who take pride in calling ourselves religious “of the Incarnate Word,” would most gravely betray our charism if we did not work to have an authentic ecclesial spirituality, which incorporates us fully into the Church of the Incarnate Word. And we want to know nothing outside of Her.
  8. The most solid unity goes together with a diversification that does not hinder unity, but rather makes it communion. The universality of the Church, “within the unity of faith and the one divine constitution of the Church”295, accepts the plurality of ministries, charisms, forms of life and of apostolate, diverse liturgical and cultural, theological, spiritual and disciplinary traditions. This reality “admirably shows the undivided catholicity of the Church”296.
  9. We must be fervent promoters “of the unity that does not hinder diversity, as well as… of a diversity that does not hinder unity but enriches it”297. We are called to build up, respect and safeguard this unity in diversity each day, above all through that love which is the bond of perfection (Col 3:14). As the Angelic Doctor says: “The Church is one… by the unity of charity, because all are united by the love of God, and among themselves by mutual love”298.
  10. An expression of this unity in diversity are the religious institutes, among which we wish to be counted, which, although they do not belong to the hierarchical structure of the Church, belong “indisputably to her life and holiness”299. When they have a supra-diocesan character, rooted in the Petrine ministry, they are also elements at the service of communion among the various particular Churches. This is an important fact for our spirituality, as we already experience.


    Being of One Mind

  11. We aspire, in accordance with the words of St. Paul, to be of one mind (2 Cor 13:11) in the Lord (Phil 4:2). This unanimity or concord that we seek means unity in the judgment of reason about what must be done, and unity of wills so that all want the same thing. Thus, this concord is born of the same faith, through which we know what we must do, and of charity, through which we all love the same goods and share the labors as good soldiers of Christ Jesus300. This union exists because all think alike about God, that is, because wisdom reigns among all, which is that savory knowledge of divine things, a wisdom that is not of this age… a divine, mysterious, hidden wisdom (1 Cor 2:6-7), which only Jesus Christ crucified can grant, the power and wisdom of God for those who are called (1 Cor 1:24).
  12. In this unanimity of feelings toward one another (Rom 12:16) the Kingdom of God becomes present, since “the nature of the Kingdom is the communion of all human beings among themselves and with God”301.
  13. This presupposes that each of the members of our Religious Family not only rejoices in the good he possesses: joy in the Holy Spirit (Rom 14:17), but also tends unceasingly toward greater perfection: Forgetting what lies behind, I strain forward to what lies ahead, looking toward the goal (Phil 3:13-14).
  14. And it also presupposes setting aside everything that may hinder or distort this unanimity of mind. In the first place, pride, by which we inordinately seek our own excellence and do not wish to submit to others or to recognize the excellence of others; and therefore where there is pride, only discord dwells: Among the proud there are always insults (Prov 13:10). Another obstacle is the presumption of wisdom, by which we do not accept the teachings of others, believing ourselves sufficient, contrary to the example of St. Paul: I set before them the Gospel that I preach among the Gentiles… to know whether I was running or had run in vain (Gal 2:2).
  15. The Holy Spirit poured into our hearts, who is the New Law, is the one who makes us preserve the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace (Eph 4:3). He it is who, through love, makes us come out of selfishness, of particularism, of the spirit of opposition and distrust, of the spirit of reserve, of non-participation, of non-agreement with others. On the contrary, the Spirit of Christ is a Spirit of communion: strive to have unanimity of feelings toward one another (Rom 12:16), so that we truly care for others, that all the members may be equally concerned for one another (1 Cor 12:25). Hence in our communities, which must be a unity among persons, a body full of members who think, no one should behave as a closed and solitary whole, like a self-sufficient nomad; rather we must learn to act as the parts or members of a whole.
  16. The concord we seek is also destroyed both by obsequiousness and by servility, which sacrifice the truth and one’s own conscience in an attempt to maintain a false peace, not to cross a friend, to avoid some problem or, at times, to gain an advantage through silence or applause. Those who do such things resemble those Jews who believed in Him, but because of the Pharisees did not confess him, fearing to be expelled from the synagogue, for they loved the glory of men more than the glory of God (Jn 12:42-43).
  17. The fruit of this common mind in the Lord is precisely peace: the fruit of justice is sown in peace for those who make peace (Jas 3:18). True peace, given by Christ302, is the tranquility of order. It arises naturally when all, through faith and charity, are ordered to God. And together with peace, another effect of this unanimity of mind toward one another according to Christ Jesus (Rom 15:5) is the unity that reigns among all, even in the least details of daily life or of any decision. This can happen because each one has the certainty that in everything we do we seek the glory of God, having for this one heart and one soul303.


    e) Holy Church

  18. The Church is the “Communion of saints”304. It is the common union through the visible sharing of holy goods, especially the Eucharist, which is the root of the invisible common union of those who share in them, that is, of the saints. There is a spiritual koinonia among the members of the same Body, which leads to an effective union in charity and in prayer. Not only among those of us who are still pilgrims on earth, but also between these and all those who have reached the end and are in heaven or on their way to it, in purgatory.
  19. There is a mutual relationship among the various states of the one Church, because, being members of Christ (cf. 1 Cor 12:12-27), in whom the fullness of grace resides (cf. Jn 1:16), in Him we also are members of one another (Rom 12:5) and benefit spiritually from one another. Hence the importance of being aware of Christ’s intercession for us: he always lives to make intercession for us (Heb 7:25); of the intercession of the saints, who “are intimately united to us”305; of their examples, which “impel us to seek the city to come… perfect union with Christ, that is, holiness”306; and, eminently among the saints, of the intercession of the Blessed Virgin Mary, “to whose protection the faithful have recourse with their supplications in all their dangers and needs”307. Hence the depth of devotion to the saints corresponds to the reality of the Church as a mystery of communion.
  20. The saints are a most eloquent sign of the vitality of the Church, and therefore they have an apologetic value for the truth of our faith, by concretely realizing the mark of the holiness of the Church. They are the best members of the Mystical Body of Christ and the greatest and most complete fruit of the Incarnation and the Redemption. And thanks to this they are the ones who transform the world by their example and by the power of their intercession. They constantly commend to us heaven, eternal life, the reward of merits, God! That is why in honoring them we honor God himself, for the saints are his masterpiece, and in “crowning their merits the Lord crowns his own gifts”308. Even after their death they fulfill posthumous missions, pushing entire generations to the heroism of following Jesus Christ, being not only for the young but also for the great majority of the nation… an example of nobility and a memorial of virtue (2 Macc 6:31). That is why there is no history more complete, more magnificent and more profitable than the Litany of all the saints; it evokes and invokes all the great spirits who have illustrated the planet and who have made humanity advance by their virtues. Likewise we must venerate their relics, which do not cease to work.
  21. Therefore, for our part, we must do everything possible so that the face of the Church may be without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but holy and blameless (Eph 5:27), always remembering the irreplaceable importance of sanctifying grace, given to us by the sacraments worthily received, which are “like divine relics of the Incarnation”309.


    f) Catholic Church

  22. The Church of Christ is the universal community of the disciples of the Lord which extends to all times, to all places, to all races, languages and cultures; that is, it is catholic, it is universal.
  23. The Church is catholic also in doctrine. Some emphasize more the teachings of St. John, others those of St. Paul, etc.; there is a variety of theological schools, there are different and venerable liturgical rites. In this regard it is our desire to be able to have in our Institute an eastern branch in order to help our brothers of the Eastern Churches, since they form part “of the undivided patrimony of the Universal Church”310. From this it follows that we must not fall into particularisms, reductionisms, partialities or one-sided approaches that offend against catholicity. The Catholic Church breathes with two lungs: “one cannot breathe as a Christian, I would say more, as a Catholic, with only one lung; one must have two lungs, that is, the eastern and the western”311.
  1. The universal Church is the “Body of the Churches”312, and is, therefore, a communion of particular Churches. These are “parts… of the one Church of Christ”313; they are constituted “in the image of the universal Church”314; in them “the Church of Christ, which is One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic, is truly found and operates”315; and, therefore, they have with the universal Church a peculiar relationship of “mutual interiority”316. The universal Church, in her essential mystery, is a reality ontologically and temporally prior to every concrete particular Church. The Church-mystery is the mother of the particular Churches, which are her daughters.
  2. We do not belong to the universal Church in a mediate way, through a particular Church. Through faith and Baptism we are immediately incorporated into the One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church, although entry into and life in the universal Church necessarily take place in a particular Church. That is why in the Church no one is a foreigner317; whoever belongs to a particular Church belongs to all the Churches –without blurring canonical dependence318– since belonging to the Communion, as belonging to the Church, is never merely particular, but by its very nature is always universal: “He who is in Rome knows that the Indians are his members”319.


    A Missionary and Ecumenical Church

  3. Because the Church is the “universal Sacrament of salvation”320, she is open to the missionary and ecumenical dynamic, and not folded in upon herself, since she has been sent to proclaim and bear witness to, to make present and extend, the mystery of communion that constitutes her; to gather all people and all things together in Christ321. That is why, in the image of the Church, our small Religious Family must never be folded in upon itself, but must be open like the arms of Christ on the Cross, whose arms were dislocated from so much opening them out of love.
  4. The Holy Spirit, present and active in every time and place, acts “in the heart of man, through the ‘seeds of the Word,’ even in religious initiatives, in the efforts of human activity directed toward truth, toward the good and toward God”322. It is our mission, therefore, to discover them, with joy and respect, in order to seek that men may awaken to a more ardent desire for truth and charity323, since “the Holy Spirit offers to all the possibility of being associated, in a way known only to God, with this Paschal Mystery”324.
  5. This presence of the Spirit “affects not only individuals, but also society, history, peoples, cultures and religions… It is also the Spirit who sows the ‘seeds of the Word’ present in rites and cultures, and prepares them for their maturity in Christ”325, for the Holy Spirit was already at work in the world before Christ was glorified326.
  6. This work of the Holy Spirit has the role of a preparation for the Gospel and “cannot but be referred to Christ, the Word made flesh by the power of the Spirit”327, so that “it must not be separated… from the special action which he carries out in the Body of Christ, which is the Church”328.
  7. That is why the teaching of St. Ambrose will always be for us like a torch illuminating our action: “Everything true, by whomever it is said, comes from the Holy Spirit”329. So the saints affirmed and believed: “all that has been said truly belongs to us Christians”330; “let every good Christian understand that any truth, wherever it may be, belongs to his Lord”331; “no one can say anything true unless moved by the Holy Spirit, who is the Spirit of Truth”332; “there is no knowledge of the truth except in the Holy Spirit, who works in the intellect and preserves it”333; “everything good and everything true is from the Holy Spirit”334.
  8. Thanks to the teaching of Christ, we know that there exists a humanity opposed to faith and to the gift of grace, which the Lord himself calls the “world”335. Faced with the world, into which Christ sends us like sheep in the midst of wolves (Mt 10:16), St. Paul admonishes: Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind (Rom 12:2). However, this difference cannot be translated into fear or contempt336, since if one is conscious of what the Lord wants, one also perceives the duty of evangelization and the urgency of the mission. For this a merely conservative attitude is not enough; rather, what is also necessary is the spreading and proclamation of the deposit of faith, according to the command of Christ himself. And “this interior impulse of charity”337 is called dialogue.
  9. This dialogue, which is a spiritual communication, has its transcendent origin in God himself, whose intention is to establish “a dialogue in which the Word of God expresses himself in the Incarnation”338. Such a dialogue of God with men is born of divine love and is addressed to all men, without doing violence to anyone.
  10. It is necessary to know and “to share common customs”339, without succumbing to the danger of attenuating or diminishing the truth, since “irenicism and syncretism are at bottom forms of skepticism regarding the power and content of the Word of God that we wish to preach”340, certain that “only he who lives the Christian vocation in its fullness can be immunized against the contagion of the errors with which he comes into contact”341.
  11. Of particular importance is preaching, which must be done “always practical in language and skilled in method, assiduous in exercise, backed by the witness of true virtues, eager to progress and to lead hearers to the security of faith”342.
  12. Although this dialogue embraces all men, nevertheless, taking into account the concrete positions of humanity, four spheres or “circles” are distinguished in which this spiritual communication takes place:
  13. – In the first place is the circle of those with whom we share human values, in order to raise them to the supernatural and Christian level343. In this sphere the problem of atheism stands out in a singular way, founded on false theories and consisting not in “a liberation but (in) a drama that tries to stifle the light of the living God”344. In these circumstances dialogue is especially difficult, and one must be “very cautious in judging and… effective in refuting”345, knowing that many situations are occasions for such an attitude.
  14. – In the second place, we find the worshippers of the one God, among whom are the Jews, the Muslims and the faithful of Afro-Asian religions. Conscious that the true religion is the Christian one, and longing that all who seek God may recognize it as such, it is necessary to recognize the many spiritual and moral values that they possess and the ideals that can be promoted and defended in common346.
  15. – In the third place is the circle of those who bear the name of Christ, with whom it is necessary to emphasize, above all, what unites us, and then what divides us. It is necessary to show how those elements of the Church that appear as an obstacle to union are the fruit of the will of Christ for the benefit, unity and freedom of all. In particular, the Primacy of Peter, perpetuated in the Pope, cannot be considered an obstacle, both “because without the Pope the Catholic Church would no longer be such”347, and because without the Roman Pontiff “as many schisms would be formed in the Church as there are priests”348.
  16. – And, finally, dialogue takes place with the Catholic faithful, children of the house of God. This dialogue “does not suppress the exercise of the proper function of authority, on the one hand, and of submission, on the other”349, but presupposes it, for “by obedience directed toward dialogue we understand the exercise of authority, entirely imbued with the awareness of being a service and ministry of truth and charity”350.
  17. In order to become men of authentic dialogue we must learn to practice the following virtues: “clarity above all: dialogue presupposes and demands intelligibility; it is an exchange of thought… Then, affability: dialogue is not proud, it is not hurtful, it is not offensive. Its authority is intrinsic, through the truth it sets forth, the clarity it spreads, the example it proposes; it is not a command or an imposition… Confidence, both in the value of one’s own word and in the disposition of the interlocutor to receive it; it promotes familiarity and friendship… Finally, pedagogical prudence, which takes careful account of the psychological and moral conditions of the hearer351… and strives to know his sensibility and to adapt reasonably and modify the forms of one’s own presentation so as not to be annoying and incomprehensible to him”352. Moreover, “it shows, on the part of the one who initiates it, an intention of correctness, of esteem, of sympathy and of goodness; it excludes a priori condemnation, offensive and habitual polemics, the vanity of useless conversation… it respects his dignity and his freedom, it seeks… his benefit and would wish to dispose him to a fuller communion of sentiments and convictions… it presupposes in us… the state of mind of one who feels within himself the weight of the apostolic mandate”353.
  18. Likewise we must embrace the holy cause of ecumenism; at stake is a promise-prophecy of the Lord: There will be one flock and one shepherd (Jn 10:16), and a prayer of the Lord: That all may be one (Jn 17:21). We must strive by prayer, penance, study, dialogue and collaboration to attain full communion in the unity of the Church “which Christ granted to his Church from the beginning, and which we believe subsists indefectibly in the Catholic Church, and which we hope will grow until the consummation of the ages”354, so that “in a renewed conversion to the Lord it may become possible for all to recognize the permanence of the Primacy of Peter in his successors, the Bishops of Rome, and to see the Petrine ministry carried out, as it is understood by the Lord, as a universal apostolic service, present in all the Churches from within them, and which, its substance as a divine institution being preserved, can be expressed in diverse ways…”355. The ecumenical commitment is “to pray and work for reconciliation and for ecclesial unity according to the mind and heart of our Savior Jesus Christ”356.


    g) Apostolic Church

  19. Another of the awe-inspiring realities of the immeasurable mystery of the Church of the Incarnate Word is her apostolicity, that is, the fact of being founded on the Apostles and perpetuated by apostolic succession.
  20. Communion among the particular Churches in the universal Church is founded –besides faith, Baptism and the Eucharist– on the Episcopate. The unity of the Church is rooted, also, in the unity of the Episcopate: “In order that the Episcopate itself might be one and undivided, he placed blessed Peter over the other Apostles, and established in him the visible principle and perpetual foundation of the unity of faith and communion”357.
  21. “Each Bishop is the visible principle and foundation of unity in his own (particular) Church, formed in the image of the universal Church”358. But for this to be so, the supreme authority of the Church must be present in the particular Church as a proper element: the episcopal College “together with its Head the Roman Pontiff, and never without it”359. The Primacy of the Bishop of Rome and the episcopal College are proper elements of the universal Church, not derived from the particular Churches, but interior to each particular Church. “In the Pope we see Jesus Christ, we follow Jesus Christ, we love Jesus Christ. And in the Bishops? In the Bishops we see, follow, venerate and love the Successors of the Apostles…”360. Each of the members of our Religious Family “wish to be something entirely of the Pope, of the Bishops and of the Church: rags, servants and most obedient children of the Church, of the Bishops and of the Pope, in humility, with fidelity, with love without limits…”361.
  22. That is why we must have a gaze of faith in order always to recognize those whom the Holy Spirit has appointed bishops to shepherd the Church of God (Acts 20:28). We must make our own the counsel of St. Ignatius of Antioch: “Nothing without the Bishop and the priests”362, and much less against them.
  1. St. Luigi Orione also counseled: “The state of the Bishop is the most perfect of all, even more than the religious state; to him belongs the fullness of the priesthood; to him above all others, the spirit of faith, of wisdom and of fortitude… The Bishop knows no enemies of his own; for him there are only children, and the littlest, the humblest, the most unhappy are dearest to him. For all he prays, for all he has words of eternal life, for all he goes up to the altar and offers the Blood of the immaculate Lamb who takes away the sins of the world… The Bishop is the Good Shepherd, who watches, feeds and evangelizes; who knows how to suffer in silence and knows how to give his life for his sheep”363. The history of the Church offers us abundant examples of glorious figures of the Episcopate: bishops who evangelized and founded nations, defended and organized peoples, guarded the deposit of faith entrusted to them364 in the midst of the bloodiest persecutions, and shored up the sound doctrine of the Church against a multitude of errors and confusions. That is why: “… let us love the Bishops!”365.
  2. Not only is communion with the bishop necessary, but also, “… under the authority of the Bishop”366, who is God’s steward (Titus 1:7), communion must be sought with the other priests, a communion that constitutes a single presbyterate founded on the common participation in the priesthood of Christ: “By virtue of their common sacred ordination and common mission, all priests are united among themselves in an intimate fraternity, which must be manifested in spontaneous and willing mutual help, both spiritual and material, both pastoral and personal…”367.
  3. Unity with the other priests, both of the diocesan and of the religious clergy, takes place above all in “intimate sacramental fraternity”368, and from there we also wish to establish close collaboration in pastoral work, since priests, “… although they devote themselves to diverse offices, nevertheless exercise a single priestly ministry on behalf of men”369. Therefore, “between the secular clergy and religious communities a renewed fraternity and a bond of cooperation must be fostered; let them hold in great esteem certain forms of mutual help and certain contacts… which help so much toward mutual trust, apostolic unanimity and fraternal concord…”370.
  4. This unity and cooperation with the other members of the presbyterate, which we wish to establish insofar as we are able, aims at our all preaching Jesus Christ by word and by the example of our life without there being divisions371, conscious of being ministers of a new Covenant, not of the letter, but of the Spirit… (2 Cor 3:6) and, therefore, that in so grave an office we must bear one another’s burdens (Gal 6:2).
  5. Paraphrasing the Apostle372, we always wish, as we have the opportunity, to do good to all, especially to our brothers in the priesthood, received through the imposition of hands373.
  6. The marvelous gift of God that consecrated life signifies, as do vocations to the priesthood, the diaconate, religious life, the missions or consecrated secularity, “affects the whole Church in one of her fundamental marks, which is that of her apostolicity”374.
  7. The foundation of this essentially supernatural work lies in God’s own choice and call: You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit (Jn 15:16); likewise, when the Lord chooses the twelve, it is said: He went up a mountain and calling those whom he wanted, they came to Him (Mk 3:13), since they are God’s property: I pray for them; I do not pray for the world, but for those whom You gave me; for they are yours, and all that is mine is yours and what is yours is mine (Jn 17:9-10).
  8. We would like God to give us the gift of being able to discover and guide so many vocations that we could fill all the good seminaries and novitiates of the whole world, according to the Pope’s recommendation: “God calls whom he wills, by the free initiative of his love. But he wishes to call through our persons… There must be no fear in proposing directly to a young person, or a less young one, the calls of the Lord”375. That is why work with vocations must be active, constant, full of drive and vitality, committed and urged on by the charity of Christ376, and necessarily opposed to a mentality of “ordinary administration or bureaucratic slowness”377, which negligently waits for vocations to knock at the door. God sows the seeds of vocation by grace with full hands378.
  9. The vitality of a community and of the Church herself can be measured by the vocations that God raises up in it, and to this fostering of vocations the right formation of those chosen is inseparably joined. Hence the importance of “making intense efforts to foster vocations and to provide the best possible priestly formation in seminaries. An abundance of vocations and an effective formation of seminarians: here are two proofs of the vitality of the Church”379.
  10. This task of discovering and guiding vocations is not carried out only through pastoral action –an enthusiastic pastoral ministry–. Essential, above all, is prayer: Ask the master of the harvest to send laborers into his harvest (Mt 9:38), for I planted, Apollos watered; but it was God who gave the growth (1 Cor 3:6). And united to it, the example and witness of life: “Where there is faith, prayer, charity, apostolate, Christian life, there the gifts of God are multiplied”380, for “the religious and priests who serenely live their vocation day by day, faithful to the commitments they have made, humble and hidden builders of the Kingdom of God, from whose words, behavior and life the luminous joy of the choice they made radiates… are precisely… those who by their example will spur many on to welcome into their hearts the charism of vocation”381.
  11. Hence every counter-witness, every inconsistency between how values or ideals are expressed and how they are actually lived, every seeking of oneself and not of the Kingdom of God and his justice382, every falsification of the word of God383, “are frequently strong obstacles for those who feel the call of Christ: come and follow me…”384.


    h) In the three white things    385

    The Eucharist

  12. Ecclesial communion has its root and its center in the Holy Eucharist. It is the source and creative power of communion among the members of the Church because it unites each one with Christ himself: “Really sharing in the Body of the Lord in the breaking of the Eucharistic Bread, we are taken up into communion with Him and with one another, because the bread is one, we are one in a single body, for we all partake of that one bread (1 Cor 10:17)”386. In the Eucharist “the whole spiritual common good of the Church is substantially contained”387; “this is Christ”388.
  13. The Church is the Body of Christ. This means that the Eucharist, in which the Lord gives his Body and transforms us into one Body389, is the place where the Church permanently expresses herself in her most essential form: present everywhere and at the same time one, just as Christ is one.
  14. In the Eucharist every member of the faithful finds himself in his Church, since in every valid celebration of the Eucharist the One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church becomes present, and this regardless of whether or not he belongs, from the canonical point of view, to the diocese, parish or particular community where the celebration takes place.
  15. The communion of the particular Churches in the universal Church –besides faith, Baptism and the Episcopate– is founded above all on the Eucharist. For the Eucharistic Sacrifice never belongs to one community alone, since that community receives, with the Lord, the complete gift of salvation, and is thus manifested –beyond its visible particularity– as the image and true presence of the One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church390.
  16. The oneness and indivisibility of the Eucharistic Body of the Lord implies the oneness and indivisibility of his Mystical Body. From every Eucharist there arises the giving of oneself to the Lord by being inserted into his one and undivided Body. For this reason, the Petrine ministry, the foundation of the unity of the Episcopate and of the universal Church, corresponds profoundly to the Eucharistic dimension of the Church.
  17. The unity of the Eucharist and the unity of the Episcopate “with Peter and under Peter”391 are not independent roots of the unity of the Church because, by the institution of Christ himself, Eucharist and Episcopate are essentially linked realities392. The Episcopate is one, just as the Sacrifice of the one Lord is one. The liturgy manifests this reality, since every Eucharist is celebrated in union with one’s own bishop, with the Pope, with the universal Episcopate, with the clergy and with all the members of the People of God393. Every valid celebration of the Eucharist expresses this universal communion with Peter and with the whole Church, or objectively calls for it, as in the Churches separated from Rome.
  18. This being so, we will always find the deepest foundation of our unity as a Religious Family in the Eucharist, which perpetuates the Sacrifice of the Cross. It must be one of our great loves, since it is the unmistakable sign of God’s measureless love for men, of God who wishes to remain among men, of God who gives himself totally to man: “In the Eucharist, the logic of the Incarnation reaches its ultimate consequences”394.
  19. The principal act of the priest is sacrifice, and the sacrifice “of praise,” “pure, immaculate and holy”395, “pleasing… and salvation for the whole world”396, which “opens the way for us” to the Father397, “unique”398, “of perfect reconciliation”399, “ours”400, “living”401. That is why in the Eucharistic Liturgy Christ speaks, above all, with the power of his Sacrifice. It is a very concise and, at the same time, ardent discourse. We must know how to listen to it attentively.
  20. Every month let one Holy Mass be offered in suffrage for the deceased members, another for the intentions of the Superior General, and another asking for all the members of our Institutes the grace of final perseverance.


    The Virgin Mary

  21. The Virgin Mary must be another of our great loves. Because of her union with Christ and with the Church. Because she begot us, the members, together with the Head. Because she was given to us as Mother when she was standing at the foot of the Cross: Behold your son (Jn 19:26).
  22. The Blessed Virgin Mary is the model of ecclesial communion “in the order of faith, charity and perfect union with Christ”402; She “is the image and beginning of the Church… she shines forth before the pilgrim People of God as a sign of sure hope and of consolation”403.
  23. She is in the midst of the Apostles, in the very heart of the nascent Church: they persevered with one accord in prayer with some women, with Mary, the Mother of Jesus, and with his brothers (Acts 1:14), and of the Church of all times.
  24. Indeed, “the Church was gathered in the upper room (of the Cenacle) with Mary, who was the Mother of Jesus, and with his brothers. One cannot, therefore, speak of the Church unless Mary, the Mother of the Lord, is present with his brothers”404. The Marian dimension is essential to the one Church of Christ, just as the Eucharistic dimension and the Petrine dimension are essential to her.
  25. We must be Apostles of Mary, giving ourselves to Her in the maternal slavery of love and doing everything “through Mary, with Mary, in Mary and for Mary”405.
  26. In times of eutrapelia let there always be some remembrance of the Blessed Virgin. Let the same be done when cultural activities take place, since after Jesus Christ no one does as much for the evangelization of culture as our Mother in heaven.


    The Pope

  27. Our third great love must always be the white figure of the Pope: “Where Peter is, there is the Church”406 and “Peter speaks through the mouth of Leo”407. After Simon bore witness to Christ: You are the Christ, the Son of the living God (Mt 16:16), Christ bore witness to Simon: You are Peter and upon this rock I will build my Church (Mt 16:18).
  1. The idea of a Body of the Churches calls for the existence of a Head of the Churches, which is precisely the Church of Rome, which “presides over the universal communion of charity”408. The Successor of Peter “is the perpetual and visible principle and foundation”409 of the unity of the universal Church and of the unity of the Episcopate. Hence the Petrine ministry is not a service that reaches the particular Churches “from outside,” but one “already belonging to the essence of each particular Church from within”410. The ministry of the Primacy entails an episcopal power, that is, as universal bishop and bishop of the diocese of Rome, which is not a mere dignity, so that everything a bishop can do in his parishes the Pope can do in each and every one of the particular Churches of the world; it is supreme power, no one else possesses equal or greater power; it is full power, not only the principal part; it is immediate, he can exercise it over bishops and faithful; it is universal, over all without excluding any; it is ordinary, derived directly from Jesus Christ; and “he can always exercise it freely”411.
  2. The primordial task of the Roman Pontiff for the whole Church is the promotion of unity, which is not at odds with the promotion of the diversification proper to communion.
  3. We make our own the teaching of St. Ignatius of Loyola: “To be right in everything, we must always hold that the white that I see, I will believe to be black, if the Hierarchical Church so determines it”412. Therefore, certain that this is the will of Jesus Christ, “let us remain deaf when someone speaks to us leaving the Pope aside, or not explicitly in favor of the Pope and of the sound and exact doctrine of the Church: such people are not a planting of the Heavenly Father, but evil offshoots of heresies that produce deadly fruit”413. Let us always remember that “the Pope must be loved on the cross; and whoever does not love him on the cross does not truly love him. To be with the Pope in everything means to be with God in everything; to love Jesus Christ and to love the Pope is the same love”414, since “… to love the Pope, to love the Church, is to love Jesus Christ”415.

    Article 6: His second Coming

    a) Prior signs

  4. Just as Jesus Christ came the first time in humility and poverty, he is to come a second time, but in power and majesty, because he will come to call to account and to pass sentence; that is why he will come in greatness and dignity.
  5. The major signs of his second Coming are three:
    a) The Gospel will be preached to the whole world416.
    b) There will be a universal apostasy417.
    c) The Antichrist will make his appearance418.
  6. There will be, therefore, a time when men will turn away from the truth and take pleasure in iniquity419, a time in which the majority will not have authentic faith in Jesus Christ. The certainty that one day this universal apostasy will occur must lead us not to hitch ourselves to the triumphal chariot of any movement or ideology that is established apart from Christ, even if it seems that the whole world follows it. We must always follow Christ. And even if the enemies seem to be the majority we must say: “we are surrounded on all sides; let’s not let them escape.” For “to the soul that contemplates God every creature becomes small”420.


    b) The final and universal resurrection

  7. Toward the end the resurrection of the flesh will take place. All men and women are to rise by the power of God. We believe in the resurrection and we are not afraid of the executioners who lie in wait for the Church in every age: do not be afraid of those who kill the body, for they cannot kill the soul (Mt 10:28).
  8. The time of this resurrection is hidden, and “all who until now have striven to determine that time have been held to be liars”421.
  9. The soul will take up its same body, whole, and will enjoy the endowments of impassibility, subtlety, agility and clarity or beauty.
  10. How great must be our confidence and joy in knowing that death has already been conquered! And it has been conquered thanks to the Incarnation of the Word and to his redemptive Sacrifice. That is why the Incarnation of the Word is the condition and guarantee for the whole universe. “The source of life and of salvation from despair for all men, the condition ‘sine qua non’ and the guarantee for the whole universe are contained in the words ‘The Word became flesh’ and in ‘faith in these words’”422. For “the Incarnation of the Son of God is the foundation, the source and the model both of a new supernatural order of existence for all men, who precisely from this mystery obtain the grace that sanctifies and saves them, and of a Christian anthropology, which is projected also into the natural sphere of thought and life with its exaltation of man as a person, placed at the center of society and –one may say– of the whole world”423.


    c) The Parousia and the final Judgment

  11. Then the Parousia of the Lord will take place: The hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice (Jn 5:28); Then they will see the Son of man coming in a cloud with great power and majesty (Lk 21:27); The sign (the sign of the Cross) of the Son of Man will appear in the sky (Mt 24:30). At that moment the universal Judgment will take place. Christ, together with his elect: you also will sit on twelve thrones to judge the twelve tribes of Israel (Mt 19:28), will separate the sheep from the goats; some he will place at his right, the others at his left, and he will say: Come, blessed of my Father, take possession of the Kingdom… (Mt 25:34) and Depart from Me, you cursed, go into the eternal fire (Mt 25:41). The wicked will go into eternal fire and the just into eternal life (Mt 25:46).
  12. We must become ever more convinced that we do not work for ephemeral or passing things, but “for the most divine work among the divine”424, the eternal salvation of souls and the glorious resurrection of bodies. On the one hand, we must say with St. Luigi Orione: “Place me, Lord, upon the mouth of hell, so that I, by your mercy, may close it”425; and, on the other, know that we work for eternal life.


    d) The renewal

  13. Then the renewal of the whole material universe will take place, since there will be a new heaven and a new earth (Is 65:17; Rev 21:1), so that man, risen in glory, may see the divinity sensibly and in some way in bodies through manifest signs. It will be fully true: “heaven and earth and all that is contained in them, behold, they tell me from every side to love you…”426.
  14. How this renewal will be in quantity and manner “is known only to the one who will be its Author”427. There God will be all in all things (1 Cor 15:28), and matter will attain its highest dignity, expressing the Divine Spirit, for it will be a perfect mirror of God.
  15. All that is left to us is to pray, work and hope, knowing that the form of this world is passing away (1 Cor 7:31).

    CHAPTER 3: EPILOGUE

  16. We wish to end this part of our fundamental code by mentioning, in summary form, what we want our spirituality to be:

    Not Jesus or Mary; not Mary or Jesus.
    Neither Jesus without Mary, nor Mary without Jesus.
    Not only Jesus, but also Mary; nor only Mary, but also Jesus.
    Always Jesus and Mary; always Mary and Jesus.
    To Mary through Jesus: Behold your Mother (Jn 19:27).
    To Jesus through Mary: Do whatever He tells you (Jn 2:5).
    First, Jesus, the God-man;
    but then Mary, the Mother of God.
    He, the Head; She, the Neck; we, the Body.

    Everything through Jesus and through Mary;
    with Jesus and with Mary;
    in Jesus and in Mary;
    for Jesus and for Mary.

    In short, simply: Jesus and Mary; Mary and Jesus.

    And through Christ, to the Father, in the Holy Spirit.

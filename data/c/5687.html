“We must have a very great devotion to the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ: ‘Everything is in the Passion. It is there that the science of the saints is learned’1”2, our Proper Law points out. And it also gives us the noble watchword of “wanting to know nothing except Jesus Christ and Jesus Christ crucified3”4, since it is precisely the mystery of Christ’s self-emptying in His Passion –the supreme priestly act–5 that is the chair from which we learn the sublime science of dying in order to live, of being buried in order to rise, of suffering in order to rejoice, of losing one’s life in order to find it, of humbling oneself in order to be exalted, of sacrificing oneself in order to be fulfilled, of submitting oneself in order to be free. So much so that we can say that “the language of the Cross teaches us that, in strict truth, reality is different from what it appears to be. For the Cross changes the meaning of things. It authentically teaches us to transignify them, since in reality the Cross gives them another end; the Cross transfinalizes reality”6.

But so that this devotion does not remain an idyll or an abstract consideration, our Proper Law also urges us to make this practice “concrete in the knowledge and love of the Gospel accounts of the Passion7, in the theology of the Passion and Redemption, in the contemplation of the holy places of Jerusalem, of crucifixes, of the Way of the Cross, of the beautiful texts of the Imitation of Christ on this subject, of the Eucharist, the perpetuation of the Passion and Cross and second act of the one drama of the Redemption, of the Cross in our life, so well taught in the Circular Letter to the Friends of the Cross of St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, and, finally, in fervor to bring the grace of the Redemption to all of reality”8.

For the Christian, and very especially for religious, the Passion is an inexhaustible source of wisdom and is a guide and model for our whole life. That is why St. Peter Claver used to say: “The only book one needs to read is the Passion”9. And the great St. Thomas writes: “Whoever wishes to lead a perfect life need do nothing other than despise what Christ despised on the Cross, and love what Christ loved on the Cross”10.

For his part, St. John of the Cross also “invites us to contemplate the mystery of the Cross of Christ, as he habitually did, in the poem ‘El Pastorcico’ [The Little Shepherd]”11, a poem that describes “Christ who suffers for the soul”12. It is a precious, very singular text that, in a poetic and mystical way, describes the centrality of the heart of Christ and of His immeasurable love for souls with singular beauty and depth.

In the midst of the Lenten season, and as we prepare to begin in a few days the celebration of the Great Week of Christendom, we believe that a weighed and reflective consideration of the profound and regal doctrine about the Crucified contained in those concise 20 lines from the pen of St. John of the Cross can be of great profit for our souls, “to nail in the heart Him who was nailed to the Cross for us”13, as the great St. Augustine beautifully said.

1. The Crucified in the spiritual striving of the poet

Before devoting ourselves to considering the poem, we would like to point out that Christ Crucified was the model to which the poet14 St. John of the Cross sought to conform himself all his life, he who from his childhood learned to read himself and to read the events of his life in the key of the Cross.

Fray Diego de la Concepción, who was prior at La Peñuela when Fray John of the Cross, on his way to Mexico, stayed there and fell ill with the erysipelas from which he died, went to visit him in Úbeda when the saint was already very gravely ill. He says in a letter that when five wounds opened in the saint’s foot, he and those who accompanied him heard him remark: “Many thanks I give You, my Lord Jesus Christ, that the five wounds Your Majesty had in Your feet, hands and side, You have wished to give me in this one foot. Where did I merit so great a favor?” Regarding this, Fray Diego comments: “Although his pains were so great, he did not complain, but bore it all with great patience”15.

Thus we see how in his daily life and “in the life of faith, the mystery of the Cross of Christ was a habitual reference and rule of life”16. And that is the very thing the Mystical Doctor recommended to all souls, as numerous testimonies corroborate, among them that of Mother Francisca de la Madre de Dios, who always carried with her some little notes with certain counsels the Saint had given her:

“Crucifiedinteriorly and exteriorly with Christ. She will live in this life with fullness and satisfaction of her soul, possessing it in her patience17”18. “Let Christ Crucified be enough for her, and with Him let her suffer and rest, and for Him let her annihilate herself in all things exterior and interior”19. “Let her love trials greatly and think little of them, in order to find favor with the Bridegroom, who did not hesitate to die for her”20. “Let her have fortitude of heart against all things that would move her toward what is not God, and let her be a friend of the Passion of Christ. He who does not seek the Cross of Christ does not seek the glory of Christ”21.

Likewise, all the friars who went about and lived with Fray John of the Cross knew his consistent conduct and attested to it in his Dictámenes. “He used to say that two things serve the soul as wings to rise to union with God, namely, affective compassion for the death of Christ and for one’s neighbors; and that when the soul is lingering in compassion for the Cross and Passion of the Lord, it should remember that on it He was alone in working our redemption, as it is written: Torcular calcavi solus22 (Is 63:3). From this it will draw, and there will be offered to it, most profitable considerations and thoughts”23.

The letters he wrote while suffering the harshest persecution say as much, a period that corresponds –according to some24– to the “moment” of the composition of the Pastorcico. To the Carmelite nuns of Beas he wrote: “Serve God, my beloved daughters in Christ, following His footsteps of mortification in all patience, in all silence and in every desire to suffer, becoming executioners of your own satisfactions, mortifying yourselves if perchance something remains to die that hinders the interior resurrection of the Spirit, who dwells in your souls”25. The Saint, placed upon the Cross by the chapter of Madrid in 1591, writes by way of farewell to Mother Ana de Jesús: “… occupy yourself in exercising the virtues of mortification and patience, desiring to become in suffering somewhat like this great God of ours, humbled and crucified; for this life, if it is not for imitating Him, is not good”26.

Since, then, “John of the Cross is a master and model by his life and his writings”27, we consider that the assiduous reading of his works and attentive consideration of the example of his life is not only of very great profit for assimilating the doctrine of the Cross, the foundation stone of our spirituality28, but also shows us “the necessity of the Cross in our lives”29. For as our Proper Law asks of us: “Jesus is loved and served on the Cross and crucified with Him, and in no other way”30.

2. The poem31

A little shepherd32

Other songs in a divine sense (by the same author) about Christ and the soul.

A little shepherd, alone, is in grief,

a stranger to pleasure and contentment,

his thoughts all set upon his shepherdess,

and his breast by love most sorely wounded.

He does not weep because love has wounded him,

for it does not pain him to see himself so afflicted,

though in his heart he is wounded;

but he weeps to think that he is forgotten.

For only at the thought that he is forgotten

by his fair shepherdess, with great grief

he lets himself be ill-treated in a foreign land,

his breast by love most sorely wounded.

And the little shepherd says: Ah, wretched

is the one who has gone away from my love

and does not wish to enjoy my presence,

and my breast for love of her most sorely wounded!

And after a long while he has climbed up

upon a tree, where he opened his fair arms,

and dead he has remained, hanging by them,

his breast by love most sorely wounded.

El Pastorcico tells in a few, shattering lines what the Night of Christ is. In this poem St. John of the Cross speaks of a profound aspect of the mystery of the kenosis, the same one St. Paul describes and to which our Proper Law refers so abundantly: Have the same sentiments as Christ Jesus. He, though He was of divine condition, did not regard this equality with God as something to be jealously guarded: on the contrary, He emptied Himself, taking the condition of a slave and becoming like men. And appearing in human form, He humbled Himself to the point of accepting, out of obedience, death, even death on a Cross. That is why God exalted Him and gave Him the Name that is above every name33.

“The three allegorical elements of the song, shepherd, shepherdess and tree, represent Christ, the soul, and the tree of the Cross”34. According to scholars of the subject, “the imitation and following of Christ, which [St. John of the Cross] teaches at the beginning of the Ascent, he simplifies in this poem. It is the image and the permanent appeal of the supreme Humility of God, inviting the soul to the encounter with Him, there on the Cross”35.

St. Edith Stein comments in her Science of the Cross that “in the Song of the Pastorcico the sentiments of the soul are not expressed directly. The poet grasped an image and expresses it artistically. ‘He sees Christ Crucified and hears the complaint He addresses to souls and how He weeps to think that He is forgotten.’ With this theme he composes a pastoral poem so much to the taste of his time, as he did in grander style in the Canticle. If for the latter he drew inspiration from the Song of Songs, for the former he thought of the Good Shepherd who gives his life for his sheep (Jn 10). And is not the shepherd’s complaint to his elusive shepherdess an echo of that plaintive call of Christ when He wept over Jerusalem? (Mt 23-37). The refrain, ‘his breast by love most sorely wounded,’ is the key to the state of his soul”36.

1.A little shepherd, alone, is in grief,

a stranger to pleasure and contentment,

his thoughts all set upon his shepherdess,

and his breast by love most sorely wounded.

The first stanza recreates the setting for us and situates us. The poem is in itself, from the beginning, an invitation to tenderness and gratitude: the little shepherd is alone. It is his solitude, that of God and that of the Sufferer. God is by nature the Holy One, without likeness, impenetrable in His judgments. That is why He is the ‘stranger’ and, of necessity, upon becoming incarnate, He must feel Himself “in a foreign land” (v. 10).

It is interesting to note the detail of St. John of the Cross in personifying Christ as the Pastorcico. Fray Luis de León, in his work De los Nombres de Cristo, points out that “the life of the Shepherd is innocent, and peaceful, and delightful, and the condition of his state is inclined to love; and his task is to govern, giving pasture and adapting his governance to the particular conditions of each one; and being wholly for those he governs, he provides them with all they need, always directing his work to this, which is to make a flock and a fold.

The genius and qualities of this Shepherd, in a thousand ways and details, reveal and make manifest the loving heart He has, to whose greatness no tongue or praise can attain; because, besides the fact that all His works are love […] the affection, the tenderness of heart, the solicitude and loving care, and the ardor and intensity of will with which He always does these same works of love, exceed all that can be imagined. There is no mother so solicitous, nor spouse so gentle, nor heart of love so tender and won over, nor any title of friendship so joined to steadfastness, that equals Him or comes near to Him”37.

Christ, though He was of divine condition38, lets Himself be seen and treated in history as one in grief [penado]. This word has a double sense which better explains the solitude of the divine Sufferer. One meaning places Him in a Penitentiary, which is a prison of the highest category because of the crimes punished there. The other sense is the one that expresses the sentence, with its consequences, for which He is there, suffering His penalty and awaiting a final judgment. He feels Himself blamed and condemned to pay, out of obedience to the Father, the debts of all men.

The pleasure and contentment of His divine nature are strange to Him here, and although He Himself is a stranger among men, He nevertheless thinks of them, moved by His love: his thoughts all set upon his shepherdess. How much beauty there is in this line! For what most aggravates His situation is loving and seeking His shepherdess (the soul) where she is not and ought to be; it is the cruelest drama for a lover, for He is not willing to see Himself defeated by lack of love. And that is why St. John of the Cross teaches that “if the soul seeks God, much more does her Beloved seek her”39. It is the absence of the shepherdess that causes his breast by love most sorely wounded.

This line, and his breast by love most sorely wounded, which is repeated with very slight variations as the ending of all the other stanzas except the second, wants to make us notice the sorrowful and painful aspect of the heart of the Pastorcico, dwelling in the breast of love, the Tabernacle of merciful love, which sets out toward Calvary. All the stanzas point to the same point of observation and expectation.

2.He does not weep because love has wounded him,

for it does not pain him to see himself so afflicted,

though in his heart he is wounded;

but he weeps to think that he is forgotten.

We can say that this second stanza solemnly proclaims the profound drama. Here the Pastorcico lets us hear his wounded heart, which, moreover, weeps, is stricken, afflicted, wounded and forgotten. With one brushstroke the Mystical author has summed up for us all the dimensions of Christ’s redemptive suffering. The most apparent, the one that outwardly tends to impress us most and represents all of them, is weeping. And he gives us the reason why He weeps: to think that he is forgotten by his shepherdess. His physical sufferings do not seem to be the principal causes of His sorrow; rather, it is the forgetfulness of His shepherdess that makes Him truly, deeply grieved.

To see that God, in His human nature, weeps for us is impressively moving and convincing. Thus St. John of the Cross reveals the sorrowing Divine Heart of our Lord so that we may experience that, in its sorrow, that Heart tenderly caresses, and is maternal in its way of loving, of bearing, of waiting, of nourishing, of forgiving its forgetful and rebellious children.

This Heart, so wounded, dwelling in the breast of love, cries out to us that only a paternal-maternal heart is capable of thinking, of measuring distances and absences, of feeling and weeping the immense sorrow that the forgetfulness of men causes God…

But instead of dwelling at length on the reason for His grief, He concentrates the whole reservoir of His tears as the supreme expression of sorrow, saying: He does not weep because love has wounded him, … but he weeps to think that he is forgotten.

3.For only at the thought that he is forgotten

by his fair shepherdess, with great grief

he lets himself be ill-treated in a foreign land,

his breast by love most sorely wounded.

This is exactly His “great grief”: only at the thought that he is forgotten, and that is the reason why he lets himself be ill-treated in a foreign land. That great grief is what provokes the sorrowful weeping that Christ could not conceal in the Garden of Olives nor while expiring on the Cross. That is why, only at the thought that he is forgotten by his fair shepherdess, He takes upon Himself all the sufferings, servitudes, poverty, humiliations, outrages, pains, injustices, calumnies, deaths… of all men, women and children, religious, priests, missionaries, monks, brothers, novices… slaves in their prisons of selfishness, of all that ill-treatment means, so long as He may recover so lamentable a loss, and He goes about with his breast by love most sorely wounded.

How can one contemplate His Heart dwelling in the breast by love so sorely wounded and not dissolve in tears and compassion for Christ in His Passion!

At the beginning we quoted a thought of St. Peter Claver, a great devotee of the Passion of our Lord. It is told of him that, when “night came, before going to bed he would sit on the bed, with a chair beside him and the book of the Passion open on it. At times he would kneel to read it, and he would read a verse and begin to weep. On other nights he would get up and, with a Cross on his shoulders, go to the chapel and stop before the Tabernacle to pray and weep. Truly for him the Passion was not something distant, something remote, but something present, living, vital, current, of now, of this moment; as it is, mysteriously and sacramentally, in the Mass.

If what is happening today in religious life is happening, if what is happening in priestly life is happening, it is because what our Lord suffered in the Passion has fallen out of the consideration of the consecrated soul. The Way of the Cross hangs on the walls, but what consecrated soul is attuned to what is represented on the walls?”40.

4.And the little shepherd says: Ah, wretched

is the one who has gone away from my love

and does not wish to enjoy my presence,

and my breast for love of her most sorely wounded!

This fourth stanza is like the Gospel condensed.

And the little shepherd says… It is the Word. His office is to say, to repeat, to warn, to alert, to keep watch, to break open all roads and directions, to become the wayside cross at every crossroads, to forestall the risks and dangers of going astray. For the soul’s absence through sin multiplies exponentially the possibilities of evil. That is why He says: Ah, wretched is the one who has gone away from my love and does not wish to enjoy my presence! The Pastorcico laments that the wayward soul cannot enjoy the happiness that Divine Love has destined for her, and that is why He goes about with his breast for love of her most sorely wounded.

5.And after a long while he has climbed up

upon a tree, where he opened his fair arms,

and dead he has remained, hanging by them,

his breast by love most sorely wounded.

And after a long while… how long has this adorable Pastorcico been waiting for the return of his fair shepherdess? That is why the expression after a long while denotes infinite patience, unhurried waiting, constant and not intermittent love, an expectation without reproach. It also shows that it is God who decides His Hour and ours, the hour of Calvary and the hour of the encounter. For as our Proper Law says: “He alone knows everything, even our soul, our feelings, our character, the secret springs that must be moved to bring us to heaven, the effects that this or that thing will produce in us, and He has all means at His disposal”41.

That is why it seems to us that this while, a rather long one, consists of the spaces of forgetfulness, of absences and of delays that we men interpose to the offer the Pastorcico makes us from the Cross.

The tree, where he opened his fair arms, is none other than the tree of the Cross, where the Divine Lover continues to wait for His fair shepherdess with arms open and fastened to the tree, in order one day to ‘absorb’ her “powerfully and strongly in the abyssal42 embrace of His sweetness”43.

From beginning to end, the Pastorcico invites the soul to rest in its center: the breast of the Beloved, where the Good of all goods dwells; until one day His shepherdess recognizes “that infinite knowledge and that hidden secret, what peace, what love, what silence is in that divine breast…!”44 and falls overcome on her knees to say to Him: “Who will be able to free himself from lowly ways and limits if You do not lift him up to Yourself in purity of love, my God? How will man, begotten and reared in lowliness, rise up to You, if You do not lift him up, Lord, with the hand that made him?”45.

In His infinite Mercy and ineffable love, the Pastorcico seeks, invites and waits for the soul, his fair shepherdess, without reproaching her for her follies, her ingratitude, her rebuffs, her forgetfulness, but appealing to the soul to contemplate his breast by love most sorely wounded, as if telling the soul to have compassion on Him, that by consoling Him she will be infinitely happy and “will see how exalted a knowledge it is that God teaches there –in that divine breast–”46. Hence our Constitutions declare plainly that our office is to weep for Jesus Christ47 and, so as not to cause our God grief, they exhort us that “the memory and remembrance of Him should never depart from our hearts. With this, sleep should come, and of this they should dream while sleeping. The heart always melted in love of Him, and the memory occupied with nothing other than Him”48.

The Pastorcico, in His solitude of love and sorrow, tries to bring about the encounter with His shepherdess. That is why St. John of the Cross makes solitude the condition, the setting and a specific note of His love. This solitude from every creature (in the sense of detachment) is the guardian key of intimacy. It is the inescapable invitation to enter into the mystery. That is why he writes: “By love alone, which at this time burns…; it is what guides and moves the soul then and makes her fly to her God by the road of solitude”49. It is in the “sounding solitude” and in the “divine tranquility” that [God] communicates Himself and unites Himself to the soul50. The reason is that love is the unity of two alone, who wish to communicate with each other alone; and “because the Beloved is found only alone, in solitude”51.

Only the soul that is alone can “come to the delights of the embrace of union with God”52, an embrace that has the property of hiding the soul within itself, where the soul sees itself so secret when it sees itself raised above every temporal creature53; and once that embrace has taken place, says St. John of the Cross, it places the soul “in the veins of the science of love”54, which is none other than the science of the Cross.

3. The school of the Cross

The Cross, in its most eloquent silence, speaks with a special power. St. Paul says that Jesus taught him this science of the Cross: …my power is shown perfect in weakness. Therefore, most gladly will I continue to glory above all in my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may dwell in me. That is why I take pleasure in my weaknesses, in insults, in hardships, in persecutions and in distresses suffered for Christ; for when I am weak, then it is that I am strong (2 Cor 12:9-10).

In the encounter with Christ Crucified, contemplating the figure of that Pastorcico who, having climbed up upon a tree, opened his fair arms, and embracing the breast of love so afflicted and so wounded, we cannot help but imitate Him and effectively desire to become ourselves an image of Christ laden with the Cross and crucified, and, conformed to His Cross, to let ourselves be shaped by Him. That is why the Saint of Fontiveros recommended: “If you wish to be perfect, follow Christ to Calvary and the tomb”55.

This demand becomes even more imperative if we ponder that we who have been called to be “other Christs” will one day be judged according to that same pattern. Fray Luis de León says: “If it belongs to the shepherd to serve in lowliness, to live in a despised garb, not to be adored and served, accustomed to the dress of his sheep and clothed in their lowliness, and if his skin served to win his flock, by this pattern must all those be cut who have received the mission of shepherds. Even so, Christ the Shepherd surpasses them in having come down from heaven to seek man as pasture and, above all, in that Jesus died for the good of his flock, which no other shepherd did, and that, to snatch us from between the teeth of the wolf, he allowed the wolves to make him their prey”56.

For this reason, St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, in his Letter to the Friends of the Cross, which our Proper Law recommends that we read57, tells the “disciples of a crucified God” that the science of the Cross “must be learned in practice, in the school of Jesus crucified”58. And he goes on to say: “Train yourselves, then, in this supereminent science, under the guidance of so excellent a Master. Thus you will come to master all the sciences, since this one contains them all in the highest degree. It constitutes our natural and supernatural philosophy, our divine and mysterious theology. It is our philosopher’s stone which, thanks to patience, will change the most ordinary metals into precious ones; the most atrocious pains into delights; poverty into riches; and the deepest humiliations into glory. Whichever of you knows best how to carry his Cross, even if he is illiterate, is the wisest of all. […] Rejoice, then, you poor unlettered one…! If you know how to suffer with joy, you know more than any doctor who does not know how to suffer as well as you do!59”60.

To train ourselves in this science, besides the contemplation of the mystery of Christ in His Passion, the Eucharist is a concrete moment during the day in which we can learn a little more of the science of the Cross. “There Christ makes Himself a victim, under species not His own, perpetuating the sacrifice of the Cross. There we offer Him as Victim to the Father, and we offer ourselves as victims together with Him. There, in receiving Him in Communion, we participate intimately in His sacrifice, making ourselves victims, eucharistically, with Him. That is why there is nothing like daily Mass for knowing Jesus better and for being set aflame with His love”61.

Finally, “we never know the Cross better than when some sorrow, some suffering, some persecution strikes us. It is the moment to experience, in some way, what Jesus experienced in His Passion and on His Cross”62. And that is why the very Cross that is suffered becomes a cause of joy: I overflow with joy in all tribulations63.

*****

Man’s “return,” so sought after by Christ, involves numerous attempts, always being once again at the beginning. Since it is a matter of love, is there anything more fickle and fragile than the human heart? The Pastorcico was willing to reckon with this. His shepherdess will continue to be fair, even though she leaves Him alone. The true return to Him will begin when the soul recognizes its disordered appetites and affections and “resolves to submit to carrying this Cross, which is truly to resolve to want to find and bear hardship in all things for God”64. For as the Holy Doctor teaches, and of this he wants to persuade us, “this road to God does not consist in a multiplicity of considerations, nor methods, nor manners, nor delights (although this, in its way, is necessary for beginners), but in one thing alone that is necessary, which is to know how truly to deny oneself, exteriorly and interiorly, giving oneself to suffering for Christ and to annihilating oneself in everything; because, by exercising oneself in this, all the rest and more than it is accomplished and found in it. And if there is a lack in this exercise, which is the sum and root of the virtues, all those other ways are a beating about the bush and making no progress, even if one has considerations and communications as lofty as the angels. For progress is not found except in imitating Christ, who is the way and the truth and the life, and no one comes to the Father except through Him”65.

Christian mortification, to which the Church invites us especially in the season of Lent, is figuratively night; subjectively (united to that of Christ), it is death; but objectively and in its outcome it is resurrection.

The whole process is directed toward the ‘Pasch of the embrace’ –not in a sensible sense– but of the mystical embrace that forms part of God’s plan and that is none other than the radiant goal of union with God. The Pastorcico, who has remained with His arms open, fastened to the tree of the Cross, speaks to us of the longing He has for this embrace. It is the image of a divine waiting, of God’s hope-filled offer to man.

Who is there who, after contemplating the Pastorcico, does not turn toward Him, does not feel himself committed? From the Cross hangs the Savior of the world! From the tree of the Cross hangs Christ with his breast by love most sorely wounded!

Wherever we may go, or wherever the reading of this writing may find us, let us embrace the Cross that God has been pleased to send us, and let us count on the fact that the definitive triumph always belongs to Jesus crucified and to those crucified with Him.

May the Holy Virgin, who at the foot of the Cross experienced this reality, obtain for us from her Only Son the grace, today and always, of wanting to know nothing except Jesus Christ crucified66.

A very fruitful Lent and a very happy Easter!

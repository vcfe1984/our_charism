The ‘indispensables’, “absolutely” and “totally”

Cf. Constitutions, 17, 95, 174 and 210; Directory of Spirituality 37

When reading our Proper Law, especially its principal documents, that is, the Constitutions and the Directory of Spirituality, it is striking that the word indispensable is mentioned five times. And on two of those occasions the Proper Law also adds the adverbs “absolutely”1 and “totally”2.

The Royal Spanish Academy defines “indispensable” as the adjective applied to a thing or person that one cannot do without; that which is necessary, which is obligatory3. In this sense, both in the Constitutions and in the Directory of Spirituality we read:

  • “We wish to manifest our love and gratitude to the Blessed Virgin, and at the same time obtain her indispensable help to prolong the Incarnation in all things, by making a fourth vow of Marian slavery according to St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort”4.
  • “With regard to joy, as a fruit of the Holy Spirit and an effect of charity, we must strive by every means that ‘no one be disturbed or saddened in the house of God’5. For this it is totally indispensable to live fraternal charity…”6.
  • “… charity is indispensable to evangelize the culture, both as the end of the agent and as the end of the work. Otherwise, ‘the civilization of love’ will not be attained7”8.
  • “He became incarnate so that we might have life and have it abundantly (Jn 10:10). To have that life in Christ Jesus (1 Cor 15:21-22) it is absolutely indispensable to be united to His Person, to have His Spirit, to assimilate His doctrine, to frequent His sacraments, to imitate His examples, to love His Mother tenderly, to be in perfect communion with His Hierarchical Church through its twofold bond, namely: through one same faith and one same charity, and through the government of one alone over all: Peter”9.
  • “We must always remember that for persecution to be blessed it must, indispensably, meet two requirements: that we be ‘reviled for the sake of Christ,’ and that what is said against us be false’10; and we must take great care not to turn over and over our troubles, dwelling on them with delicate self-indulgence, or falling into ‘that Luciferian belief that we are something great,’ that we are suffering a great deal”11.

Each of these paragraphs, with its own particular nuance, highlights precisely that which is essential, vital, irreplaceable to us—that is, indispensable—if we are to carry out with growing “perfection the service of God and of men”12, which is the same as saying “a true service to the Church”13.

In light of the force of these texts, it becomes telling to delve into that without which we would not be living our vocation to the full, and whose absence would be greatly detrimental to the work of the Institute.

1. Absolutely indispensable

We would like to begin by developing what the Constitutions, in number 210, define as absolutely indispensable, the expression underscoring the essential character of what follows.

The first thing mentioned there is precisely the end of the Christian life: “to be united to His Person,” that is, to be united to Christ. In this lies “the true greatness of the Christian and religious vocation: union with God”14.

Indeed, it is for this that we became religious, to this that we consecrated our lives, and that is why the Directory of Consecrated Life, quoting the Magisterium of the Church, points out: “The religious who consecrates himself to God in a particular way, seeking union with God through the practice of perfect charity, must do so through Christ, with Christ and in Christ. He is called to be united to God in Christ and to imitate more closely and represent perpetually in the Church ‘that form of life which the Son of God took up when He came into this world to do the will of the Father, and which He proposed to the disciples who followed Him’15”16.

What is more, “if this applies to every religious, ‘all the more reason must we live this reality as religious of the Family of the Incarnate Word’17”18. The tireless search for union with God is essential to every religious and must be so for us. Moreover, this Christocentric imprint must be branded into us and into our apostolate of evangelizing the culture. That is why the “reality of being other Christs is central in our spirituality”19.

Our Proper Law expresses it magnificently in a line that should become the banner we hold aloft every day: “If we are religious, it is in order to imitate the Incarnate Word, chaste, poor, obedient and son of Mary”20.

Now, religious life as the following of Christ in order to attain the perfection of charity “is only to be lived within the framework of one’s own institute: ‘Growing conformity to Christ is brought about in accordance with the charism and norms of the institute to which the religious belongs. Each institute has its own spirit, character, purpose and tradition, and it is by conforming to them that the religious grows in his union with Christ’21”22. For this reason the Mystical Doctor St. John of the Cross, in his Counsels to a Religious on How to Reach Perfection, writes: “In no way should you wish to know things, but only how you will better serve God and better keep the things of your institute”23. For this it is necessary to know in depth the spirit of the founder and the sound traditions of the Institute, to be ‘specialists’ in the Proper Law and to draw from it the criteria of discernment for one’s own action and conduct; it is also necessary to know the writings of the founder and to grow fond of what is our own, of the ideals and holy interests of the Institute. Each member of the Institute must make the non-negotiable elements his own, interiorize them and live them as such. For precisely what is so well explained and developed in each of those elements is what God has called us to live; it is for this that God has gathered us from so many countries and from such diverse cultures, and it is to this very thing that we committed ourselves before God and men when we made our religious profession. For do we not say that we consecrate ourselves in poverty, chastity and obedience “according to the evangelical way traced out in the Constitutions of the Institute of the Incarnate Word24”? That is why it is indispensable that “all the members of our Institute not only fulfill with the greatest possible perfection the evangelical counsels and the self-giving to Jesus through Mary, but also ‘order their life according to the Proper Law of the Institute and thus strive to attain the perfection of their state’”25. For, in the end, in all these elements we have just mentioned we have the concrete expression of the path we must follow toward union with the Incarnate Word. It is very important to bear this in mind because there is no lack of religious who nourish themselves on a foreign spirituality, which may be very good—excellent—for religious of that other congregation, but it is not ours and it is not what God wants for us. That must be clear.

Throughout the length and breadth of our Proper Law and the abundant treasure of the Institute’s patrimony, we find a whole range of guidelines that converge precisely on union with God. Among them we would like to highlight a few:

– Prayer: it is in prayer—vocal or mental—that the soul finds a favorable means of uniting itself to God, and it is there that it finds that familiarity with the Incarnate Word26 which is so fundamental for one who calls himself His religious. That is why each document of our Proper Law, with paternal insistence, addresses to us the call to “unceasing prayer and contemplation”27, since it is in prayer that the soul gradually puts on the “purity, taste and will”28 of our Lord.

Blessed Giuseppe Allamano used to say to his religious: “If there is piety, there is union with God, and everything else comes in addition, because the Lord gives us the graces we need when we pray well”29. And along the same lines Blessed Paolo Manna wrote: “without prayer there is no union with God, and without union with God there is no stability in the good”30.

Likewise, and within the sphere of the life of prayer, silence is a privileged means for union with God: “Silence is a need of the soul, to manifest in the deepest way that, in the presence of God, there is nothing more to say”31. It is a means to attain union with God and, therefore, it should lead to the summit of prayer.

– The practice of the religious vows: for these “contribute not a little to purity of heart and spiritual freedom, continually stir up the fervor of charity and […] are able to make the Christian more like the kind of virginal and poor life that Christ the Lord chose for Himself and that His Mother, the Virgin, embraced”32. Therefore, if the specific end of the Institute consists in attaining the perfection of charity, “by the practice of the evangelical counsels ‘charity toward God and toward neighbor is singularly fostered”33. Consequently, “the evangelical counsels have an eschatological meaning and, in particular, consecrated celibacy announces the life of the hereafter and union with Christ the Bridegroom; poverty provides a treasure in heaven; the commitment of obedience opens the way to the possession of the perfect freedom of the children of God in conformity with the will of the heavenly Father”34. All of which is wonderfully expressed in the very formula of the religious vows: “… chaste, for the Kingdom of Heaven, poor, manifesting that God is man’s only true wealth, and obedient, unto death on a cross, in order to follow more intimately the Incarnate Word in His chastity, poverty and obedience”35.

– Apostolate or work itself, whatever it may be, is conducive to union with God: since “all apostolic activity must be animated by union with Christ, toward which religious cannot fail to tend by virtue of their profession”36. “The apostolate being a supernatural reality—fully theological—its fruitfulness depends on union with God and with the Church”37. In turn, “identifying ourselves with Christ must lead us to act as He did, of whom it is said that He has done all things well38”39; “bearing the weight of work in union with Jesus”40.

– The cross in our lives: that is, sufferings. Like a refrain, this must resound in the depths of our soul: “neither Jesus without the Cross, nor the Cross without Jesus”41. Both are indispensable in our life; that is why we must learn to profit from the crosses God sends us, which always come accompanied by the intimate and real companionship of the Incarnate Word. The Directory of Spirituality tells us so splendidly: “All the co-redemptive efficacy of our sufferings depends on their union with the Cross, and in the measure and degree of that union. […] No pain is redemptive unless it is united to the Passion of Christ. If we do not learn to be victims with the Victim, all our sufferings are useless”42. That is why “one must live dying”43. “This is the secret of all supernatural fruitfulness! Everything lies in knowing how to die! That is the great science!”44. And to encourage us to suffer in union with Christ it tells us: “Nothing attracts divine benevolence as much as suffering”45.

So fundamental is the cross in our lives that our Proper Law takes as its own the statement of the Mystical Doctor of Fontiveros, who says: “If… anyone should try to persuade you… of a doctrine of laxity and greater relief, do not believe it or embrace it, even if it be confirmed by miracles, but rather penance and more penance and detachment from all things; and never, if you wish to come to possess Christ, seek Him without the cross”46.

– The help of Most Holy Mary, for no one was united to God as she was, in being espoused to the Holy Spirit and bearing the Word of God in her most pure womb. She is “the proximate end, the mysterious center and the easy means for going to Christ”47. Each member of the Institute must learn to depend “totally and in every way on God through Mary”48 if he wishes to attain union with God, since for us there is no smoother, easier or shorter way to go to God than “through Mary, with Mary, in Mary and for Mary”49. Without this, we could not say that we are essentially Marian50.

All these things, when well lived, practiced conscientiously and with conviction, bring with them certain attitudes, ways of proceeding, and criteria for undertaking or setting aside apostolic endeavors, which must gradually transform us from within into ‘other Christs.’ Our Spiritual Father St. John Paul II expressed it beautifully and with great power of synthesis as follows:

“The truth of consecrated life as union with Christ in divine charity is expressed in certain fundamental attitudes, which must grow throughout one’s whole life. Broadly speaking, they can be summarized thus: the desire to transmit to all the love that comes from God through the heart of Christ, and, therefore, the universality of a love that does not stop before the barriers that human selfishness raises in the name of race, nation, cultural tradition, social or religious condition, etc.; an effort of benevolence and esteem toward all, and especially toward those whom one humanly tends to neglect or despise most; the manifestation of a special solidarity with the poor, the persecuted or those who are victims of injustice; solicitude to help those who suffer most, such as, at present, the many handicapped, the abandoned, the exiled, etc.; the witness of a humble and meek heart, which refuses to condemn, renounces all violence and all revenge, and forgives with joy; the will to foster reconciliation everywhere and to see that the evangelical gift of peace is welcomed; generous dedication to every apostolic initiative that tends to spread the light of Christ and to bring salvation to humanity; assiduous prayer according to the great intentions of the Holy Father and of the Church”51.

It is likewise absolutely indispensable “to have the Spirit of Christ and to assimilate His doctrine.” This is so, purely and simply, because the Spirit of Christ, that is, the Holy Spirit, is the spirit of our Religious Family52. “By the proper charism of the Institute,” say our Constitutions, “without coercion of any kind, scrupulously respecting consciences, promoting healthy pluralism, leading to the full living of the freedom of the children of God53, because where the Holy Spirit is, there is freedom54”55. Consequently, it is vital to employ the means to keep the soul constantly ready for whatever God may dispose56.

Inexorably united to docility to the Holy Spirit is the assiduous reading, “deepened within the Church”57, of Sacred Scripture, which must always be “the soul of our soul, of our spirituality, theology, preaching, catechesis and pastoral work”58, because it is there that the doctrine of Christ, as understood in the Church, is found59. Put in a few words: “Only one who is totally faithful to the doctrine of Christ can be an effective apostle”60.

It is not surprising, then, that one of the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism is precisely docility to the Magisterium of the Church, and that our members be formed in the “strictest fidelity to the supreme Magisterium of the Church of all times”61, solidly instructed in a sound theology—which “proceeds from faith and seeks to lead to faith—”62, built upon “a profound knowledge of the philosophy of being”63.

The Constitutions also point out that it is absolutely indispensable for each member of the Institute “to frequent His sacraments, to imitate His examples.” And regarding this we find abundant provisions in our Proper Law, which not only prescribe and encourage us toward the fruitful reception of the sacraments—making sacramental life an essential part of the discipline of the Institute—but also make it central when it comes to mission. “Authentic evangelization must lead to and culminate in the worthy reception of the sacraments, for through them the grace of the Holy Spirit is ordinarily communicated”64. Indeed, several of our documents develop, from different points of view, the sacramental life both for the members and for the faithful in our missions. It is natural, then, that Eucharistic devotion and the worthy celebration of Holy Mass, as the liturgical act par excellence of the mystery of the Redemption, should be another of the non-negotiable elements.

Regarding the imitation of the examples of Christ, we have already said something above. Let us add here that “if every Christian should pass through this earth in imitation of the Incarnate God, all the more reason must we live this reality as religious of the family ‘of the Incarnate Word.’ And not only must we ourselves live the life of Christ, seeking God in all things, but we must spread the life of Christ in others, and inform with it the cultures of men in order to elevate man”65.

With various expressions our Proper Law insistently stresses how indispensable it is for us “to be ‘other Christs,’ to be ‘like a new incarnation of the Word,’ ‘like another humanity of His,’ so that the Father may see in us ‘nothing but the beloved Son’66”67. In fact, this is the very thing we have committed ourselves to under vow, when we say that we wish “to follow more intimately the Incarnate Word” so that our “life may be a living memorial of the way of existing and acting of Jesus, the Word made flesh68, before the Father and before men”69. All our documents are nothing but an unfolding of guidelines, attitudes, criteria, explanations, etc., on how best to imitate the Incarnate Word. Truly, in our spirituality—if it is to be serious, as we want it to be—the imitation of Christ is irreplaceable.

“To love His Mother tenderly.” We have said it and we wish to emphasize it: love for the Mother of the Savior, devotion to the Blessed Virgin—which must be great, tender and constant70—is indispensable in the life of every member of the Institute. For ours is a consecration in slavery of love to the Mother of God. And as such we must “invoke her, greet her, think of her, speak of her, honor her, glorify her, commend ourselves to her, rejoice and suffer with her, work, pray and rest with her”71. For this reason, from our entrance into the Institute, we can say that our formation is “a school of filial and profound love for the Blessed Virgin Mary”72.

She is, moreover, not only the Model and protection of our consecrated life73 but also our Model as missionaries74, since it is from her that we learn everything, particularly “docility and promptness in carrying out what the Holy Spirit asks”75 and, truth be told, her “help is indispensable to us to prolong the Incarnation in all things”76. For this reason, one cannot belong to the Incarnate Word without at the same time belonging to the Mother of the Incarnate Word.

Finally, the paragraph of the Constitutions we have been developing ends by saying that it is absolutely indispensable “to be in perfect communion with His Hierarchical Church through its twofold bond, namely: through one same faith and one same charity, and through the government of one alone over all: Peter.” This is so because the Institute was born in the Catholic Church, and is of the Catholic Church and for the Catholic Church; therefore, all of us members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word are formed and work for the building up of the one Church of Christ.

In this sense, we read in the Directory of Spirituality that “to love Jesus Christ and to love the Pope is one and the same love”77, since “… to love the Pope, to love the Church, is to love Jesus Christ”78. That is why “our motto is ‘with Peter and under Peter’”79.

For it could not be otherwise, since “in the very spirit of the Institute of the Incarnate Word we find the foundation of our love and fidelity to the Church—of an authentic ecclesial spirituality—for ‘Christ Himself is Incarnate in His Body, the Church’80; and in every man, because ‘Christ mysteriously identifies Himself with every man […] ‘in Him we are christs and Christ’81”82. And, furthermore, as true devotees of the Blessed Virgin Mary, who is the model of ecclesial communion83, we cannot but tend toward the unity of the universal Church. Since perfect communion with His Church is thus indispensable for each one of us and for the work of the Institute, we strive “to achieve, through prayer, penance, study, dialogue and collaboration, full communion in the unity of the Church ‘which Christ bestowed on His Church from the beginning, and which we believe subsists indefectibly in the Catholic Church and hope will grow until the consummation of the ages’84”85. From this follow, as indispensable, our fidelity to the Magisterium of the Church, our unfailing love for the figure of the Holy Father, the embracing of the cause of ecumenism, and the carrying forward, with the zeal of the saints, of the missionary work on behalf of the Church, with the sole objective that Christ may reign.

2. Charity is indispensable

In this second point we would like to treat, albeit very briefly, what the Constitutions say in numbers 17 and 174, which point to charity as indispensable—that is, cardinal and capital—for our evangelizing task, if we are to carry it out fruitfully and joyfully.

Quoting the words of St. Paul VI, our Proper Law says: “… charity is indispensable to evangelize the culture, both as the end of the agent and as the end of the work. Otherwise, ‘the civilization of love’ will not be attained86”87.

For “to love God by manifesting it in concrete love for our brothers is the only possible way of loving God, as Jesus Christ taught us”88; that is why “our Religious Family, as another prolongation of the Incarnation of the Word, ‘in order to incarnate Him in all that is human,’ humbly dedicates itself to achieving this through the works of mercy”89, spiritual and corporal.

In this sense we are convinced, and it has been our experience over the years, that the specific and singular end of the Institute is in absolute harmony with the spiritual works of mercy, and that it is these which most spread the good90.

However, since “love of God and love of neighbor merge with each other: [for] in the humblest we find Jesus Himself and in Jesus we find God”91, and because, furthermore, our Redeemer did so, we also dedicate ourselves to the corporal works of mercy92, especially with the disabled and the poorest. That is, it is our preferential option to serve those who find themselves in a situation of greater weakness and, therefore, of graver need93, since “charity toward the poor […]; care for the sick and those who suffer, contribute significantly to the mission of the Church”94. Hence we consider the members of the Institute who work in this kind of apostolate as “key pieces of the apostolic endeavor of the Institute”95.

Our Proper Law indicates that it is, then, the works of mercy “that, by giving concrete witness of charity, will win many men for the kingdom of Christ, giving glory to God and saving many souls, thus fulfilling our universal end and also the specific end of our religious family”96.

Our Institute having been conceived as missionary—with active and contemplative members—we must say that mission itself is already a work of charity and that the works of charity themselves form an “essential part of the mission of the Church”97. Consequently, “together with the proclamation of the Word and the administration of the sacraments, the service of charity also forms part of the essential spheres of evangelization”98.

“The advantages that the works of mercy have for the concrete realization of the charism of the Institute are manifest, above all in countries where the explicit proclamation of the Gospel is forbidden and the only way to do it is through the silent witness of the religious. In Muslim countries, for example, it may become the only means of evangelization. But it also allows, in places where one can preach with the word, to corroborate with deeds what is proclaimed”99.

“For this reason, among the variety of apostolates of our Institutes, a preferential place must be reserved for charitable work, which is an essential component of the evangelizing mission of the Church and an indispensable element for the evangelization of culture”100. For, as Pope Benedict said, this “remains the royal road for evangelization”101.

It is understood, then, that the works of mercy or charitable activities, whatever they may be, are indispensable to our missionary task and a non-negotiable element attached to the charism of the Institute.

But we must say that paragraph 174 of the Constitutions which we have quoted, immediately after emphasizing that “we must give priority to the care of the poor, the sick and the needy of every kind” because the charity of Christ urges us102, exhorts us to practice “charity concretely, as a witness, in the first place, to all the members of our Institutes that ‘charity, charity alone, will save the world; blessed are those who have the grace of being victims of charity!’103”.

“In this work of apostolate, in which ‘one is a missionary above all because of what one is… before being one because of what one says or does’104, the first place is held by the witness of life, ‘the first and irreplaceable form of mission’105, so that the charity of Christ may shine forth among the faithful106”107. If this witness of charity is lacking among the members of one’s own Family, not only is the message preached not credible, but many are prevented from embracing it, to the great detriment of the missionary work that has been entrusted to us.

This “practice of charity is manifested in the attitude of service to others: in imitation of the Incarnate Word who, by His gesture of washing the feet of His disciples, revealed to us the depth of God’s love for man: in Him, God Himself places Himself at the service of men! Thus giving us an example that our religious life is fully realized in self-offering love, of concrete and generous service”108.

“If in mission lands the religious communities are truly such, and in them there is an effort to live fraternal charity intensely with each and every one of their members109, then much fruit may be expected. If, on the contrary, a missionary does not know how to live with his peers, is ignorant of the fundamental principles of dialogue, does not practice fraternal charity de facto, and makes everything revolve around himself, in that community a good share of energy will have to be spent merely in bearing with the weak brother”110.

There are many who call themselves very faithful to the Institute because they have been in it for many years, because they quote some phrases of the Proper Law with a certain ease, because they hold or have held some important office, because they occasionally take part in some celebration—without realizing that “it is not enough to take part in some meeting or festivity to be fully a religious”111–, etc., and yet, at the same time, they allow themselves serious faults against charity. Among them, “the one that is sometimes committed with the least remorse is murmuring”112. Our Proper Law calls these religious “‘two-faced men,’ because they sow confusion among many who live in peace113”114, and they do not realize that they are attacking concord, the union of the brothers of one same Family and the peace of the community, spreading sadness—so often the fruit of an evil spirit and of unjust comments—thus squandering the basic foundation of their consecration in this beloved Institute, which is precisely love and service of Christ, “both of the physical Body of Christ in the Eucharist and of the Mystical Body of Christ, which is the Church, formed by ourselves… and by all men”115, among whom are the members of the Institute. We must be ready to die for one another, to defend the Institute with all its non-negotiable elements, to guard firmly the very rich patrimony that has been bequeathed to us: that is why whoever adopts an attitude, so often a comfortable one, and does not fight for the interests of the Institute or to keep the banner of our ideals held high… such a one has, in some way, already deserted. Have we not read that each one should have “the soul ready to accept death, if necessary, for the good of the Institute in the service of Jesus Christ”116?

Let us understand this well: if we do not practice charity concretely with the members of the Institute, we are straying from the path of perfection to which God has called us. “The missionary is the man of charity”117, and without charity we are not missionaries, and we do not even reach the level of a ‘good Christian.’ The apostle St. Paul told us so: Though I were to speak all the languages of men and of angels, if I do not have love, I am like a resounding bell or a clanging cymbal. Though I had the gift of prophecy and knew all mysteries and all knowledge, though I had all faith, a faith able to move mountains, if I do not have love, I am nothing. Though I were to distribute all my goods to feed the poor and hand over my body to the flames, if I do not have love, it profits me nothing118. One may know many languages, have much learning, have published books and given countless lectures; one may know the whole world, have friends of some influence, have considerable personal gifts and abundant means for the apostolate, but once again… if I do not have charity, all of that profits me nothing.

“This is the reason why we must nourish fraternal charity119 in our religious communities. It is true that there will always be difficulties, because we are creatures, fallible, and therefore we can fail. But these difficulties are borne, they are resolved. That is why the lack of charity and, specifically, murmuring in fraternal life, comes from a lack of understanding, or from not wanting to understand, the commandment of love in an exquisite way. Would that it could be said of the members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word what was said of the first Christians: See how they love one another!”120. And if anyone thinks these are sugary words for beginners, let him read what the Constitutions themselves say on the matter: “And let no one think that this is a utopia, for we have often already heard similar expressions. We must have the firm resolve always to safeguard charity, even though there may be false brethren121, who slip in to spy on the freedom we have in Christ Jesus122, who seem to be with us, but who are not of us123. Charity will never die!124”125.

All our members should always keep in mind the counsels of St. Benedict: “Let them be first to honor one another. Let them bear with the greatest patience one another’s weaknesses, whether of body or of character; let them vie with one another in obedience; let no one seek what he judges useful for himself, but rather what is useful for others; let them practice fraternal charity chastely; let them fear God with love; let them love their Abbot with sincere and humble affection, and let them prefer absolutely nothing to Christ, and may He bring us to Eternal Life”126.

Without the practice of charity there is no joy. For “only he truly rejoices who rejoices in love”127. Therefore, the greater our fidelity to these indispensables, the more vehemently we will be tending toward perfection, the broader and more creative our charity will be, the more disinterested our sacrifice, the better we will fulfill our end as religious of the Incarnate Word, the greater our joy will be, and we will be in the fullness of our grace.

* * *

Perhaps we will be persecuted for all this, in which case we must give thanks to God who has found us worthy, and reread the indispensable requirements for persecution to be blessed128, taking great care not to turn over and over our troubles, dwelling on them with delicate self-indulgence, or falling into ‘that Luciferian belief that we are something great,’ that we are suffering a great deal129.

May the Most Holy Virgin, Mother of the Incarnate Word and our Mother, who knows our interior struggles and our needs, when the storm rages, repeat to us with a maternal accent the do whatever He tells you130 that she said to the servants at that wedding, and may the water of our frailty and weakness be changed into the wine of her strength.

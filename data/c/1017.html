“Priestly lordship”
“Let them live to the full Christian and priestly kingship and lordship” Constitutions, 214

“We want to form priestly souls and priests who are not ‘tributaries’1”2, our Proper Law declares with all firmness and clarity.

What does it mean to be a tributary? Our documents also explain it to us: “A tributary […] is one who acknowledges the lordship that another has over him and pays something as a manifestation of it. Tributary means: ‘to offer or show veneration as a proof of gratitude or veneration (…) it is one who subordinates himself –unduly– to temporal powers, to cultural fashions, to the spirit of the world, as though they were the ultimate end instead of God”3. Understood in this way, when the priest is a tributary he becomes a vassal who hands something over to his master in recognition of his lordship. And as the Mystical Doctor of Fontiveros, St. John of the Cross, magnificently explained: “thus, he who loves a creature remains as low as that creature, and, in some way, lower; for love not only makes equal, but even subjects the lover to what he loves”4 and all “temporal lordship and temporal freedom before God is neither kingdom nor freedom”5.

1. Tributaries: incapable of coherence

Let us give more concrete examples, since historically various forms of tributarism have occurred on the part of some members of the Church. To illustrate: “Within the Protestant movement this became very evident, because they had to pay for the concessions they made to the temporal power in order to triumph in their rebellion against the Church. Thus they became subject to the prince, who took the opportunity to seize the riches of the Catholic Church and the consciences of his subjects”6.

But more recently still, “the progressives who attempt to reconcile their supposed Catholic faith with modern ideologies are tributaries. Supposed Catholic faith, because one who adheres to principles and norms that are not in keeping with the faith and tradition of the Church is not Catholic; nor is one who tries to subject the Church to the civil power”7. That is why those are also tributaries who, in their eagerness to attract vocations, propose an ‘attenuated’ style of life, to the point of accommodating themselves to a worldly model of life that sets aside the radicalness of the beauty and worth of following Christ Crucified. Or those who take away the strength and solidity of the doctrinal formation they offer, diluting it to adapt it to passing doctrines, or rather, embracing a doctrine that “escapes all classification and has only one distinctive and characteristic note, which is the note of perfect and absolute incoherence”8 and which seems rather to worship dilogy9, ambiguity, multiplicity of meanings, equivocation and amphibology10.

Such people are scandalized, considering that ‘it is not normal’ for an Institute to flourish in vocations precisely because it offers a “witness to the Transcendent,” because of its austerity and manner of life, because of the spiritual atmosphere and serene joy lived in its communities, because of its fervor in apostolic works, because of the solidity of its formation rooted in the most absolute fidelity to the Tradition of the Church and to the teachings of the Pontifical Magisterium—when vocations are precisely a concrete proof of the goodness of God, and the works of religious who live in communion with the legitimate Pastors and are faithful to their vocation in the Church are a manifestation of the kingdom of peace that the Lord has promised us and won for us by his cross and resurrection.

Those also behave as tributaries who make use of “pretexts” to “oppose evangelization,” as St. John Paul II taught with clear-sightedness: “Nor are there lacking internal difficulties within the People of God, and these are certainly the most painful. My predecessor Paul VI pointed out, in the first place, «the lack of fervor, all the more serious because it comes from within. This lack of fervor is manifested in fatigue and disenchantment, in accommodation to the surroundings and in lack of interest, and above all in lack of joy and hope»11. Great obstacles to the Church's missionary activity are also the past and present divisions among Christians12, the de-Christianization of Christian countries, the decrease in vocations to the apostolate, and the counter-witness of faithful who do not follow the example of Christ in their lives. But one of the most serious reasons for the lack of interest in the missionary commitment is the indifferentist mentality, unfortunately widespread even among Christians, often rooted in incorrect theological conceptions and marked by a religious relativism that ends up thinking that «one religion is as good as another». We can add—as the same Pontiff said—that there are also «pretexts which seem to oppose evangelization. The most insidious of these are certainly those for whose justification people wish to employ certain teachings of the Council»”13.

In the same way, those are tributaries who try to distort prescriptions of Canon Law, or of the Proper Law, seeking to safeguard their own comfort, and thereby making true once again that “internal difficulties within the People of God are certainly the most painful”14. Do they perhaps not realize that “those who await Christ are still an immense number”15 and that “there is no mission without missionaries”16 and that there will be no missionaries if there is no one to send them?

No less tributaries are those who criticize what is in fact being done for evangelization: they criticize now the lack of means, now the shortage of missionaries, now the ‘quality’ of the work; they criticize everything, forgetting that God is the Author of the mission. Without making the proper distinctions they are criticizing God and opposing, in vain, the plans of God, but God laughs at them17. One last example: those are tributaries who consider it rash to undertake works trusting in Divine Providence. And out of fear of lacking what is necessary, or out of hunger for material securities, they hand over their works to civil organizations or to the State, corrupting Catholic institutions and abandoning the reality of trust in Divine Providence18. Such people forget that “bodily danger does not threaten those who, with the intention of following Christ, abandon all their things, entrusting themselves to Divine Providence”19.

We, not only as priests and members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word, but also as simple Christians, have “not only the right and the duty, but also the satisfaction and the honor of confessing the sublime lordship of God over things and over men”20. That is why we are told explicitly: “The priest must not be a tributary by reason of his investiture and his ministry. He must transmit the truth of God, even at the cost of his blood. He must transmit the holiness of God, accepting to be a sign of contradiction. He must transmit the will of God even to giving his life for the sheep”21.

Let it be well understood that being a tributary implies in some way losing one's Christian identity, which makes one let oneself be carried along by worldliness and give in to the discourse of whoever proposes renouncing the existential recognition of God's lordship over all things, over society and its intermediate bodies, to the point of becoming incapable of coherence. The Holy Father Francis warned us of this with marked emphasis when he said: “worldliness destroys our Christian identity, leads us to a double life—the one that is appearance and the one that is real—and takes you away from God”22.

That is why our Proper Law continues to teach: “The priest (and every Christian) is a man of two kingdoms: he is a citizen of the Kingdom of God and a citizen of the kingdom of the earth. When the priest becomes a tributary, he becomes a traitor twice over: he betrays the Kingdom of Heaven and he betrays the kingdom of the earth, because he does not give the latter what it demands of him, which is the truth and the freedom that come only from the Kingdom of God”23. Indeed, the Christian and priestly lordship asked of the members of the Institute demands—out of loyalty to the world and out of loyalty to God—that we be “independent in the face of the maxims, mockery and persecutions of the world, depending only on our upright conscience illuminated by faith; ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God, which constitutes the full and total rejection of the evil world”24. Hence what belongs to us is subjection and “generous self-giving to the service of Jesus Christ, the only King who deserves to be served”25, in order thus to attain “an effective kingship, though spiritual, over men, even over those who have power and authority, and even over those who abuse it”26. To act otherwise is to be tributaries, and to be tributaries is a counter-witness, an incoherence.

Why do we say this? Because “every counter-witness, every incoherence between how values or ideals are expressed and how they are actually lived, every seeking of oneself and not of the Kingdom of God and his justice27, every falsification of the word of God28”29 involves some pact, some compromise, some capitulation, negotiation, concession or deal with the spirit of the world.

In this sense “hypocrites live on ‘appearances.’ And like ‘soap bubbles’ they hide the truth from God, from others and from themselves, displaying a ‘holy-card face’ to ‘put make-up on holiness’”; “they make themselves look righteous, good,” and they often do this to win the applause of the world, to promote themselves and—if not that—out of some financial interest… ‘they are hypocrites’”30. Hence we say that tributarism, besides being an incoherence, is a counter-witness.

Throughout the short existence of our Institute many, “under color of zeal”31 and “under the cloak of virtue”32, have objected to, criticized, obstructed, ridiculed, accused without reason, prohibited and even, it would seem, set out to ‘dynamite’33 the small work of our Institute. These hypocritical persons—inside and outside the congregation—are those who “always accuse others but have not learned the wisdom of accusing themselves”34. For hypocrisy has the attraction of not saying things clearly; the fascination of the lie, of appearances, is a spirit of revolt and confusion which, “in plain language, means the spirit of understanding things backwards”35.

This hypocrisy sows division, and the Gospel is full of examples. One of them is that of the Pharisee and the publican: The Pharisee, standing upright, prayed in his heart in this way: ‘O God, I thank you that I am not like other men, who are thieves, adulterers, nor like that publican there’36. Immediately the Pharisee declares his distance: that publican there, he says mockingly. For the hypocrite tries to finish off—or at least to denigrate as much as he can—the one whose virtue, holiness or good works make him uncomfortable; the radicalness of the other shakes him, and in his attempt he only makes his own meager virtue and twisted interests stand out all the more.

Another beautiful passage of Sacred Scripture teaches us the hatred of the wicked against the just man: “Let us lay traps for the just man, because he annoys us and opposes our way of acting; he reproaches us for our transgressions of the Law and rebukes us for our faults against the teaching we received. […]He is a living reproach against our way of thinking and his mere presence is unbearable to us, because he leads a life unlike that of others… For if the just man is the son of God, He will protect him and deliver him from the hands of his enemies”37. A passage that is applied to Christ: Let us put him to the test with insults and torments, to know his temper and try his patience. Let us condemn him to a shameful death, since he claims that God will visit him”38. Pope Francis comments: “This prophecy is very detailed; the plan of action of these wicked people is one detail after another, let us spare nothing, let us test him with violence and torment, and let us put his spirit of endurance to the test… let us lay snares for him, let us set traps for him, [to see] whether he falls… This is not a simple aversion; there is no wicked plan of action—certainly—of one party against another: this is something else. This is called fury: the devil is always behind all fury, he tries to destroy and spares no means. […] What does the devil do? He rages. Always. Behind all fury is the devil, to destroy the work of God”39.

Deceiving themselves in their hypocrisy, and in their purpose of finishing off that person, that Institution that challenges their way of living, the tributaries carry on the “little daily lynching, [the one] that tries to condemn people, to create a bad reputation…, to discard them, to condemn them: the little daily lynching of gossip that creates an opinion; […]and with that ‘it is said that’ an opinion is created in order to finish off a person. […] And in our Christian institutions we have seen so many daily lynchings that were born of gossip.” In fact, “gossip is also a form of fury”40.

We know well that “even before having begun our Congregation—on March 25, 1984—we already had people opposing us, and not just in any way, but with rage.” How many threatening voices were raised throughout all these years with pitiful calumnies: that we were more Lefebvrist than Lefebvre—they told us—, that we were disobedient to the Pope, closed to dialogue, without sensus ecclesiae… and how many unfounded accusations: that we do things without permission, that there is no selection and that is why we have many vocations (or if not, they accuse us of ‘brainwashing’ them), that we have many foundations because in some cases religious are sent alone… With what heavy-handedness they have wanted to ‘change’ us, they have wanted to ‘convince’ us of the contrary: change this, write this, sign here and your problems are over… with what cunning ‘factions’ have been formed to orchestrate perverse machinations and to obstruct noble projects, pastoral initiatives, decisions of government, and to carry off some good people in the revolt…

It should not astonish us that, almost exclusively, these attacks come from consecrated persons. “This is a sickness of the Church,” says the Holy Father, “a sickness that arises from a worldly way of thinking and feeling that makes itself the interpreter of the law”41.

That is why we dare to say that behind all these accusations there is a nihilistic indignation, like that of one who seeks annihilation. The confrontation is, in its ultimate foundation, theological. One who gives radical, explicit—positive and negative—witness to Jesus Christ, our Lord, cannot fail to be attacked. That is, one who gives witness to the light that came into the world; and the darkness continues to hate the light for the same reasons: and men loved darkness rather than light because their works were evil42. Jesus prophesied it: they have hated me, they will hate you too43; St. Paul says it: all who wish to live piously in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution44. Almost every page of the Gospel teaches it, in which the Lord himself calls blessed those who suffer for his sake45.

We would like here to recall once again that for “persecution to be blessed it must, without fail, meet two requirements: that we be ‘reviled for Christ's sake,’ and that ‘what is said against us be false’46; and we must take great care not to turn our troubles over and over, entertaining ourselves with delicate complacency in them, or falling into ‘that Luciferian belief that we are something great’47, that we are suffering a great deal”48. That is why, and within this framework, we would like to warn that one must be very careful not to fall into the error of settling in one's heart that whoever contradicts us is persecuting our virtue, or has little experience of spiritual things, or is envious, or has similar faults, in order thereby to excuse ourselves from receiving correction from anyone. There have been many who, tempted by vainglory or moved by who knows what passion, pretended that they were persecuted for their virtue and did not understand that it was not so, but rather for their ‘worldliness’; and “believing themselves to be something great”—since according to them they were persecuted for their virtue—they secretly went on nourishing in their hearts an idol of their own self-esteem, and although sporadically they seemed to humble themselves in their thoughts and words, in reality these were not humiliations made before the Majesty of God with holy fear of offending him, but before the secret and disguised idol they had made of themselves. May it not happen to us as it did to them, who dressed self-love in virtuous garments and then wanted to be adored. And if anyone did not adore their statue, they then judged him to be a persecutor of their virtue or contrary to their law. For they made their own tastes, their criteria, their conduct ‘the rule of virtue.’ So much so that everything that departed from that, they condemned and despised and even strove relentlessly to destroy. St. Peter Julian Eymard came to affirm concerning them: “Bear in mind that all who abandoned their vocation did not esteem the rule, to which they wished to add or from which they wished to take away. Jesus Christ rejects them, because he does not want two laws, nor wills contrary to his own, indicated to the founder”49.

We must open our eyes and “not let ourselves be weakened by the spirit of the world, and live coherently, without giving in and without compromises.” Pressures, being taken aside by someone who tries to convince you by offering you a comfortable solution, uncertain situations, the risk of being caught up in gossip, the risk of losing everything and of our works coming to nothing, the risk of exposing oneself to unpleasant consequences, demotions, persecutions—all of this is always on the horizon. That is why we must feel as our own, and very intimately, the exhortation of St. John Paul II who told us: “Show by the depth of your convictions and by the coherence of your conduct that Jesus Christ is our contemporary”50.

St. Alphonsus Maria de Liguori already said that “whoever wishes to be glorified with the saints of Heaven needs, like them, to suffer on earth, for none of them was loved and well treated by the world, but all were persecuted and despised, so that the words of the Apostle himself were verified: All who wish to live piously in Christ Jesus will be persecuted”51.

2. Firm in our convictions

If it is said of tributaries that they are ‘incapable of coherence,’ what is asked of us, so as not to be such, is precisely coherence, avoiding all false duality, as one dimension of fidelity. “To live in accordance with what one believes. To adjust one's own life to the object of one's adherence. To accept misunderstandings and persecutions rather than permit ruptures between what one lives and what one believes: that is coherence. And it is, perhaps, the innermost core of fidelity”52. For this implies being aware of one's own identity and manifesting it, with complete respect, but without hesitation or fear.

“But all fidelity must pass the most demanding test: that of duration. Therefore, another dimension of the fidelity asked of us is constancy. It is easy to be coherent for a day or a few days. What is difficult and important is to be coherent all one's life. It is easy to be coherent in the hour of exaltation, when everything is ‘going well,’ but it is difficult to be so in the hour of tribulation. However, only a coherence that lasts throughout one's whole life can be called fidelity. Our Lady's fiat at the Annunciation found its fullness in the silent fiat she repeated at the foot of the cross. To be faithful is not to betray in the darkness what one accepted in public”53.

What is asked of us is a coherence that is not ephemeral, but constant and persevering. To belong to the Church, to belong to the Institute, to serve the Church, to be faithful to the charism received is today something very demanding. Perhaps it may not cost the shedding of blood, but it may cost contempt, indifference, insults, marginalization and even prison. The danger of fear, of weariness, of insecurity is then easy and frequent. But we must not let ourselves be overcome by these temptations. We must not let the vigor and spiritual energy of our being “of the Incarnate Word” and servants of his Church fade away.

“The tributary priest is an aberrant being who has ceased to be salt and has ceased to be light, and like salt that has lost its savor: It is no longer good for anything but to be thrown out and trampled underfoot by men54”55. Far be it from us to be such.

For each of the members of the Institute has been called to be an imitator of the Incarnate Word, in whom “everything is transparency, authenticity, sincerity, coherence and truth”56. We must be firmly planted in that conviction. We must show nobility of soul, fidelity to our own conscience, acceptance of the merciful designs of God—individually speaking and as an Institute—which of course also includes its risks, but convinced that these are things worth dying for. To behave otherwise, that is, to depart at any point from that transparency, authenticity, sincerity, coherence and truth, is a departure from the Ideal and—to a greater or lesser degree—will also be a betrayal of one's own conscience, of Truth itself, of the vocation received and of the principles that were instilled in us.

Our convictions tell us that “in our spirituality we must never dialectically separate teaching from action, nor action from teaching. We must always unite integrity of doctrine with uprightness of life, orthodoxy and orthopraxis”57. It is also our “most firm conviction that only in the strictest fidelity to the doctrine of Jesus as understood in the Church is the strongest, most living, fresh and graceful originality to be found”58. These are all expressions of the Proper Law. We are convinced, moreover, that “truth contemplated makes one teach with conviction and—according to its degree of theological certainty—with an authority that is nothing other than a participation in the authority of Christ”59, and that, therefore, “to teach one's own doctrines, to demand undue assent, to emphasize the accidental, to have a doubtful attitude about everything, to remain in insinuations or approximations, to problematize the simplest things, to obscure what is clear and not clarify what is obscure, to seek oneself […] to be padded with filler instead of sober, opaque instead of transparent, confused instead of precise, […] to stir up the waters so that others will think they are deep… is not to have the spirit of Christ who said: Let your word be yes, yes; no, no; anything beyond this comes from the evil one60”61.

We are convinced of the immense value that the apostolate has for the life of the Church62 and “that the best way to carry out an effective apostolate is in the closest union with the Incarnate Word and in love for souls to the point of the heroism of unreserved self-giving”63. And that is why we go to the mission with the impetus of the saints64, trusting only in the Providence of God65.

We are convinced that in the “spirit bequeathed by the Founder and in the fulfillment of the Constitutions, that is, in the patrimony of the Institute, are contained the riches that the Holy Spirit has granted it for the good of the Church”66. Therefore, to the extent that we offer the world a radical witness of Christ Crucified—in contradiction with the spirit of worldliness—we will be able to say that our collaboration in the cause of evangelization is effective, because only thus will we be fit for the Master67, since we will be living “to the full Christian and priestly kingship and lordship”68.

That is why a moment of crisis is a moment of choice; it is a moment that places us before the decisions we must make. In those moments the Incarnate Word could also ask us, as he asked his apostles: Do you also wish to go away?69. “In my homeland there is a saying: ‘Don't change horses in the middle of the river,’” the Holy Father commented; “in times of crisis, one must be very firm in the conviction of faith. Those who left ‘changed horses’; they looked for another teacher who was not so ‘hard,’ as they said to Him. In times of crisis we have perseverance, silence; staying where we are, standing firm. This is not the moment to make changes. It is the moment of fidelity, of fidelity to God, of fidelity to the things [decisions] we have made before. […] May the Lord give us the strength, in moments of crisis, not to sell out the faith”70.

From being a tributary follows the fruit of barrenness: “they are often strong obstacles for those who feel the call of Christ: come, follow me…”71; moreover, it seeks to break apart, it sows division. From not being one follows precisely the fruit of fruitfulness, since these religious “by their example spur many to welcome into their hearts the charism of vocation”72; in addition to the fruit of unity: “A profound understanding of the charism leads to a clear vision of one's own identity, around which it is easier to create unity and communion”73. The choice is ours.

If we are truly to be coherent so as not to be tributaries, then we must, “even at the cost of renunciations and sacrifices, always seek the truth and never sell it or conceal it out of a desire to please men, to astonish, or out of originality or a desire to make a show”74. For “there can be no unity at the expense of truth”75. Indeed, an attack on unity is “both obsequiousness and servility, which sacrifice truth and one's own conscience in an attempt to maintain a false peace, not to cross a friend, to avoid some problem or, at times, to gain an advantage through silence or applause”76.

That is why the cohesion of all the members, always resting on the truth, becomes imperative. All “the good and the evil of the Institute concern everyone equally”77, Blessed Giuseppe Allamano told his own, and the same applies to us. That is why the Proper Law tells us: “that in our communities, which must be a unity among persons, a body full of members who think, no one should behave like a closed and solitary whole, like a self-sufficient nomad, but rather we must learn to act as the parts or members of a whole”78.

We will have many difficulties, we will have defects and weaknesses to spare, but if we remain united, each giving a coherent witness from his own place, contributing not criticisms, not fictitious plans, but practical solutions, initiatives in keeping with our spiritual patrimony, giving ourselves fully to the mission in total forgetfulness of self, God will preserve his work, the work of the Institute.

“Do not let yourselves be deceived by gusts of wind that pass and sweep everything away”79, said our Spiritual Father. Let us defend with complete freedom of spirit the great good of unity that tributarism and hypocrisy seek to break apart; let us work in unity of purpose, making of our communities, of each province and of all the provinces among themselves one heart and one soul80.

No matter how many deadly blows they launch against us, how many lynchings by gossip they organize around us, with how many incoherences they strive to accuse us, with how much fury they try to see us disappear, let us persevere in a “perfect spirit of union and personal self-giving”81, solid around the charism. That is, living it without cuts, guarding it jealously, deepening it and developing it over time, in homogeneous continuity, whatever the historical circumstances may be82. And all this in the most absolute fidelity to the Church our Mother.

It would be a grave error to want to level our charism or make it uniform according to pastoral needs polarized around a one-sided objective83, or worse still, to want to modify it to please some ‘worldly criterion.’ What is properly ours is that, with the dignity and nobility of soul that a coherent life gives, we bear unequivocal witness that the Word was made flesh, even though many may come wanting to ‘buy us off’ by telling us: “be a little more normal, like other people, sensible…”

We must never tire of insisting on the fact that “it redounds to the good of the Church herself that institutes have their own particular character and function”84, and for that reason we must always strive diligently to preserve the place that Divine Providence has assigned us in that same Church, of which we are honored to be sons, responding as far as we are able to the new needs that arise in the Church, without ever departing from our patrimony and sound traditions. Our respect must be the greatest for the riches that have been bequeathed to us in the charism, as it was conceived, and which have been approved by the legitimate Authority of the Church.

Let us be persuaded that “union is life and confrontation is death; for where there is union there is virtue, and in confrontation there is only disorder and sin; for where there is union there is vigor, prosperity and progress, while discord leads to weakness, to decline, to nothingness. […] Unity strengthens families”85. And “if union in a religious family is a powerful evangelical witness, division among brothers is a stumbling block to evangelization”86.

That is why, in a certain way, everything comes down to our personal fidelity. A fidelity that implies, in the first place, the search for the Will of God. For there can be no fidelity if, instead of seeking the Will of God, I seek to follow my own. A fidelity that also implies—besides the coherence and constancy we have already mentioned—taking a stand and accepting the risks that must come with it. Let us not be naïve: we will not lack crosses, nor does God want us to lack them. Strength and courage are needed to live the identity of a religious, and of a religious of our beloved congregation of the Incarnate Word, without compromises, without a double life. But we need even more fortitude in times of greater combat, a combat that God permits in order to purify the soul and raise it to the highest summits of heroism—as may be the case when an entire Institute is shaken and tossed about by the storm so as to reveal all the beauty of the patience, fidelity and submission of its members.

You know it well: “fortitude is a firm disposition of soul to bear courageously any kind of evil, even the worst and most prolonged”87. That is why fortitude “always requires a certain overcoming of human weakness and, above all, of fear. For by nature we men spontaneously fear danger, unpleasantness and suffering. […] Fear sometimes robs us of courage when we are in the midst of a climate of threat, oppression or persecution. Thus those men have special courage who are capable of crossing the so-called fear barrier in order to bear witness to truth and justice. To reach such fortitude we must in a certain way ‘go beyond’ our own limits and ‘surpass’ ourselves, running the ‘risk’ of finding ourselves in an unknown situation, the risk of being frowned upon, the risk of exposing ourselves to unpleasant consequences, to calumnies, to demotions, to material losses and even to prison or persecutions. To attain such fortitude, one must be sustained by a great love for the truth and for the good to which one gives oneself. Let us understand it well: the virtue of fortitude walks at the same pace as the capacity for self-sacrifice”88. “This is the resounding idea: to sacrifice oneself. That is how history is directed, even silently and hiddenly”89, even if it costs us our lives.

That is why, all of you, listen well: We need strong men! For the truly just man is only the one who has the virtue of fortitude. Let us ask God for all the members, present ones and future generations, the gift of Fortitude, so that when we lack the strength to surpass ourselves with a view to higher values such as truth, justice, vocation, this “gift from on high” may make each one of us a strong man and may whisper within us at the opportune moment:

Take courage!90.

God's Special Providence toward Missionaries

In 1929 Pope Pius XI wrote a letter to all seminarians, especially to his “Jesuit sons,” asking them to enter a new seminary that had just begun in Rome to prepare young priests for possible missionary work in Russia. Walter Ciszek, who had entered the Society of Jesus in the United States the year before, volunteered to go. There he studied his theology and learned to celebrate Mass in the Byzantine rite, but after he was ordained1 –and, incidentally, Fr. Ciszek was the first American priest ordained according to the Byzantine rite– it was impossible to send priests to Russia. So they sent him to Poland, to an Eastern-rite mission that the Jesuits had in Albertyn. The war began in 1939.

In the confusion, and as a result of the invasions, Fr. Ciszek entered Russia on March 19, 1939 –the feast of St. Joseph– accompanying the Polish refugees, in the hope of being able to serve them spiritually. But soon, in 1941, the Soviet secret police discovered him and arrested him –with only 4 years of priesthood– on the false charge of ‘being a Vatican spy,’ and sent him to prison. He spent 6 years imprisoned in Lubyanka, and most of those years he was in solitary confinement. Moreover, in 1942 he was found guilty of espionage and, consequently, 4 more years were added to his sentence. By 1947 he had already been declared ‘legally dead’ in the United States. Afterward he was sentenced to 15 years of forced labor in the prison camps in Siberia. He was put to work in outdoor construction in extreme arctic cold, and he also worked in coal mines, poorly clothed, poorly fed and housed in miserable conditions, in barracks fenced in by barbed wire. He himself bears witness to how men, especially those who gave up hope, died. When the 15 years had passed, he remained living in small Siberian towns, because he could not leave Siberia nor even go to the main cities of Russia. So he worked as a mechanic and in other trades of that sort until the American government exchanged him, on October 12, 1963 –the feast of Our Lady of the Pillar– for two Russian spies. Twenty-four years had gone by, his first 24 years of priesthood. When, upon his return, people asked him how he had survived, how he had persevered, he stated: “I trusted in God; I never felt abandoned or without hope, and I survived along with many others. I never thought that my ‘survival’ was anything special or extraordinary, but I give thanks to God for having sustained and preserved me throughout all those years”2.

Concerning his experience in Lubyanka, and after recounting the inhuman situation in which he lived and the injustice of having been imprisoned without reason, and telling in detail how in prison he was despised precisely for being a priest, he writes: “No situation is lacking in value or meaning within divine providence. It is a very human temptation to feel frustrated by circumstances, to feel overwhelmed and helpless before the established order, whether that be a prison, or the Soviet system as a whole, or this whole oppressive and rotten world! In the worst circumstances imaginable, man remains man, endowed with a free will, and God is always ready to help him with His grace. What is more, God expects him to act in those circumstances, in that situation, as He wants him to act. For those situations too, those people, those places and those things are what God wills for him at that moment. It may not be in his power to change the ‘system,’ just as it was not in mine to change the conditions of the prison, but that is no excuse for failing to act. Many men feel frustrated, or discouraged, or even defeated when they find themselves facing a situation or an evil against which they can do little. Poverty, social injustice, hatred and resentment, war, corruption and the oppressive bureaucracy of institutions: all of it can give rise to a bitter frustration and, at times, a feeling of utter hopelessness. But God does not expect any man to change the world by himself, to put an end to all evils or cure all diseases. What He does expect of him is that he act as He wants him to act in the circumstances arranged by His will and His providence. To act in this way he will not lack the help of divine grace.

The feeling of hopelessness that we all experience in circumstances such as these actually arises from our tendency to bring too much of ‘our self’ into the picture. […] We tend to focus on ourselves, to think about what we can or cannot do, and we forget about God, about His Will and His Providence. God, however, never forgets the importance of each person, his dignity and his worth, and the role He asks us to play in the work of Providence. For God, every individual is equally important at every moment. We do matter to Him. But He also expects each one to accept, as coming from His hands, the daily situations He sends us, and that we act as He wants us to act, with the grace He grants us for it”3.

Fr. Walter Ciszek died on December 8, 1984 –the solemnity of the Immaculate Conception– in New York, United States.

Certainly his was an extreme experience; nevertheless, who among us has not experienced –to a greater or lesser degree– circumstances that put our trust in Divine Providence to the test? Even so, nothing less is expected of us than the attitude of trusting abandonment to the Providence of God, because “He alone knows all the secret springs that must be moved to bring us to heaven”4.

Therefore, whatever the particular circumstances in which we find ourselves, personally or as an Institute, it is fitting to follow the 11th rule of discernment that St. Ignatius gives us in his Spiritual Exercises: “… let the one who is in consolation strive to humble and lower himself as much as he can, thinking how little he is worth in the time of desolation without such grace or consolation. On the contrary, let the one who is in desolation think that he can do much with the sufficient grace to resist all his enemies, taking strength in his Creator and Lord”5. That is why it has seemed to us that it can be very wholesome for our souls to reflect on the special Providence that God has over His missionaries. A Providence that we have experienced not only individually but also as a missionary Institute throughout these 37 years of existence. In fact, we would dare to say that the birth, growth and life of our Institute is the continuous work of merciful Divine Providence and of the “maternal providence of Mary”6, through whose hands all graces come down to us.

1. Divine Providence in the life and works of our Institute

We would like, then, to begin by recalling –even if only in broad strokes– the blessings that God has been pleased to pour out upon our Institute, in spite of our great miseries, in spite of the threatening fists raised as we passed, of the steep difficulties we had to climb, of the countless obstacles that sowed fear in our souls… and how in all of this the Institute, for the glory and honor of Divine Providence, has come out victorious, has kept going forward, and we have afterward been able to say with St. John of the Cross: “that all is brief, that all lasts only until the knife is raised, and then Isaac remains alive, with the promise of a multiplied offspring”7.

For who can deny the special Providence of our Lord when Bishop Kruk, bishop of San Rafael, on October 7, 1983, authorized the experience of religious life and at the same time entrusted to our Founder the task of also beginning the work of the diocesan Seminary? How can we fail to see the delicate maternal hand of Most Holy Mary, who arranged that the Institute be founded on the providential date of March 25, 1984, when, at the conclusion of the “Holy Year of the Redemption,” St. John Paul II consecrated the world to the Immaculate Heart of Mary together with all the bishops of the world, and published the Apostolic Exhortation Redemptionis donum on religious consecration in the light of the mystery of the redemption?

How can we fail to see the interweaving of various providential circumstances, the fact that the following year, in 1985, the Villa de Luján was founded –our beloved Finca– where our Mother House was established, certainly in great poverty and yet in an overabundance of joy and self-giving– and which to this day is the house of the Institute that has given birth to the most missionaries for the Church?

And so we could go on listing the foundations: first in Suncho Corral (Santiago del Estero, Argentina) and then in Cusco, Peru; the founding of the Minor Seminary with the first adolescents who were arriving –who today are already missionary priests in different parts of the world–; the founding of the contemplative branch, the founding of the “Alfredo Bufano” Humanistic High School –the first humanistic academic institution in San Rafael and the first school of the Institute–. How can we not mention that on April 16, 1990, the first major house of formation of the Institute was founded: the “Mary Mother of the Incarnate Word” Seminary in San Rafael? And so on throughout the years, like continuous links of the admirable Providence of God over the Institute, foundations and new projects have kept opening, more vocations have arrived –even from countries where we are not even present–, more opportunities for apostolate have begun, there have been more threats from enemies and more interventions from friends, mistakes on our part and undoubtedly providential successes, trials that narrowed hope to the utmost, and interventions from heaven that widened our trust once more and drove fear from our brows; so that in the end we can say: Blessed be God for the trials to which He subjects us and the graces He grants us!

Moreover, we cannot fail to see the fact that God not only willed that our Institute be born under the pontificate of St. John Paul II, but that the Holy Father himself had a providential role in the young life of our small Religious Family. Indeed, when many were opposing what was ours, he knew how to say firmly, “regardless of what others may say, I want this institute to go forward”8. That is, the approval of our Institute was promoted and discerned by the Holy Pope, with all that this signifies. But his interventions were providential not only in his acting as an instrument ‘external’ to the Institute, taking care of us with an exquisite paternal solicitude, but even intrinsically, given that our Founder wrote the Constitutions, in 1992, taking as his guide for many of the subjects treated the magisterium of the Great Pope. In fact, there are more than 1000 explicit citations of St. John Paul II in our Proper Law, and many implicit ones. That is something to which we have perhaps grown accustomed over the years, but even when someone recently mentioned that fact to Cardinal Dziwisz9, he was pleasantly surprised.

Something more still: we must even acknowledge, with deep gratitude and in the most genuine humility, that even though “we live in a time characterized, in its own way, by the rejection of the Incarnation”10, our good God –who knows the times and the seasons– has raised up within His Church and for His Church our beloved Institute, which, although small and scant in means, has the most noble task of bearing witness to the world that the Word became flesh! And of “assuming in Christ all that is human”11 in order to elevate it, dignify it and perfect it12. Therefore, we can say that our Institute, with our specific charism and its proper mission, is a ‘work of God’ for the good of His Church. All of which requires not only fidelity on our part –guarding our identity with all its non-negotiable elements, transmitting all that very rich patrimony with all its force to future generations– but also –and this we would like to emphasize– it requires trust in Divine Providence. Because if the congregation is a work of God, and as the Apostle says, the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable13, He will know how to defend it in the face of the various vicissitudes we may go through now or in the future.

We have to be realistic: our life as an Institute that is “of the Incarnate Word” and that wishes always to follow the path He has shown us demands of us that we learn to live among thorns, without having anywhere to lay our head, afflicted in every way, but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; struck down, but not destroyed14; rather, comforted by God, who is ready to work wonders in the humble who entrust themselves to Him with all their heart.

At the same time, we must have great trust in the founding grace, especially in times of trial, because often the temptation can come to want to change things, that is, to want to change the charism that God has inspired, and therein often lies the ruin of congregations: in wanting to reform the intentions of the Founder regarding the charism of the Institute. Thus some think other things better because they see them in other Institutes; however, that is not what God asks of us. We must have great trust in our charism, because it is to live that that God has called this beloved Institute, and not to live something else. And if we do that, if we live according to the charism to which God in His Providence has destined us by granting us the religious vocation, we must be confident that we will become holy. Only in this way will we become members of the Institute “fit for the Master”15. That is why St. John Bosco gave this recommendation to the Salesians, which holds for us as well: “let us flee from the itch for reform. Let us strive to observe our rules without thinking of improving or reforming them”16.

When one learns or reviews the history of the Institute, one has no choice but to confess that we have more than enough reasons to rejoice and to thank God for the delicate Providence that has guided our steps, that has been a refuge in the storms, that has given us so many consolations in the midst of persecutions by men, that has been a kindly hand and a tender caress when thorns surrounded us and all doors seemed to be closing. That is why we can say with the Doctor St. John of Ávila: “May God be blessed in all things, may His judgments be adored, for where it seems to us a loss, there with His lofty wisdom He wins for us; and this in order to make us understand our little knowledge and insufficiency, and so that we may offer ourselves wholeheartedly, full of faith, into His hands, hoping for a remedy without knowing the way by which it is to come”17.

It is also true that each one of us has anecdotes and stories –some of them most incredible– in which Divine Providence has manifested itself plainly, for no other explanation would fit. What is more, we can affirm without fear of being mistaken that each mission of the Institute, each house, each work begun –the Seminaries, the Hogarcitos, the schools, the monasteries, etc.–, the vocations that God has been pleased to send us –from the most diverse cultures, from countries where the Institute is not even present, from the most diverse social and educational backgrounds–, the many and different activities organized by the Religious Family, even at the international level, have arisen, have been sustained and have grown undoubtedly thanks to Divine Providence. Without looking any further: the immense majority of us came to know the Institute in a providential way and have experienced from the moment of entering how ours is to live truly hanging on the divine bounty, because, as the Saint of Fontiveros says, “He who takes care of the beasts will not forget you”18. So that there has been fulfilled in us what Blessed Giuseppe Allamano told his own: “Divine Providence thinks of you, and you will never lack what is necessary”19. And we are persuaded that this has been so, because those who throw themselves into following Christ, leaving all things behind, trusting in everything to divine Providence, God does not abandon20.

What we have just said, all those graces and divine favors, should be kept very much in mind, always being grateful for them, so that their memory, above all when we pass through storms and “the soul comes to believe that all good things are finished forever… and that it will not return as it did the last time”21, may serve to prepare the soul against hopelessness.

“The times are difficult,” said St. John Paul II, “and at times the spirit feels troubled and depressed”22. Even so, and even when the circumstances are worse and are prolonged indefinitely, “keep your spirit fervent in spite of adversities and temptations, remembering what Don Orione said: ‘For us there is no other school, nor other master, nor other chair but that of the cross. Live the poverty of Christ, the silence and mortification of Christ, the humility and obedience of Christ, with purity and holiness of life; patient and meek, persevering in prayer, all united in mind and heart to Christ; in a word, live Christ’”23. That must be our attitude.

We must make the exercise of driving deep into the soul the revealed truth that all things work together for the good of those who love God24. “In saying all things,” as our Proper Law rightly explains, “he excepts nothing. Therefore here are included all events, prosperous or adverse, what concerns the good of the soul, the goods of fortune, reputation, all the conditions of human life (family, study, talents, etc.), all the interior states through which we pass (joys, gladness, privations, dryness, displeasures, tedium, temptations, etc.), even faults and sins themselves. Everything, absolutely everything. In saying they work together for the good, it is understood that they cooperate, contribute, happen, for our spiritual good. One must have this view and not that of the carnal or worldly man. One must see everything in the light of the loving designs of the Providence of God”25. And when the storms rage and everything seems to collapse, “let us set aside the grief and weakness that hold us fast, and let us run with patience to the war that is set before us, always looking, in order to take courage, to Jesus Christ, who, just as He is the author of our faith, will bring it to completion and perfect it in us; and just as He, though able not to die, willingly did not disdain to undergo every labor and affront, even to dying so shameful a death as that of the cross”26, so too let us be ready to die, if necessary, for the good of the Church and of the Institute in the service of Jesus Christ27.

Blessed Allamano made this request of the Consolata Missionaries, a request that today we would like to take as addressed to us as well: “I would like our Institutes in general, and you in particular, always to have this great trust in God [because]: He who trusts in the Lord will not be disappointed”28.

“Trust is a loving familiarity with Divine Providence, which accompanies us at every step of our life. Let us abandon ourselves to God and leave everything in His hands. He is a father and does everything for the best, for our good. We must never fear, either for the Institute or for each one in particular. In everything, even in the smallest things, let us raise ourselves to God and trust in Him alone, whatever the course of events may be. Let us not found our trust on the human means that are in us: talent, strengths, virtues, etc., or that are in others. Let us always do what we can on our part, then leave everything in the hands of the Lord, without fear. He never leaves His work half done”29.

Let us understand it well: our life as missionary religious entails considerable difficulties and obstacles. It is normal that this should concern us; however, at the same time that situation must be a providential stimulus for our Institute, so that with ever greater ardor we may overcome the temptations of discouragement and devote ourselves with vigor and self-giving to the marvelous task of inculturating the Gospel, without retreating from it a single step. Rather, our response should be to deepen the particular characteristics of the missionary vocation that distinguishes us and to renew ever more the charismatic commitment we have received, clinging ever more to the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism; in a word: being ever more religious of the Incarnate Word.

“Do not be afraid! Never doubt… never lose trust in the ‘consistency’ of your mission. The daily and generous profession of faith –I believe in You, Lord, who have willed me to be Your priest [or Your monk, or Your religious brother, or Your seminarian or Your novice of the Institute of the Incarnate Word] and a continuer of Your mission– must instill in you the daily and firm trust to remain at your post, to refresh your energies at the inexhaustible springs of grace, to resist the temptation of discouragement and abandonment”30.

2. To console the afflicted

At the beginning we quoted Fr. Ciszek, who bore witness to how men, especially those who gave up hope, died. It is true that in times of particular tribulation the most common temptation is to let oneself be carried away by discouragement and the loss of hope. So far, nothing new.

What must be new, in the sense of being different, is the attitude with which one faces the trial, the affliction, the tribulation, the persecution, the continual refusals; in short, everything ‘that is not pleasing’ and that, rather, causes us great sorrow.

Many religious, not infrequently with good reason, live exceedingly ‘worried’ and even anguished about ‘what is going to happen with the Institute,’ ‘how this is going to continue,’ ‘how much longer this can go on,’ and they feel dejected because they see no ‘way out,’ forgetting that the designs of God’s Providence transcend our ignorance and human limitation, and that very probably the help, the solution, the hoped-for ‘breathing space’ will come from where we least expect it.

The great Doctor of the Church, St. John of Ávila, in one of his letters addressed “to an afflicted maiden,” offers a treasure of counsels that we think can well be applied to this kind of worry we have just mentioned (and certainly to any tribulation, especially of the spiritual order). We are not going to transcribe the letter in its entirety, but some extracts that we will then comment on.

It may be that many times we find ourselves in such great straits that we seem to be in “the anguish in which the people of Israel found themselves when, going out of Egypt, they saw themselves hemmed in on the sides by very high mountains, and in front by the sea, and by the enemies coming from behind (cf. Ex 13:9); and you will often feel what David said and felt in himself: I said in the alarm of my soul: I am cast out from before the sight of Your eyes (Ps 30:23). […] And it will happen to you to call and not be heard; and in that in which you sought and hoped for a remedy, there greater desolation will befall you […] But amid these things, what do you think should be done? Shall we perhaps lose the confidence of our remedy, which Christ so many times commanded us to have? Shall we follow the faintings that the devil and our flesh bring us, or the hope that we can draw from the kindness of Him who, when He is angry, remembers His mercy? There is not much to deliberate here, but rather to carry out; there is no reason to lose heart, but rather to make an effort”31.

Here is the first lesson: the more the trial rages, the harder the suffering, there is not much to deliberate; rather, one must carry out; there is no reason to lose heart, but rather to make an effort. That is, the fiercer the struggle appears, the more threatening the snares, the more one must know how to see in that very thing a spur that urges us on to combat. One must ‘face up’ to the situation, in the sense of accepting the trial and confronting it with “attitude.” Because there are some who have so much patience in this matter of wanting to move forward that, as St. John of the Cross says, “God would not wish to see so much of it in them”32. Let us be persuaded that what “is needed [are] holy priests and religious who are… soldiers… who fight”33, “not with a childish spirit, but with a robust will”34. We must not wait for someone else to do it for us, we must not wait for the superiors alone to take charge; rather, each one from his own post must fulfill what he has to do with zeal, with humility and with trust in God, without losing heart if he has to make some sacrifice, but rather thinking that his labors will redound to the benefit of the Institute to which we have consecrated ourselves.

We have often said that we must have an ‘esprit de corps,’ function as ‘one body,’ which is not reduced exclusively to being united and ‘on the same page’ regarding our principles and values, but also means that the effort, the struggle, belongs to everyone. For not infrequently some, although with a good heart they are distressed by the present situation, believe and demand that the solution must come from others, when in reality one must “know oneself capable of helping”35 and in fact put it into practice, each one in his place, with his own duties and carrying his own crosses.

One must know how to see the “grace of the trial,” in which God, says St. John of Ávila, “like a good father, hides the love He has for His children, so that they may not become slack and falsely secure, but may always have a little apprehension, so that they do not grow careless and lose the gift and inheritance that He keeps for them in heaven. And although He knows what a great hardship it is for them to feel that He is not pleased, and how many temptations rise up in them when He seems to turn His face away, nevertheless He wants them to pass through these anguishes; and, seeing them and loving them, He conceals the love He has for them and teaches them what, though it pains them, keeps them secure”36. It is fitting, then, in the face of the trial, to fill one’s heart with courage and one’s courage with trust in God, because He who has called us to this vocation will never abandon us, if we do not let go of His hand. We must reject the temptations of distrust in Divine Providence, which would never have entrusted us with so many apostolates, sent us so many vocations, chosen us to care for and assist so many of His poor and marginalized in our Hogarcitos, if at the same time it had not destined for us a help, an aid, a very sufficient and abundant grace for our support and so that we might move forward.

The Master of Ávila goes on to say: “And what is most to be marveled at is that He not only lets them suffer persecutions raised up by the devil and by other persons, but the very Father of mercies (2 Cor 1:3) and true lover of His children above all the fathers there are, […] not only sees what we suffer from our enemies and keeps silent, but He Himself raises up labors for us and puts us into the war. He is the one who is wont to give us joy after much sadness, as He gave to Abraham and to Isaac. […] And just as He commanded the father to kill the son that God Himself had given him (cf. Gen 22:2), and put into sadness the one whom He had first consoled, so He is wont to take joy away from His own and tell them to kill it, and that they should live in continual sadness.

[But] why, then, should you be anguished over what our Lord sends? Why does the medicine that has passed through the hand of your loving Father taste bad to you? Do you think perhaps that He has severity to afflict you, and not power to deliver you from wherever you have fallen, and mercy to forgive you and show you greater mercies than before? […] God keeps you safe among those thorns, to spare you those that will never end, as He says speaking of His vineyard: Night and day I guard it (Is 27:3f); I have no anger against it; […] for whether He consoles now or afflicts now, His sacred vigil37 is over us, and is then nearer when we consider it farther away”38.

It is what we have so often repeated: “God does not abandon us, but governs us.” With a courageous and calm attitude in the face of the evil that assails us and the trials that God permits to befall us, let us repeat with St. Boniface: “Since things are so, and the truth can be attacked but not defeated or deceived, our weary mind takes refuge in those words of Solomon: Trust in the Lord with all your soul, do not rely on your own understanding; in all your ways think of Him, and He will make your paths straight. And in another place: A most strong tower is the name of the Lord; the just man hopes in Him and is helped.” “Let us stand fast in righteousness and prepare our souls for the trial; let us know how to endure until the time God wills, and let us say to Him: ‘Lord, You have been our refuge from generation to generation.’ Let us have trust in Him, who is the one who has laid this burden upon us. What we cannot carry by ourselves, let us carry with the strength of Him who is almighty and who has said: my yoke is easy and my burden light. Let us stand firm in the struggle on the day of the Lord, since days of anguish and affliction have come upon us. Let us die, if God so wills, for the holy laws of our fathers, if God so wills, so that we may deserve, like them, to obtain the eternal inheritance”39.

In another passage of his letter the Doctor of Ávila says: “You will not be able, with all your thinking and straining, to add, as the Gospel says, a single cubit to your stature (cf. Mt 6:27). […] And the more it seems to you that you find no ford for your ills, nor where nor how they are to be remedied, the more hope there is of a remedy; for where human counsel and strength are lacking, there God is accustomed to put His hand, and that is the very hour He was waiting for to show mercy, so that men may know that not by their sword nor their bow (cf. Josh 24:12), but in the pleasing and loving will of God lies their remedy. And therefore, the fuller of miseries you see yourself, the more you should consider yourself prepared and disposed for God to work His mercy in you, because compassion for our anguishes moves Him to set His eyes upon us. Where miseries abound most, there His mercies abound most […] And if this day does not come as soon as you desire, do not be troubled on that account, for to delay is not to take away, especially when the giver is truthful.

[…] Remember that the people of God were never so afflicted, having burden upon burden laid on them and being given cruel lashes, as when they were on the eve of liberty. And just as after night and rain there usually comes day and a very bright sun, and after the storm came calm, and after the pains of childbirth the joy of the child born, so think that your great labors are messengers of great joy.”

How profitable this counsel is, above all for those religious who are frightened by the possible scenarios, who want to draw back before the objective difficulties that present themselves, or who remain with their arms folded waiting for the solution to ‘come from above.’ Such as these forget that the Put out into the deep!40 pronounced by the Incarnate Word on the lake of Gennesaret, and since then and until the end of time repeated to countless souls, entails “taking seriously, thoroughly, the demands of the Gospel”41, for it requires “humble, hardworking men who do not fear dangers, watchful, patient in the long vigils, constant in repeating their outings to sea… ready to die”42. Therefore, we must nourish hope, fill our souls with courage, knowing ourselves loved by God, strengthen ourselves interiorly, thinking that “the measure of Divine Providence over us is the trust we have in it”43, and that as the painful circumstances arrive, God, whose we are and to whom we belong, will deliver us from them. And if He has protected us up to the present, if we continue clinging firmly to the hand of His Providence, He Himself will assist us on every occasion, and if we were unable to go on, He will sustain us. We must not lose heart! We must not look back! The recognition of our limits and weaknesses –which seem to shine forth even more in times of trial– can be transformed into an occasion to experience the extraordinary strength and richness of God’s grace, as in fact we have already experienced so very many times throughout the life of the Institute.

“God is keeping you in trial,” the letter of St. John of Ávila continues; “be faithful to Him in obeying all that He sends you; love Him, even though He scourges you; follow Him, even though He turns His face from you; importune Him, even though He does not answer you; and know that you will not labor in vain, because He is faithful and cannot deny Himself, and He will not despise to the end the prayer of the poor (2 Tim 2:13; cf. Ps 101:18). He will rise up and command the sea to be calm; He will give you your Isaac alive, and turn your weeping into song, and give you abundance of peace for the wars you have suffered. And if you do not deserve this good, He has the goodness to do it. What is asked of you is that you learn to live among thorns, without having anywhere to lay your head; and if you can do little, let it be made up for by suffering; and that you stand firm on the way of God, since only he loses the crown who flees and abandons it; for as regards your remedy, the Lord will give it to you when and how you do not know; and for the present labor He will give you abundance of joy with which to praise Him here and in heaven, to the perpetual honor of His Majesty”44.

We have said it on another occasion: not fighting, fleeing, setting aside the things for which it is worth dying, that is already losing even before having begun the struggle. Our Proper Law says it: “cowards die many times before their death”45. We must remain firm. Let us be men of faith, of “a living, firm, intrepid, eminent, heroic faith; a faith convinced that God would not be God if we were capable of encompassing Him with our limited intelligence, if we understood all His judgments and ways”46. Let us be men who act as He wants us to act in the circumstances arranged by His will and His providence, moved by that faith which “triumphs over the world and evil, which builds great things, which illumines life and gives it meaning, which strengthens, encourages, comforts and casts out fear: Do not be afraid! It is I47. And with that filial trust that faith itself inspires in us, “let us wrest graces from the Lord. [For] much trust is needed to be able to be a little bold, a little ‘overbearing,’ in asking for miracles. The Lord is not offended by that. […] Let us pray with perseverance, without losing heart if God does not answer our prayers right away. Let us knock at His door: if He does not open, let us knock harder; if that is not enough, let us break down the door!”48.

This is the final message: let us not live cowed, fearful, faint-hearted, but with trust in the merciful designs of God, and let us launch out boldly to restore all things in Christ49.

We must not fear! Notice what St. John of the Cross says, which we can well apply to the Institute and to each one of its faithful members: “for, since He is omnipotent, He does you good and loves you with omnipotence; and since He is wise, He does you good and loves you with wisdom; and since He is infinitely good, He loves you with goodness; and since He is holy, He loves you and grants you favors with holiness; and since He is just, He loves you and grants you favors justly; since He is merciful, compassionate and clement, [He makes you experience] His mercy and compassion and clemency; and since He is a strong, lofty and delicate being, He loves you strongly, loftily and delicately; and since He is clean and pure, He loves you with purity and cleanness; and since He is true, He loves you truly; and since He is liberal, He loves you and grants you favors with liberality, without any self-interest, only to do you good; and since He is the virtue of supreme humility, He loves you with supreme goodness and supreme esteem, saying to you, not without great jubilation on your part: I am yours and for you, and I delight in being such as I am in order to be yours and to give Myself to you”50.

And let us remember that in everything we count on the most effective help of Most Holy Mary, Mother of Divine Providence. She “takes care of our human affairs”51. For this reason we trust that God, who, looking upon her, did “great things” in her, will do as much with us, her sons of the Institute of the Incarnate Word, who have given everything and have given themselves through the hands of His Holy Mother, for His glory and that of His holy Church.

Let us pray with filial trust to the Mother of God, who keeps nothing for herself52, for ourselves and for the Institute, the ‘overbearing’ prayer with which St. Francis de Sales ‘importuned’ the Virgin:

Remember, most sweet Virgin,

that you are my Mother and I am your son;

that you are most powerful

and I am small, poor, wretched and weak.

I beg you, my sweet Mother,

to govern and defend me

in all my undertakings and actions.

Do not tell me, gracious Virgin, that you cannot;

for your beloved Son has given you all power.

Nor tell me that you ought not,

for you are the common Mother

of all poor human beings,

and mine in a singular way.

If you could not, I would excuse you, saying:

-It is true that she is my Mother

and that she loves me as her son,

but she lacks the power.

If you were not my Mother, I would rightly be patient, saying:

-She is very rich and could help me;

but alas, since she is not my Mother, she does not love me.

But, O most sweet Virgin,

since you are my Mother

and since you are powerful, how could I excuse you if you did not protect me?

You see, then, my Mother, that you are bound to attend to all my petitions.

For the honor and glory of your Son,

accept me as your son,

without regard to my miseries and sins.

Deliver my soul and my body from all evil,

and give me all your virtues, above all humility.

In short, obtain for me all the gifts,

goods and graces that are pleasing to the Most Holy Trinity,

Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Amen.

May the Most Holy Virgin, who in the most profound obedience to the divine designs virginally conceived and gave birth to the Son of Man grant us the grace to have a providential view of all of life, to be men truly “abandoned to Providence”53, and may her maternal providence guide and protect, today and always, the little boat of our life, both individually and as an Institute.

Come then! Take heart! For we belong to the Virgin and the Virgin is ours.

[Exordium] In the Gospel we have just heard, the evangelist mentions how, after the healing of the leper, as the fame of Christ spread more and more, the crowds came flocking in great numbers to hear Him and to be healed of their illnesses1.That is, Jesus devotes Himself to those who suffer, to those on the margins of society. He heals them and thus opens up for them the possibility of living and deciding together with others; He brings them into equality and fraternity.

This, obviously, concerns all of us as well: Jesus shows all of us the direction of our action; He tells us how we must act. And since today too there are pathologies and deadly illnesses that infect our religion and our very reason, it is necessary that we too take on the responsibility of healing. What illnesses are these? For example, the destruction of the image of God because of hatred and fanaticism, the passion for novelty that forgets the truth, accepting everything ‘unfiltered’ in the eagerness to appear tolerant or ‘open-minded,’ raising the banner of equality where everything ends up being a shapeless mixture and one’s own identity is diluted. We must be careful about this, because none of us is exempt from it. G. K. Chesterton warned with his usual irony: “Do not be so open-minded that your brain falls out”2. “Merely being open-minded means nothing. The purpose of opening the mind is like that of opening the mouth: it is in order to close it again on something solid”3.

That is why our task continues to be not to propose “technical solutions” to poverty, to the tragedy of racism still prevailing today in many countries, to the precarious health situation that affects so many places; rather, our “first contribution to the solution of social problems must be the proclamation of the truth about Christ, about the Church and about man, applying it to a concrete situation”4. This is what we call ‘biting into reality.’ “The social question and the Gospel are truly inseparable. If we give men only knowledge, skills, technical abilities and tools, we give them too little”5.

Therefore, it is necessary to make Jesus Christ known, loved and believed in; hearts must be converted, so that there may also be progress in the social sphere, so that reconciliation may begin, so that injustices and the social scourges of drug addiction, adultery, etc. may be fought, truly confronting their deep causes and healing the sick with due attention and compassion.

The world needs God. We need God. We do not impose our faith on anyone. That kind of proselytism is contrary to Christianity. But we must also say that faith can only develop in freedom. And it is to the freedom of men that we appeal, asking that it open itself to God, seek Him, listen to Him, let itself be healed. We do not disrespect other religions and cultures, we do not disrespect their faith, if we confess aloud and without half-measures the Incarnate Word, that God who opposed His suffering to violence, who in the face of evil and its power raised up His mercy. For Who is it that overcomes the world but he who believes that Jesus is the Son of God?6.

What the world needs is intolerance7

We can say then, with the Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen, that the world today does not suffer from intolerance, but from tolerance. Yes, listen carefully.

Our world, our country and so many others are not suffering from a lack of tolerance, but from a lack of intolerance. For people tolerate, as if it were nothing, the jumbling together of good and evil, of truth and error, of virtue and wickedness, of Christ and chaos. That is why we can say with Sheen: our country –present-day society– has not been invaded by intolerant people, but by the ‘open-minded’8. Because if a man reaches a decision in an orderly way, discerning, guided by principles, just as any man makes up his bed every morning, then he is called ‘intolerant,’ a fanatic, ‘self-referential’; but if a man cannot make up his mind, and instead shows himself ambivalent before the options, he is called tolerant, open-minded.

An intolerant man is one who refuses to accept a reason for anything; an open-minded man is one who accepts anything for a reason, provided it is not a good reason. It is true that there is a demand for precision, exactness and definition, but it is only a demand for scientific precision, not logical precision. This has produced a mental collapse, says F. Sheen, which shows itself in three ways: the tendency to settle problems not with arguments but with mere words, the willingness to accept without reservation anyone’s authority on the subject of religion, and, lastly, the love of novelty.

The fact is that the truth of our holy religion has the right to be heard from the lips of qualified spokesmen, just as the science of physics or astronomy has the right to be heard through its qualified spokesmen [hence the urgent need for a solid formation]. Religion is a science, even though some turn it into a feeling. Religion has its principles, natural and revealed, which are more demanding in their logic than mathematics. But the false notion of tolerance has obscured this fact in the eyes of many who are extremely intolerant about the smallest details of life but extremely tolerant in their relations with God: they teach aberrations contrary to the faith, they commit grave injustices, everything good is suspect to them, all heroism seems to them extremism, etc .

Another sign of the collapse of reason that has produced this strange mushroom of ‘open minds’ is the passion for novelty, as opposed to the love of truth. Truth is sacrificed for an epigram9, the Divinity of Christ for a headline in Monday morning’s newspaper. What is more, more than one ‘modern pastor’ is much less concerned with preaching Christ and Him crucified than with his popularity among the people. That lack of intellectual backbone makes men talk nonsense by the heap, and although we will not give examples here, it is enough to repeat what Chesterton said: “Those who abandon the tradition of truth do not escape into something called freedom. They only escape into something else that we call fashion.” That is why we very rarely see an accusing finger raised against the injustices that occur in the courts, against parents who favor and even encourage homosexuality in their children; no voice rings in the ears of the rich, saying with something of ‘intolerance’: It is not lawful for you to live with your brother’s wife. Rather we hear: “Friends, times are changing!”

To believe in the existence of God, in the divinity of Christ, in the moral law, is considered a thing of the past. But, mind you, the latest thing in this new fashion of tolerance is what is considered the true, as if truth were a fashion, like a hat, instead of being a well-founded institution, like the head.

Finally, this broadness of outlook makes us think that truth is novelty and that, therefore, ‘truth’ changes with the passing fancies of the moment. Like the chameleon that changes color to adapt itself to its surroundings, they claim that truth changes to adapt itself to the weaknesses and crookedness of the age. Pay attention: The nature of certain things is fixed, and none more so than the nature of truth. Truth may be challenged a thousand times, but that only shows that it is strong enough to survive a thousand assaults. For someone to say: ‘Some say this, some say that, therefore there is no truth,’ is as logical as it would have been for Columbus, hearing some say, ‘The earth is round,’ and others saying, ‘The earth is flat,’ to conclude: ‘Therefore, there is no earth.’ And just as a carpenter might get rid of his ruler and use each beam as a measuring rod, so too those who have cast aside the standard of objective truth have nothing left to measure by except the fashion of thought of the moment.

The giddiness of novelty, the sentimental restlessness of an unstable mentality and the unnatural fear of a good dose of solid thinking all seem to combine to produce that little band of the tolerant who think there is no difference between God as Cause and God as a “mental projection”; or who put Christ and Buddha on the same level, or who then, in a sweeping overflow of their ‘already open minds,’ say not only that any ‘Christian’ sect is as good as another, but even that any religion in the whole world –whatever that religion may be– is as good as another, or that every religious institute must be the same as the others –and therefore forget its own identity– practicing a general love of God and neighbor. How many religious, carried away by an excessive desire for adaptability and spontaneity, in their eagerness to be ‘open-minded,’ to progress, to update themselves, have abandoned the characteristic features of their religious family so as to blend in with the others, and have left the works they were carrying out for fear of being branded ‘rigid and old-fashioned’10.

Meanwhile, any sensible man wonders how there can be progress without direction and how there can be direction without a fixed point. And because one speaks of a “fixed point,” they immediately say that one is backward, that such people are closed, when in reality they are beyond time, mentally and spiritually speaking.

That is why, says Fulton Sheen, in the face of this false ‘open-mindedness,’ what the world needs is intolerance. The world seems to have completely lost the faculty of distinguishing between good and evil, right and wrong. There are some minds that believe intolerance is always wrong, because they make ‘intolerance’ mean hatred, intellectual narrowness or fanaticism. And they are the same ones who believe that tolerance is always right because, for them, it means ‘charity,’ being intelligent –precisely because ‘they are open-minded’– and having a good nature, which more often than not is falsely compassionate.

What is tolerance?

Tolerance is an attitude of reasoned patience toward evil and a restraint that keeps us from showing anger or inflicting punishment. But what is more important than the definition is the field of its application. The important point here is this: tolerance applies only to persons, but never to truth. Intolerance applies only to truth, but never to persons. Tolerance applies to those who err; intolerance to error.

That is why today the society of the contemporary world does not suffer so much from intolerance, understood as fanaticism, but from tolerance, which is indifference toward truth and error, and that philosophical indifference which is interpreted as being open-minded. Do not misunderstand me: of course greater tolerance (in the good sense) is desirable, because one can never show too much charity to people who differ from us. In fact, our Lord Himself asked that “we love those who slander us,” because they are always persons, but He never told us to love slander.

In accordance with the Spirit of Christ, the Church has always encouraged us to pray for all those who are outside the bosom of the Church and asks that the greatest charity be shown to them. Charity, then, must be shown to all persons and, in particular, to those who are outside the fold, who must be drawn or brought back by charity, so that there may be one flock and one Shepherd. Shall God, who does not look with an equally tolerant eye on all religions, perhaps be denied the name of ‘Wisdom’ and be called an ‘intolerant’ God?

The Church identifies herself with Christ both in time and in principles. She began to reflect on her first principles, and the more she reflected, the more dogmas she developed. The Church never forgot those dogmas, but remembered them, and her remembering is what we call the Tradition of the Church. The dogmas of the Church, the truth preached by the Church throughout all these centuries, are like bricks, solid elements with which man can build, not like “religious experience,” which, like straw, is good only for burning. The Church has been and will always be intolerant with regard to the rights of God, because heresy, error and falsehood do not concern personal matters in which one may yield, but the very Divine Law itself, in which one must not yield. Truth is divine; the heretic is human. If due reparation is made, the Church will admit the heretic back into the bosom of her Wisdom, but never the heresy. What is right is right even if no one is in the right; and evil is evil even if everyone is mistaken.

[Peroration] To conclude: The attitude of the Church toward the modern world on this important question can be better grasped by following the story of those two women in Solomon’s courtroom. Both claimed the child. The rightful mother insisted on having the whole child or nothing, because a child is like the truth: it cannot be divided without being ruined. The false mother, on the contrary, agreed to a compromise. She was willing to divide the baby, and the baby would have died because of that ‘broad-mindedness.’

We must perceive our mission in history and try to correspond to it. “It is important to say clearly in which God we believe and to profess with conviction the human face of God”11. What is needed is responsibility and concern for our salvation and for the salvation of the whole world. Let us be servants of the Truth, as our Proper Law asks of us: of the truth about God, of the truth about man and his mysterious destiny, of the truth about the world. A difficult truth that we must seek in the Word of God and of which we are neither the owners nor the arbiters, but the depositaries, the heirs, the servants12.

Hence we must, even at the cost of renunciations and sacrifices, seek the Truth, form ourselves in the Truth, assimilate the Truth that we must transmit to others, without dissembling out of a desire to please others, to astonish, or to make an impression. Without obscuring the Truth through laziness in seeking it, or for the sake of comfort; rather, we must serve it without trampling on it.

In this Mass we ask the Blessed Virgin for the grace to strip ourselves of the old man, which is like the leprosy we read about today in the Gospel, and instead to be clothed by Her with the purity, the tastes and the will of our Divine Savior. So be it.

“The devil you know not”
Part IV

Today we are going to present the third strategy the devil uses, which is diversion (in the two previous conferences we spoke of deception and of division), always using as our basis Louis J. Cameli’s book The Devil You Know Not.

  • Diversion: I would like to begin speaking of this third strategy of Satan by reading a passage from the book of Exodus: When the people saw that Moses delayed in coming down from the mountain, they gathered around Aaron and said to him: ‘Make us a God who will go before us, for we do not know what has happened to Moses, that man who brought us out of Egypt.’ Aaron answered them: ‘Take off the gold rings that your wives, your sons and your daughters wear in their ears, and bring them here.’ So all of them took off their earrings and gave them to Aaron. He received the gold, worked it with a chisel and made a calf of molten metal. Then they said: ‘This is your God, Israel, who brought you out of Egypt.’ Seeing this, Aaron built an altar before the statue and announced in a loud voice: ‘Tomorrow there will be a feast in honor of the Lord.’ And the next morning, very early, they offered holocausts and communion sacrifices. Then the people sat down to eat and drink, and afterwards rose up to amuse themselves. The Lord said to Moses: ‘Go down at once, for your people, whom you brought out of Egypt, have become corrupt. They have quickly turned aside from the way I had pointed out to them, and have made themselves a calf of molten metal.’ Then they bowed down before it, offered sacrifices to it and cried out: ‘This is your God, Israel, who brought you out of Egypt’1.

Undoubtedly the devil wants our ruin; he wants us too to suffer his situation, that is, enmity with God. We have already seen how he employs deception and suggestion to divide us. Now we will see how he strives to divert us from the path pointed out by God, in the same way that he diverted the people of Israel, who were on their way to a free land, drawing them away from the worship of the one God.

Idolatry, in fact, represents one of the main forms of diversion the devil employs. And now we will also see others that the devil uses with great effectiveness to derail us on our way to God.

Pay attention: the aim of the tactic of diversion is simply to make us lose focus, that is, the center of our attention, namely: Jesus Christ; and also to make us lose the meaning of our life and the end toward which we are walking; it really derails us, draws us aside, makes us miss the mark, diverts us from our mission (or at least he tries to by every means). So, while deception and division can at times become dramatic, with a more explicit manifestation, so to speak, diversion normally operates subtly. And in truth, it may take us some time to realize that we are being led in another direction or that we have been going astray for a long time.

The basic remedy for this diversion is simply the disposition to be attentive and focused on what God wants of us, and to cling to Christ, who always shows us how to remain faithful and how to live in accordance with the end we are aiming at.

Let us now look at some of the manifestations of these strategies of the devil in our lives.

  • Diversion by becoming absorbed in a task, in an office. At first sight, becoming absorbed in the task that falls to us may seem like “staying on track,” that is, like a way of aiming at the end toward which we are heading. But in fact, being absorbed in a task that has been given to us does not mean that we have correctly understood the direction we must take. Being absorbed in a task can block our attention from the purpose par excellence, which is union with God, and toward which we must orient ourselves beyond the intense demands our office makes on us. Mind you, it is not that what keeps us busy is bad, but at times it may take away from us the attention and energy that we ought to use in being transfigured into Christ. A classic example is that of Martha and Mary.

What Jesus says to Martha He says to us: what occupies us—and occupies our time, our energies, our attention—may not be what ought to occupy us. How many priests spend their time listening to little videos by political commentators, reading the newspapers of the USA, of Europe, of Argentina… and obviously, since out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks, the only topic of conversation they have is that… If only they would spend all that time and energy reading something for ongoing formation, calling the loneliest parishioners, those who are sick, to see if they need anything; thinking about what they can do to reach souls… Do you see? Many other examples could be given: the one who spends his time climbing mountains with young people and it seems that his whole priestly life is just organizing outings; the one who spends more time busy with ‘administrative’ tasks of the parish than in contact with souls; the one who thinks that instead of being a pastor he is the head of a construction company, and instead of pastoral activities has only building initiatives; or, on the contrary, the one who gives an excessive importance to the care of souls, or to his spiritual life (and spends his time shut up in his room reading) and forgets that the church is falling to pieces on him, that it is dirty, that for months there has been a burnt-out bulb in the sanctuary that needs changing, that the sound system does not work and a new one must be obtained…; or the one who concentrates only on the liturgy but neglects the catechetical preparation that this liturgy requires, and both he and the people end up dissatisfied with the mess that results… The point is that being wrapped up, concentrating excessively on one activity, can make us stray from the path on which God wants us to walk.

The story of Martha and Mary illustrates, it seems to me, how subtle and how powerful this strategy can be, which uses self-absorption, our being absorbed in an activity, to draw us away from what we ought to do. Notice that Martha is doing something good, very good: serving. Her moral uprightness is beyond question. Notice also that she is striving to achieve something of great worth: to serve Our Lord. Now, one small detail: doing a good thing that really involves something of great worth, and moreover doing it in such a way that it ‘shows’ that I do it better than others, is really a powerful temptation to become wrapped up in it, and it works with many souls. Other things, which may be much more important, run the risk of being easily eclipsed. I leave it there for you to think about.

Another of the ways in which this strategy of diversion can be identified is, in a certain way, the opposite of the previous one, that is, by disdain for the activity that has been entrusted to us. It may happen that we are absolutely certain of the direction we must follow and the goal we must reach. We know exactly what we are supposed to do, or if you want it more clearly: what we are obliged to do. But at the same time we are repelled by the idea of having to do it and having to walk that path. The devil, who is very cunning, takes advantage of some thought of ours, of a lack of ‘liking,’ of some hidden laziness, and fixes us on something negative, making us despise what we have to do, as if it lowered us…

The classic example is the story of the prophet Jonah. God entrusts him with an important mission: Set out at once for Nineveh, the great city, and cry out against it, for its wickedness has come up before me2. But instead of doing what God asks of him, Jonah set out to flee to Tarshish, away from the presence of the Lord3. Literally. Eventually he goes to Nineveh, fulfills his mission, the Ninevites repent and God forgives them. Nevertheless, Jonah was extremely displeased that God had forgiven them, and he considered them contemptible people. This contempt he had for them was so strong that it diverted him from fulfilling the mission God had entrusted to him. He knew very well what his mission was, but the thought that he was among lowly, rough, poor people made him turn aside from the mission for which God had chosen him.

Many times the same thing happens to us: we think we are among ‘savages,’ that they are ignorant people who will never understand, etc., and we do not teach them, we do not catechize them, and on top of that we excuse ourselves by saying: “ah, it’s because in my parish there are no nuns”: then you teach them! Something similar happens to others regarding their place of mission: that it is a place of punishment, that they sent me here so I wouldn’t bother anyone… “am I not good for something better?”, some ask themselves… And watch out, because thinking that way, that same questioning, that dissatisfaction, that disdain can then be carried over to the vocation itself: “I didn’t become a priest to look after the disabled”; “my thing is teaching and here I am…, they send me to a parish I don’t like, in the middle of the city…”. Conclusion: so I leave. Such men forget that our calling “is not command or honors, but total self-giving to the service of God and to the pastoral ministry”4, and that implies any work, among whatever people, and for as long as God wills. To act otherwise is to set aside the charism of the Institute, which commands us to work in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions5. What is more, the Constitutions also say that we must “Gladly accept, and even ask for, the lowliest offices, the most difficult assignments…”6“those where no one wants to go”7.

Notice that this strategy of diversion is particularly ‘successful’ in the Church, and the best of missions can be ruined by it. At times one sees so much mess in the Church, so many problems, so many injustices, that really the little, the very little one does seems no longer to make sense: what for?! Thank goodness St. Ignatius did not get caught up in that thought, otherwise the Counter-Reformation would not have happened.

Another way in which this strategy of diversion presents itself: diversion means aiming a little bit to the side instead of at the center. But that slight deviation can, in the long run, make one deviate seriously from the initial starting point.

Generally the way the enemy operates, according to this strategy, is to begin with a clear and noble mission, but then, by means of temptations, he gradually moves you in a very different, even opposite, direction. If that happens, the original vision we had is corrupted and one ends up turned in on oneself.

St. Anthony the Abbot had a temptation of this kind. St. Athanasius tells it: “But the devil, who hates and envies what is good, could not bear to see such resolution in a young man, and tried by every means to do what he had done with so many others. First of all, he tried to draw him away from the discipline, whispering to him the memory of his wealth, the care of his sister, the claims of his kin, the love of money, the love of glory, the various pleasures of the table and other relaxations of life, and lastly the difficulty of virtue and of the labor to attain it; he also worked on him with thoughts of the infirmity of his body and the length of the trials. In a word, he raised in his mind a great cloud of debate, wishing to draw him away from the purpose on which he had settled.”

The devil tries to do the same with us. Note that this strategy takes advantage of our psychology to maximize the power of temptations. On the one hand, it takes advantage of our inclination to have “human securities,” especially in what concerns material things (that I have health insurance, that I know what is going to happen from now until next Easter, that I can have a car at my disposal, that the superior supports me in ‘my projects,’ etc.). At another level of temptations, it awakens in me a certain nostalgia for the things I have left behind: “ah, when I was in Guyana… I did what I wanted, the people were wonderful!”; “ah, when I lived with so-and-so… it was fantastic.”

And at a third level these temptations trigger a temptation that is in some way more dangerous than the previous ones: discouragement. Now, listen carefully: at all those levels at which temptation operates there is a central and very powerful message that the devil wants to suggest to us: all resistance is impossible. Only clinging to Christ tooth and nail is effective in fighting against this temptation and finally overcoming it.

This kind of temptation, if one does not know how to overcome it, can have a devastating effect on the one who suffers it. The power of the temptation derives from the total reversal of what the person was initially seeking, from the discouraging fact that this reversal still feels like a real possibility, and—what is most significant—from the sense of total loneliness that accompanies the temptation. Only in the solace of Christ’s presence can that crushing loneliness find relief.

An even more common way of diversion is by means of distraction. There is a military strategy that says that to check the advance of an army one has to destroy its communications command. Because if they cannot communicate with one another, they will surely fall. Similarly, if the devil can make us stray from our path toward union with God, it will be precisely by interrupting our communication with God, that is, our prayer. We are not speaking here of the common distractions that occur in our daily prayer. But of those distractions that come to us from anxieties or fears about the future, from the slights and offenses we have suffered at the hands of others, from comparing our situation with that of others, and from thinking about the pleasures of the present moment.

Regarding fears and anxieties, let us say that they are like a fixed feature of human nature. Now, if we realize that we keep going around in the same circles of worry again and again, it is because we are stuck (stagnant) within the perimeter of “unnecessary anxiety.” Why worry any more about what I cannot solve! When these anxieties plague my prayer life, they have simply diverted me from contact with the Incarnate Word, in whom my future rests. I have to realize that this bombardment of worries (perhaps very legitimate ones) prevents me from contact with Our Lord, who is precisely our surest source of security.

The other distraction that can draw us away from our path is turning over and over within ourselves the offenses we have received. How many religious spend their hour of adoration opening and reopening the wounds they have received. It is true: being offended, being treated with disrespect, being criticized or falsely accused knocks us down. Now, if for whatever reason one cannot resolve that offense, that wound can fester and reappear at some other moment of difficulty. And with it return all the feelings of anger, of resentment, of not wanting to cross paths with that person again, etc. These thoughts invade many religious especially during the time of prayer, as perhaps some of you have experienced. Now, if that is the case, how is it resolved? Simply with prayer and trust in God as the avenger of the wrongs done to me. And of course, by learning to forgive and by being humble. Those are the two means by which we can break the spell of the distraction that resentment causes us and that diverts us from contact with God in prayer.

The third kind of distraction is comparing my situation with that of another with respect to who we are and what we have. This is a dangerous temptation because it makes us fix our eyes on others. In most cases it makes us envious: envy of the other because they gave him the parish or the mission I wanted; envy because the other was asked to go and preach who knows where and they never send me; envy because the superior always does what the other tells him and never what I tell him; envy because all the books the other writes get published and mine don’t; envy because they send the other someone to help him, and I have been asking for ages and am still waiting… Envy is infectious and can invade the way we think about and judge things, people, situations, and it can greatly influence our behavior. And in this sense it can mean a great diversion from our path. Other times—though perhaps not so common among us, but it certainly can happen and does happen, albeit covertly—it is making the comparison the Pharisee made in the temple: “I thank you, Lord, that I am not like these people: thieves, adulterers, or even like that tax collector”8.

Pay attention: one cannot move forward if one is constantly looking off to the sides.

The last form of distraction is becoming absorbed in momentary consolation. Enjoying something is not forbidden, but drowning in it can certainly divert me from the path. As happened to the rich man Dives in the Gospel of Luke. We know the story. The rich man was so absorbed in the world of his own satisfactions that he did not even notice the presence of Lazarus. He was shut up in himself and so he strayed from the path of compassion.

Another way in which this strategy of diversion manifests itself (among several others we could mention) is straying precisely by remaining conservative—not in the sense of religious orthodoxy, but in the sense of remaining fixed in one’s quirks, in one’s habits, in one’s ways of doing things, which make one averse to everything that is new or that moves us toward a range of new possibilities, and divert us from the mission God has entrusted to us.

The example that comes to mind now is technology, the whole world of social media today. If the pastor has an aversion to technology, preaches all the time about its excesses but never about its good uses and the benefit of using them well, such a man is stuck in the past. I have known some who do not allow their students computers, because they have to learn to write. Fantastic! But tomorrow they also have to go out to work, and if they do not know how to use a computer, what are they going to do? There are others who are professors—for years—and who argue that, because the doctrine of St. Thomas Aquinas has not changed, they keep teaching the same classes in the same way they have been doing for the last 30 years. Don’t even mention to them using a PowerPoint, incorporating some teaching technique… They are excellent and very solid in their doctrine, but they don’t connect, because they are stuck in the past and do not know how to present the material in a way that responds to the sociocultural idiosyncrasy of the students. And that way, what culture are they going to evangelize?

When God says through the mouth of Jeremiah: amend your conduct and your deeds9, it means: “you have to change what you have been doing. You have to go in a new direction.” Obviously this does not apply to everything, but to those things that precisely do not allow me to advance in the direction in which I have to advance. It is also somewhat what Christ says in the parable of the talents regarding the one who buried the only talent he received—why? Because he was afraid. They choose a “safer path” and so they do not advance, because they are attached to their “old securities.” ‘No, it’s that I have this sermon that I preached when I was at the top of the mountain, and it was excellent, it came out really well.” Yes, my dear, but now you are in New York City. Do you follow? The man in the parable does not advance because he opted for the ‘conservative,’ and therefore unproductive (in the sense we have been mentioning). Some act this way because they are comfort-seekers (in which case the temptation does not come from the devil but from ourselves), but many times (and this is indeed from the devil) out of fear.

Another way of ‘being conservative,’ in the pejorative sense it has here, can be seen in St. Paul’s Letter to the Galatians. You will remember when the converts from Judaism insisted to the Galatians, who were converts from paganism, on the necessity of circumcision. Those who came from Judaism clung inflexibly to the past they had inherited, to the rituals, to the laws, and placing their security in those things drew them away from Christ and emptied the faith the Galatians had received. By remaining “fixed” in the past they cannot embrace the new that comes to them through Jesus Christ.

One last way the devil tries to divert us is by means of relativism. The opposite of the previous one. Practical relativism holds that everything is on an equal footing and that nothing matters in particular. There is no stable truth, so there is no solid foundation, and therefore there is no correct or appropriate direction. Watch out! For this can also happen to us, even though we are re-vaccinated against progressivism and relativism. In fact, there has been someone out there who had even written a book about progressivism and ended up a relativist on certain matters of religious life. In a religious context, relativism is like a practice of atheism, or it suggests that if God exists He does not care much how one acts or what decisions one makes. It is what Psalm 10 expresses when it says: the wicked man exclaims in the height of his arrogance: ‘There is no God who will call me to account.’ This is all he thinks10. And a little further on it says: ‘God forgets; He turns away His face and never sees anything’11. And so one who formerly ‘loved’ the Institute and who made his perpetual vows thinks that God forgets him, and even ends up becoming an enemy of the Institute. One who formerly deplored religious who indulged in luxuries himself chooses a diocese where he earns a good salary and is full of human securities. And one wonders: how is it that you changed the whole set of values you used to have? God does not act that way. The truth is stable. In short, the devil finds a way, and one can freely cooperate with him in derailing oneself.

Another example of how this relativism also represents a temptation for us religious appears in something the prophet Isaiah expresses very well. ‘Why do we fast and you do not see it, humble ourselves and you do not acknowledge it?’12. How many times we complain: “but I’m doing everything right! I pray, I do penance, I put my heart into the apostolate, I humble myself and go and speak with so-and-so, and still things go badly for me, still they correct me, still they criticize me…”. And of course, as a practical corollary of these musings comes the thought that no matter what I do, it seems God does not care.

One way to resist this ‘relativism’ is to plant oneself securely in Jesus Christ. God sees it! Do we not say that God is Omniscient? As St. John Newman says: “He knows what he is about.” God knows what He is doing; I am the one who does not know. Clinging to Christ and orienting myself toward Him establishes me in the truth beyond all the ‘variables’ and factors that may influence my life.

To sum up: on this Ascent of the Mount of perfection, as St. John of the Cross himself warns, and as you can see in the traditional drawing made by the saint himself, there is the risk of straying. These are the two paths that veer off to the right or to the left and that definitely do not end in union with God (one because it seeks the goods of heaven and the other because it seeks the goods of earth). The only straight path is that of nothing, nothing, nothing, which seeks only—as we have been saying in these Exercises—the glory and honor of God. Let us realize that walking on the path of the Ascent of the Mount is not the same as having arrived, and therefore I am inevitably going to encounter difficulties and a great many challenges. That is why the words of St. Peter suit us very well as a call to be realistic: Be sober and always alert, because your enemy, the devil, prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour. Resist him, firm in the faith13.

What, then, must we do to “resist firmly,” as St. Peter says, and not stray? First of all, we must exercise ourselves regularly in being vigilant, and the best thing for that is the examination of conscience. We have to examine ourselves, review our life in the light of God’s will, of His Word. And at the same time as we do that, we have to pray to the Holy Spirit to keep us in fruition with the will of the Incarnate Word. We must not take that for granted simply because we are priests. The Letter to the Hebrews expresses this perseverance in our ‘attachment’ to the path undertaken in the company of Jesus Christ, saying: Therefore, since we are surrounded by a true cloud of witnesses, let us strip ourselves of everything that hinders us, especially of sin, which always besets us, and let us run resolutely the contest that lies before us. Let us fix our gaze on the pioneer and perfecter of our faith, on Jesus, who, instead of the joy that was offered Him, endured the Cross, disregarding its shame, and is now seated at the right of the throne of God14. This running resolutely that St. Paul mentions implies great trust, not in ourselves but in God, who began the good work and will bring it to completion15.

A well-known letter published on October 15, 1989 by the then Cardinal Ratzinger under the title “Letter to the Bishops of the Catholic Church on Some Aspects of Christian Meditation”, and the very recent document Placuit Deo: “Letter of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith to the Bishops of the Catholic Church on Certain Aspects of Christian Salvation”, published on the feast of the Chair of St. Peter of this year 2018 (February 22), clearly indicate the insistence of the recent magisterium in warning us about two grave erroneous tendencies of our time stemming from two ancient heresies. These heresies are Pelagianism (or neo-Pelagianism) and Gnosticism. And both have at their root a common error: the denial of the Incarnation of the Word or of the consequences of the Incarnation, which in the end is one and the same thing.

[Pelagianism] Placuit Deo says: “in our times, a kind of neo-Pelagianism is proliferating, for which the individual, radically autonomous, claims to save himself, without recognizing that he depends, in the deepest part of his being, on God and on others. Salvation is then entrusted to the strength of the individual, or to purely human structures, which are incapable of welcoming the newness of the Spirit of God”1. “In this vision, the figure of Christ corresponds more to a model who inspires generous actions with His words and gestures than to the One who transforms the human condition, incorporating us into a new existence reconciled with the Father and with one another through the Spirit”2. Let us recall that St. Thomas already pointed out that ‘by contriving [Pelagius] that we do not need grace for salvation, he made void the coming of the Son of God in the flesh…’, derogating from the dignity of Christ”3.

[Gnosticism] Pope Francis affirms that there is a real and fierce tendency toward “a certain neo-Gnosticism which presents a merely interior salvation, closed up in subjectivism4, which consists in rising ‘with the intellect to the mysteries of the unknown divinity’5. In this way it claims to free the person from the body and from the material cosmos, in which the traces of the provident hand of the Creator are no longer discovered, but only a reality without meaning is seen, foreign to the ultimate identity of the person, and manipulable according to the interests of man6. It proposes a man falsely spiritualized by gnosis, who has nothing to do with man who is a substantial unity of soul and body, and who by grace is elevated to the supernatural order. It has nothing to do with the incarnate God, the God-Man, true God and true man, perfect God and perfect man. In whom both natures are united in the one divine Person of the Word, in a union that is by assumption (the divine assumes the human), and in which both natures are perfect in the unity of the Person, each with its own operation, without mixture or confusion7.

These errors, ancient and new, affect the world, which we are called to evangelize. Placuit Deo says: “Both neo-Pelagian individualism and the neo-Gnostic contempt for the body deform the confession of faith in Christ, the one and universal Savior. How could Christ mediate the Covenant of the whole human family if man were an isolated individual who realizes himself by his own strength, as modern humanism proposes? And how could salvation come through the Incarnation of Jesus, His life, death and resurrection in His true body, if all that matters is to free man’s interiority from the limitations of the body and of matter, according to the new neo-Gnostic vision?”8 In the face of these tendencies, we do not hesitate to affirm that Gnosticism continues to be one of the greatest dangers the Church has faced in all times9. Today as yesterday, the words of Blessed Pope Paul VI in the Consistorial Allocution of May 24, 1976 are most eloquent: “We do not accept the attitude of those who seem to ignore the living tradition of the Church… and interpret in their own way the doctrine of the Church, even the Gospel itself, spiritual realities, the divinity of Christ, His Resurrection or the Eucharist, practically emptying them of their content and thus creating a new gnosis…”10. That is why we do not hesitate to affirm that we are in a Gnostic age. “And this is the battle: against the cerebrations of gnosis, only the reality that the Word became flesh stands opposed”11.

Gnosticism was the first heresy that Christianity had to combat in the first centuries of our era. And although on another occasion I intend to speak more in extenso about Gnosticism, I would like to say that at present, although the sociocultural context has changed and evolved, it must be recognized that the underlying questions remain. Today there is a subtle Gnostic tendency that expresses itself in many forms: the reduction of faith to mysticism and to spiritual wisdom (a mysticism that has nothing to do with the encounter with the transcendent God in the fullness of love, but refers to the experience provoked by turning in upon oneself; an exhilarating feeling of being in communion with the universe; and things of that sort); the displacement of Christocentrism in favor of a theocentrism in the form of mystery, seeking an unconscious reduction to the common denominator of the different universal religious experiences; making the Gospel one wisdom among others; the substitution of faith by religious wisdom; the constant tendency to spiritualize the faith; the surpassing of, and the desire to transcend and free oneself from, all historical, institutional and religious mediations for the sake of a more brilliant spirituality; the Eastern wisdoms of interiority; etc.

In short, this Gnostic tendency seeks to dissociate faith and ethics, presenting a disincarnate experience of God. It must be proclaimed loud and clear that our God reveals Himself in the human, both in interior spiritual experience and in historical experience. Gnostic tendencies give primacy to wisdom or experiential knowledge of God over historical revelation and the incarnation of the Son of God, and even over the ethics of love of neighbor.

Well then, these two heresies that seek to exalt man can be encompassed under the name of a new humanism, a modern humanism, which is atheistic, as we shall see, and which in the end destroys man by separating him from God, for understanding rightly the relationship between the supernatural and the natural, between grace and nature, whose analogatum princeps is precisely the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word. This “bad mixture” of nature and grace proper to this humanism lies at the essence of the modernist errors, as the First Vatican Council teaches so well12. It is a bad mixture of everything: supernatural and natural, theology and philosophy, good and evil, truth and falsehood, yes and no, Church and world, virtue and sin, Church and State, freedom and addiction, integrity and corruption, etc.13.

1. It is a humanism that presents a disfigured Christianity14

Paganism and modern humanism are in some way doing the same thing that the Christian world has always done, but it does so for a different reason. It has preserved the external form of things, but has emptied them of their content and meaning. Christian terms and practices are preserved, and the consecrated words of revealed religion even continue to be used, but they are preserved and used in the same way that a new firm trades under the name of an old company, that is, only to win the goodwill of its former customers. The Christian world, for example, recommends fasting; the modern world fasts too, under the name of “diet”, not to beautify the soul, but to make the body more beautiful. Christianity recommends the examination of conscience: the modern world does the same under the guise of psychoanalysis. The Christian world recommends telling one’s sins to the confessor: the pagan world recommends telling them to the world (think how abundant talk shows are on television). But in the first case the reason for the confession is to elevate the soul through purification; in the second case it is to relax the body through sublimation. Another example of this tendency is to feed ourselves on a movie world, thinking that the crosses of this life can be borne just by repeating a couple of phrases heard on television… thus presenting a pseudo-religiosity, in the manner of fashionable thinking or of the shapers of public opinion, often on a massive scale.

In this way the world is being corrupted not only by bad things but also by good things; one notices such a regimentation of the good things of life that at times it becomes more intolerable than open or violent persecution.

2. Contemporary humanism

The general attitude of doing Christian things for a non-Christian reason is a consequence of what we have called “Contemporary Humanism”, which is as old as gnosis or Pelagianism. Humanism has been defined as “the endeavor to maintain the best spiritual values of religion while surrendering any theological interpretation of the universe”. In its broadest sense it is an endeavor to have Christianity without Christ, divinity without God, and Christian hope without the promise of the next life. It is a sort of disguised atheism. That is why Fr. Cornelio Fabro says that “this positivity of the new atheism is expressed in the ambitious epithet of ‘humanism’ which the atheists of the modern age claim especially from Feuerbach onward”, who goes so far as to affirm, for example: “God is nothing other than humanity”15, and that is why one even speaks of the “drama of atheist humanism”16. “That is the drama of modern humanism: in the name of a supposed exaltation of man, it destroys him”17.

This description is fairly precise, but it does not describe humanism in all its phases. Fulton Sheen says that humanism, like many other “isms”, suffers from a lack of definition. There are some of its minor prophets who make it little better than a vague humanitarianism. Others are on a slightly higher level, like Shakespeare’s Polonius18, who end up making an old test tube (that is, old errors repeated) into a refuge to preserve us from being eaten by “the acids of modernity”. There is also a more classical type of fallacious humanism behind the sentimental effusion of the philosophy of religious experience, which has its source in Rousseau19, and the pragmatic-scientific glorification of the forces of nature, which has its source in Francis Bacon20 and his scientism. Thus, religion is nothing other than a sociology, morality is nothing more than new scientific names that serve to excuse old sins, and ethics is the line of least resistance to evil, under the false colors of self-expression.

Well then, according to Fulton Sheen, there is something common to these forms of humanism, and what they have in common is something very old, in fact fifteen hundred years old: Pelagianism, with its denial of the supernatural and the consequent emancipation of man.

It is curious and interesting that Pelagianism and Humanism began with men who knew Plato21 and the Stoics better than Christianity. Both currents of thought originated in men of the West influenced by ideas from the East. Both movements have emphasized the will and subjectivity at the cost of intelligence and grace. The difference between the two movements lies in their surroundings. Pelagianism appeared in a society in which there was great intellectual light (great thinkers; these were the times of Augustine and Ambrose, among so many others), and therefore its deficiencies became evident very soon. Modern Humanism, on the contrary, appears in a society in which many of the lights have gone out, and therefore it appears wiser than it truly is, among other things because a lantern in the darkness gives more light than a lantern in the sunlight.

Here I want to mention what, in my view, is an enormous obligation and a tremendous responsibility that we have: namely, the pressing need to value our efforts in the intellectual apostolate more and more each day; the importance of knowing, studying, deepening and disseminating in an effective and universal way the works of Fr. Cornelio Fabro, the foremost interpreter of the thought of St. Thomas Aquinas; and particularly of restarting in a solid, concrete and stable way our Center for Higher Studies, so as to succeed in consolidating an authentic school of thought.

Let us look more closely. Pelagianism was a doctrine taught by a student of Greek philosophy, Pelagius22, who held that human nature by its own power is capable of saving itself without the help of God’s grace. Well, that is the central doctrine of Humanism. That is to say, what it does is “to emancipate the inferior from the superior, attacking the right primacy of the spiritual”23. Let us note the parallel between the two. Both deny original sin, but both admit the conflict between matter and spirit as a psychological factor; both deny that it is necessary to have recourse to the grace of Christ, although they admit the beauty of Christ; finally, both appeal to the will of man as sufficient to save himself without the new interior strength of grace, which makes man supernaturally a child of God, thus elevating his created nature. Well then, these observations and descriptions seem at the same time to give us a description of what we know as “Christian progressivism”, which in the end is a Gnostic, Pelagian and immanent movement.

That ancient humanism which bore the name of Pelagianism was finally condemned at the Council of Carthage in May of 418. But soon a new humanistic movement followed in its footsteps: Semi-Pelagianism, elaborated by monks of Marseilles, which admitted, in response to the decrees of Carthage, that man needs grace, but they held that by natural good works man could merit grace. The Semi-Pelagians made concessions to the supernatural that the Pelagians did not make, just as today some contemporary (progressive) humanists make concessions (that is, admit in a certain sense) to the supernatural that others do not make. In its essence, the humanism of today (or the progressivism of today) is nothing other than a revival of Pelagianism; it is the heresy of human action and intellection, the affirmation that humanity can scale divine heights without divine help and that of itself and by itself it is sufficient for the perfection of its capacities and powers. In brief, it is the affirmation that the human mind does not need true and real faith, and that the will of man does not need grace. That is why what is achieved, at best, is a Gnostic and disfigured construction of a religion. A religion that is not one, because it is “a religion my way”.

Modern humanism, because it is Gnostic, has the two fatal defects of Pelagianism, and this is the particular critique that I wish to make of it in this presentation: first, it fails by not taking into account the great failure of humanism in the past, and second, it is too inhuman. Let us look at these two shortcomings separately

3. Atheist humanism and its lack of memory

Humanism fails by not taking into account the sad lesson that humanity learned during the millennia years prior to the Incarnation24, namely, that neither by his human knowledge nor by his human will is man capable of making himself a perfect man, not even in the natural order. The two great peoples into which the world was divided before the Incarnation bear witness to this sad truth: the Jews and the Gentiles.

The Gentiles, principally, have shown later civilizations that man by his own knowledge cannot attain humanistic perfection. The Greeks, masters of philosophy and well trained in what our modern humanists call “decorum”, fell into the grossest errors. Plato, for example, held that women, though so beautifully human, were the property of the soldiers, and Aristotle25, while admitting a supreme first cause, denied a providence that regulated the celestial spheres (the heavenly bodies). Epicurus26 founded a school on pleasure, and Zeno27 built another on the foundations of vanity, the one degrading man to the level of the beasts, the other exalting him to the heights of an intoxicating pride. Protagoras28 taught that man was the measure of all things, as the pragmatists of today do; Pyrrho29 led men to believe that nothing was certain, just as modern skeptics assure us that they are certain that certainty cannot exist.

Not only in the age of Pericles30, but also in the age of Augustus31, not only in the land of the Greeks but also in that of the Romans, human minds testified to the need for another light besides that of reason. In the practical moral order, the wife was the slave of passion; children were exposed at the gates of the city to be devoured by wolves. A slave was crucified because he broke a vase. “Virtue, you are only a name”! said Lucretius32, and he explained the gods away as myths. Cicero33 tells us that in his time philosophy and atheism were synonyms. Seneca34 denied that there was anything beyond the graces. Horace35, as harsh as a Pharisee, did not hesitate to say that death was too good for an impure vestal, but at the same time he urged seizing pleasure on the wing because it is a gift of heaven. In that falsely humanist paganism a merchant could kneel before Mercury36 and ask him for the power to deceive his customers, and a lawyer could beg his favorite goddess that his deceptions and frauds be covered with a cloak of virtue. The light of reason was going out. Men began to transfer the worship of God to their intellectual genius. Then they began to see gods in heaven and on earth, in fire and in air. There followed the deification of nature, and so the sun, the moon and the stars were worshipped as divinities. Finally, in a further plunge, God became simply divinized man, and idolatry was the rule. A profound forgetfulness of God and an indescribable contempt for man are inseparable; the one always begets the other. Amid so much abuse of knowledge, in both the speculative and the moral order, Tertullian37 could say with some truth to the Roman magistrates: “who is there here among you who can say that he has not exposed his own son to death?”

If the Gentiles bore witness to the inability of human knowledge to satisfy the demands of perfect Humanism, it is the Jew who has borne witness to the inability of human power to produce a perfect human being without divine grace. From the time of Adam until Moses, those who were destined to be the chosen people learned the same lesson as the Gentiles: the insufficiency of purely humanistic wisdom. Then God gave them knowledge, a knowledge not like that of the nations, but of a higher kind, brought down from heaven. Amid the lightning and thunder of Sinai, God gave Moses and his people a new knowledge, a more perfect moral code, the knowledge of the Law, the 10 commandments. God was saying to them: “Your human wisdom is insufficient. Now I will give you knowledge”. But it remained to be seen whether with this knowledge they would have the power and the will to put this law into practice.

And behold, even while Moses was receiving the law, his people had rebelled against his brother, saying to him: “make us gods to go before us”. The commandments of God were forgotten. Korah, Dathan and Abiram38 denied the special prerogatives of the priesthood of Aaron and the divine mission of Moses, and named a priest without the divine call or anointing. David, some time later, being rich and lacking nothing for his material happiness, fell into adultery and became guilty of the murder of Uriah, with whose wife he had committed a shameful sin. Of the 20 kings who ruled over Judea, unfortunately the majority served idols; two of them even offered their own sons in sacrifice to Moloch39; Ahaz40 closed the temple to worshippers, and Manasseh41 set up altars to false gods in the courts.

Even with the knowledge of what was right and what was wrong, they became “transgressors from the womb”, seeking “consolation in idols under every green tree and sacrificing their children in the torrents”.

Forty centuries of humanist experiment had passed, and humanity had learned the lesson of the insufficiency of human knowledge and power for salvation. Help was needed; humanism was not enough. Cries multiplied toward God, not only from the Jews, who asked the heavens to make a Savior spring forth, but also from the Greeks, who in their great tragedies pleaded for a redeemer, for example, in the words of Aeschylus42: seek no other end to this curse unless some god appears to take your pains upon his own head in your place. This meaninglessness of the pagan world, of this pagan humanism, is very well described by the brilliant G. K. Chesterton in his work “The Everlasting Man”.

In response to the longings of humanist hearts, there appeared from heaven Christ, the Son of God, “the Power and the wisdom of God”, the power that the Jews were seeking and the knowledge that the Gentiles longed for. But some insisted that Humanism was sufficient, and of these it has been written: He came to His own and His own did not receive Him43. To the Jews He was a stone of stumbling44, because Power came in the weakness of the cross; to the Gentiles He was foolishness, because Knowledge came in the form of one who “never learned” and taught a wisdom that was folly to the world. But to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ is the Power of God and the Wisdom of God – because the foolishness of God is wiser than men and the weakness of God is stronger than men45.

Our modern humanists, immanentist and Gnostic, who ask us to reject the superhuman Christ, either because eternal life is not necessary for them or because faith in Him is reduced to “imagination”, are asking us to flee in the face of the 40 centuries of experience of pre-Christian history. They would have us relive the worldwide experiment that ended in the Incarnation and believe that man should try once more to go forward with the sufficiency of human power without grace, and act as if the incarnate God meant nothing to man. To ask us to do this is to commit the typical sin of Western civilization, the sin of pride, of self-sufficiency.

The Eastern world fell into the excess of believing that God does everything and man does nothing; this is Eastern mysticism46 and quietism47. Our Western world has fallen into the other excess of believing that man does everything and God does nothing; this is Humanism. The true position is the one that stands in the middle. Man can do something with the help of God’s grace that sustains him. The Eastern world needs to learn from St. Paul, who said: I can do all things in Him who strengthens me48, and the Western Humanists need to learn the words of our Lord: Without me you can do nothing49.

5. Modern humanism is inhuman

This brings us to the second critique that I would like to make today of contemporary thought: this modern humanism (which in reality is ancient) is too inhuman. In the end it places a very heavy weight on poor human nature. Human nature, by virtue of an immortal soul, has something infinite in it; it has infinite aspirations and desires for truth, for beauty, for love and for life; it refuses to be pacified by the pleasures of time and space, and is always eager to climb up to the “hidden battlements of eternity”, where there is nothing but the Infinite Perfection of the Life of God.

The humanist will admit the infinity of these aspirations, and therein lies his fallacy. To ask man to satisfy that passive capacity for the infinite with a finite charm; to drink the waters of time to quench his thirst for eternity; to feed on corruptible food to satisfy his hunger for the Bread of Eternal Life, and to rest in the human when he longs for the divine—this is to thwart human nature in everything that makes it human. This is not human, even though it is called humanism.

It is a strange paradox, yet a true one, that man only becomes more human when he becomes more divine, because he has been destined from all eternity to be conformed to the image of the Son of God. Therefore, any form of humanism that denies the need for grace and attempts to perfect man without it is asking man to grow without an environment in which to grow. To remain on the level of the purely human, and to uphold the ideal of “decorum”, is to allow man to expand horizontally, in the direction of the human, but not vertically, in the direction of the divine (in which we can recognize another definition of modern progressivism). Humanism advocates the expansion of man on the plane of nature, but not so that his being may be elevated to the plane of grace. And yet elevation is far more important than expansion. If it denies the order of grace and the kingdom of the fatherhood of God, what environment is left to humanity in which to grow, except poor and weak humanity itself? Plants live thanks to the environment that is outside of themselves, an environment with which their structure is in harmony. Since the soul is spiritual, man needs the environment not only of humanity, which belongs to the realm of his body, but the environment of the spirit to which his soul belongs. And it is only by coming into harmony with that higher environment that he attains the end for which he was created. That is why Humanism without the transcendent is not humanism but Naturalism. Man by nature is not an idol but a worshipper, and to turn him back upon himself is to condemn him to selfishness, which is his death.

* * * * *

Fulton Sheen says that there is a great difference between the Church and modern Humanism. The Church invokes a Person; the humanists, a philosophy. The Church says that Humanism consists in reflecting in our own lives the eternal image of the Person of Christ; the humanists say that Humanism consists in reflecting in our lives the abstractions of Eastern mysticism or those of Stoic philosophy50.

Modern Humanism forgets that life is not only a push from below, but also a gift from above, and that man, in his noble humanist efforts, has been met halfway along the upward path by the Person of Christ, who came to earth to take not only man’s hand but his very nature, to divinize it, to lift it up to intimacy with God, to call God “Father”, and to make of this the source and model of a continued incarnation, in which we will be better men because we will be other christs.

At bottom, the whole humanist error (which, once again, is Pelagian, Gnostic and progressive) consists in a misunderstanding, which turns into a denial, of the august mystery of the Incarnation.

In a message to the Dominican Order, the great St. John Paul II said51: “We live in a time characterized, in its own way, by the rejection of the Incarnation. For the first time since the birth of Christ two thousand years ago, it is as if He no longer found a place in an increasingly secularized world. Christ is not always denied explicitly; many even say that they admire Jesus and value some elements of His teaching. But He remains distant: in reality He is not known, loved and obeyed, but relegated to a remote past or to a distant heaven”. And then he listed some consequences of this denial of the Incarnation, which ends up being against man: “Our age denies the Incarnation in many practical ways, and the consequences of this denial are clear and disturbing”52.

  1. «In the first place, the individual relationship with God is regarded as exclusively personal and private, so that God is removed from the processes by which social, political and economic activity is governed». Forms of denial of the Incarnation in these fields are savage liberalism, Marxism, technocracy, and the secularism that seeks to set God aside. The world that man builds without God turns against man. When one does not want God to reign, man becomes enslaved.
  1. «In turn, this leads to a notable diminishing of the sense of human possibilities, since Christ is the only one who fully reveals the magnificent possibilities of human life, the only one who ‘fully reveals man to man himself’53». When the sense of human possibilities is diminished, one falls into marginalization and social exclusion, into the plague of unemployment, into the exploitation of workers, into the accumulation of wealth in the hands of a few while the poor become ever poorer and ever more numerous and have ever less share in the common wealth. Healthy creativity is lost.
  1. «When Christ is excluded or denied, our vision of the meaning of human existence is reduced; and when we hope for and aspire to something lesser, hope gives way to despair, and joy to depression». That is to say, reductionism in human existence leads man to enslave himself to things inferior to him, contrary to what St. Paul teaches, that we must not be subject to the elements of the world54, which, at bottom, is clearly manifested in all the known forms of addiction: to alcohol, to drugs, to excessive haste, to money, to sex without responsibility, to power, to the internet, to irrational violence, to television, to the cell phone, to speed, to noise, etc. One falls into existential meaninglessness and into the loss of the Christian’s lordship. That is why humanity in the modern world looks like a flock of sheep in which everyone thinks, more or less, the same thing, which is what the media repeat daily, ad nauseam, under the dictatorship of the ‘givers of meaning’.
  1. «There also arises a profound distrust of reason and of the human capacity to grasp the truth; even the very concept of truth is called into question. Faith and reason, impoverishing each other, become separated, degenerating respectively into fideism and rationalism55». Man becomes a slave of his subjective whim and of the dictatorship of relativism. In the end, liberally, everyone is right. Everything is the same. Man is not interested in the truth. He is not interested in extramental reality and in what is outside him, above him and superior to him: «I make the truth, for I am the measure of all things».
  1. «Life is no longer appreciated or loved; that is why a certain culture of death advances, with its bitter fruits: abortion and euthanasia». And in contraception and anti-natalism, in cloning, in divorce, in widespread suicide, in the killing of the innocent, in pederasty, in the death of the soul through not having recourse to the grace given by the sacraments. And so men and women live like animals without sacraments: they do not go to confession, they do not receive Communion, they do not go to Mass on Sundays, they do not receive the anointing of the sick, they do not marry in the Church…. Anything is done in order to destroy the divine image of marriage and the family, and the dignity of human work.
  1. «The body and human sexuality are not rightly valued or loved; from this derives the degradation of sex, which manifests itself in a wave of moral confusion, infidelity and pornographic violence». Men and women are pushed toward every form of pathological sexual excess: pederasty, transvestism, sadism, masochism… pornography that invades everything: magazines, newspapers, cinema, radio, television, the internet… Virginity is not loved and purity is vilified. Humanity is suffering a true eclipse of ethics and morality.
  1. «Not even creation itself is loved and valued; that is why the specter of destructive selfishness is perceived in the abuse and exploitation of the environment». Because creation is the great page written by God, and there is also a desire to destroy the image of God inscribed in it.

I consider that with this description by the Pope it is clearly seen that this false humanism, which claims to promote man while separating him from God and denying the redemptive Incarnation, is completely anti-human. It shuts man up within himself, in a Gnostic manner, and leaves him to his own strength alone, in a Pelagian manner.

The Great Pope concluded: «In this situation, the Church and the Successor of the apostle Peter look to the Order of Preachers with the same hope and confidence as in the time of its foundation. The needs of the new evangelization are enormous. Certainly, your Order, with its numerous vocations and its extraordinary heritage, can play a fundamental role in the mission of the Church to put an end to ancient errors and to proclaim effectively the message of Christ at the dawn of the new millennium».

Well then, we members of the religious family of the Incarnate Word must also be in the front line and in the first person to fulfill our mission. That is why I insist once again on the pressing need we have to prioritize our intellectual apostolate, to create a true school of thought based on the philosophy of being, in which man may find his true foundation, which is being, and, moreover, find his ultimate end, which is Being by Essence, and his depth, which is freedom56.

That is why, and with this I am now drawing to a close, we are asked in our Proper Law “not to content ourselves with a superficial knowledge of philosophy and theology, incapable of understanding in all its depth the drama of contemporary atheism and therefore incapable of remedying it”57. It falls to us, by the mission we have received, to have a strong cultural formation that takes a stand before modern culture58. What is needed is a “metaphysics with grip”59, one that bites into reality. This is essential to our charism.

The present age urgently needs Jesus Christ. The needs of the new evangelization are enormous, and we cannot evade the challenging call that Christ makes to us to go into the whole world proclaiming the Gospel.

Let us always remember it: Our pastoral work must tirelessly propose Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word, the fullness of all authentically human life and culture60. “A task which—says our Proper Law—is not only for specialists but for everyone”61, and for which there is need—I repeat—of a solid philosophical formation, since in pastoral life we must all face the demands of the contemporary world and examine the causes of certain behaviors in order to give them an adequate response.

I conclude by quoting once more our dear St. John Paul II: “The basis for building a true civilization is to collaborate with all our strength so that ‘an authentic sense of man may prevail in the world, not closed in a narrow anthropocentrism, but open toward God’”62. This basis is the Rock63 itself, that is, the very mystery of Jesus Christ the Incarnate Word. And He is the model from which our spirituality and our pastoral work are rooted and derived64. That is why what is ours, today and always, will be to bear witness to Jesus Christ, that is, to bear witness that the Word became flesh.

A.M.D.G.

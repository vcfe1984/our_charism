Sermon belonging to the series of sermons on Our Lady of Guadalupe preached for the first time at the priests’ gathering of the Province “Madonna di Loreto,” in Val d’Aosta, from August 20 to 30, 2006

We can now describe the image of Our Lady of Guadalupe as it is imprinted on the tilma of St. Juan Diego, highlighting only some of the most beautiful or striking aspects it contains.

She is clothed with the sun, like the woman of the Apocalypse (12:1); like her, she also has the moon under her feet, and a little angel beneath her as well, who holds in his hands the tunic and the mantle of the Virgin.

Let us highlight some important elements of this image, which has been studied so very much over the centuries.

1. Christ

Our Lord is indicated in the image of the Guadalupana. On the womb of Most Holy Mary there is a four-petaled jasmine (Philadelphus mexicanus), a stylization of the center of the sun stone, which in the Nahuatl culture symbolizes fullness (the nahui ollin). The Virgin is with child, and holds the Son of God in her womb. We do not grasp it because we are not indigenous, but for them the message was very clear.

That is also why the area of Mary’s Womb is the most illuminated of the whole image, more so than Mary’s face itself.

2. The Virgin

From the first moment the face reflects the attitude of the Virgin, which is one of deep prayer: the lowered gaze, the seriousness, and the drooping of the eyelids, the very sweetness and peace she radiates.

The shape of the face is oval, and the complexion dark. It is a mestizo face. The Virgin, who is an evangelizer, wants to win over the indigenous people, who were extremely harsh. They were terribly bloodthirsty. If at sunset they saw that the sky was red, they understood that they had to offer blood to the sun god, and they carried out veritable hecatombs of children from the subjugated tribes. More or less as is done today with abortion, with the difference that now they are offered to the god of sex. There was very great corruption: homosexuality, incest, cannibalism, all mixed with great cruelty. That is why evangelization was not advancing. The Franciscans themselves could not find the vein, a vein that opened only with the apparition of the Virgin. And she even made up for the great poverty of means the missionaries suffered. They did not even have oils for the chrismations (later the pettifoggers of the day came along and said that all the baptisms they had performed had been invalid; thank goodness the Holy See sided with the Franciscans). They were so poor that later the rumor spread that they had changed the color of their habit and made it blue, whereas in reality it was because they had no other habit nor brown dye, and with successive washings it kept fading and degenerating into an old bluish color.

Returning to the face of the image. As we have also pointed out, the skin tone varies in each photograph. In the description of the Nican Mopohua it is said to be “tobacco-colored, or of dark corn.” Others have described it as “just slightly darker than pearl” (Miguel Cabrera, 18th c.) and, in our day, as “olive.”

Her eyebrows are very fine and well defined, more similar to European features; her eyelids are full and the eyes almond-shaped. The nose is fine and straight in form, not of indigenous characteristics.

Her mouth is located right over a defect (a tear) in the tilma, and yet it looks delicate and anatomically perfect, sketching moreover a sweet smile. Beneath the graceful chin a long, strong neck can be glimpsed.

Her hair, parted down the middle, is dark mahogany in color.

The face of the Virgin is fresh, young, tender, clean and with well-defined features. Feminine qualities of gentleness and firmness can be observed.

Not for nothing did they describe her in Nahuatl as “she who is perfectly looking in every direction.”

The hands joined in prayer have a beautiful particularity that the Virgin willed: the left hand is an indigenous hand, dark, plump; while the right is Spanish, slender and white. The scholars we have already cited (Callahan and Smith) say that the pigments with which the hands are colored, like those of the face, are unknown and inexplicable.

Her garments are full of details charged with symbolism, of clear meaning for the indigenous people.

The color of the tunic is an indefinite ochre, ranging from pale pink, almost white, to dark shades of the same color.

The golden stars of the mantle (46 in all) represent the constellations visible in Mexico in December, the month of the apparitions.

The flowers that appear on the dress and on the mantle, and which, in the manner of indigenous painting, do not follow the folds of the garment (as if they were on a flat surface), are all from the area of the apparition.

The belt is in a high position, indicating that she is awaiting the birth of the Savior.

In addition, there are some details that enhance the delicacy of the Image represented and the rank of “Lady” of the Virgin, such as the golden brooch at her neck (with a black cross inside it), what appear to be two golden bracelets, and in her hair, what could be a comb or an accessory to hold the mantle.

3. The angel

Besides the moon, the Virgin has an angel as the throne of her sacred feet. Just as Jesus Christ, Son of God, and the most perfect creature, who is Mary, are present, so the angelic order is represented in the image.

At first sight the colorful wings catch the eye; they follow the anatomical shape of the wings of the eagle, but their colors follow those of another bird of Mexico, called the tzindzcan.

The angel has a reddish tunic similar to the shirt the Indians used in the early days of evangelization, which replaced the loincloth.

He has a brooch on which, according to experts, some letters can be made out: JU, at that time the abbreviation of “Juan”; and a D with a drawing that looks like a little pot, which according to studies could well be the abbreviation of Diego, “Do.”

The angel, moreover, has his hair cut in the style of the “macehuales,” or common men among the indigenous people, the class to which Juan Diego belonged.

4. The Indians, her children

The image is full of references to the culture and customs of the Indians, healed and elevated by the Gospel. This can be seen in the very presence of the angel with its probable reference to Juan Diego (or with the angel almost identifying himself with him), and also in the fact that he is grasping and, as it were, covering himself with the mantle of the Virgin.

The image is an icon of great transparency and of unsurpassable catechetical value for the evangelization of the indigenous Mexicans. They saw themselves present there in every symbol, in every detail, in the very features of the Virgin. They felt identified. But we must not forget, and the Indians understood it so, that there are also the details that refer to the great Spanish (Greco-Roman) civilization through which the Gospel reached them. Thus the Indians learned to grasp the Virgin as Lady, both of themselves and of the Spaniards, and they knew her to be their Mother. And even today it is so for all the peoples of Spanish America.

It is a prototype of the evangelization of culture. We who have as the end of our Religious Family the evangelization of culture must learn from her, and learn from those great men who were the Franciscans of all times, who did those stupendous works, among which is the evangelization of America. Of the 250,000 missionaries who went to America over five centuries, most of whom were sent by Spain, 70 percent were Franciscans, that is, 180,000 missionaries. Even today Spain has more than 23,000 missionaries in America.

And they, the Franciscans, have great glories. One of them is the evangelization of America. Another of them, perhaps even greater, is that they always defended and spread devotion to the Immaculate Conception, until it was finally declared a dogma of faith. And the other great thing about the Franciscans, which must be remembered, is the Custody of the Holy Land, entrusted to them by the kings of Spain, Spain being the one that paid all the costs and expenses it entailed. The Custody of the Holy Land at that time covered Cyprus, Turkey, Egypt, the Holy Land, Jordan, Iraq, Iran, and reached as far as China, where Fray John of Montecorvino, a Franciscan, arrived among the first and established himself there for more than 30 years, in what was at that time called Cathay, today Beijing.

That is why, looking at the Virgin, we must kindle in our hearts enthusiasm for evangelization, which is indeed the new evangelization, but which is the evangelization of always, and which needs well-disposed men, with light in their heads, with enthusiasm in their hearts and with great courage so as not to lose heart.

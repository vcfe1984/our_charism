“If we seriously consider the moment in which we live, we must admit that the situation for us Christians in Germany is much more uncomfortable and confusing than it was for the first Christians during the bloodiest persecutions. Many people will perhaps think, ‘Why does God make us live in a time like this?’ We cannot blame God for this, nor lay the blame on other people […] [However] we must not consider the suffering of this world as the worst thing that can happen to us: even the great saints had to suffer terrible things until God took them to His heavenly mansions; nor did the Lord spare His Apostles great sufferings, and in spite of this they did a great work for Christ and most of them died as martyrs. And perhaps we—in spite of our sinful life—would like to have a life free of pain and struggle and a peaceful death, and then enjoy eternal beatitude as a reward. Christ Himself, the most Immaculate, suffered more terribly than all other men and purchased heaven for us by His suffering and His death—and are we not willing to suffer for Him?”1.

These words we have just quoted were written by Blessed Franz Jägerstätter, martyr—a layman, husband and father of a family—who at the age of 36, having persevered firmly and courageously in his decision not to fight for the Nazi army in Germany, and after enduring prisons and very great mistreatment, endless humiliations and cruel abuse, died beheaded for his convictions of faith, and his body was then cremated.

Before being arrested for refusing to take the oath of loyalty to Hitler, mandatory for every recruit, many—among relatives and friends—insisted that he not resist, that he think of his family, to which he answered again and again: “If I must write with my hands in chains, I would find that much better than if my will were in chains. Neither prison, nor chains, nor a death sentence can rob a man of his faith and his free will. God gives one so much strength that it is possible to bear any suffering… People worry about obligations of conscience with regard to my wife and children. But I cannot believe that just because he has a wife and children, a man is free to offend God… Did not Christ say that ‘whoever loves his wife, mother and children more than me is not worthy of me’?”2.

“Everyone tells me, of course, that I should not do what I am doing because of the danger of death. I believe it is better to sacrifice one’s life immediately than to put oneself in grave danger of committing sin and then die”3.

To his wife Franziska Schwanniger he wrote from prison: “You must not be sad about my present situation… As long as a man has a clear conscience and knows that he is not really a criminal, he can live in peace, even in prison”4.

On the night of August 8, 1943, they opened Franz’s cell and handed him a piece of paper, telling him: “write your last letter.” He addressed it to his wife and daughters: “They brought me to the prison of Brandenburg together with several others condemned to death… We did not yet know what would happen to us. Not until noon did they tell me that my sentence had been confirmed on July 14 and would be carried out today (August 9, 1943) at 4:00 p.m.… Dear wife and mother, I thank you once again from my heart for everything you have done for me in life, for all the sacrifices you have made for me. I beg you to forgive me if I have hurt or offended you, as I forgive you everything… May God accept my sacrifice in reparation not only for my sins but for the sins of all. It will not be possible for me to free you from the pain you must now suffer on my account. How hard it must have been for our dear Savior to know that, by His sufferings and death, He would cause great suffering to His Mother; and They suffered all that out of love for us sinners.

[…] I thank God that I have the privilege of suffering and even of dying for Him. I trust that God, in His infinite Mercy, has forgiven me everything and will not abandon me in my last hour… Keep the commandments and, with the grace of God, we will soon see each other again in heaven. The heart of Jesus, the heart of Mary and my heart are one in time and in eternity”5.

The witness of this father of a family is undoubtedly challenging for every Christian, but even more so for us members of the Institute, who say that we want “to be victims with the Victim”6 and who “ask to be brought by His Passion and cross to the glory of the Resurrection”7.

Nevertheless, it may happen to us that when the cross of persecution rises up on our path, we are shaken by turmoil, we become discouraged, and instead of promptly rejecting everything that implies not only infidelity but even ambiguity in our choices of faith or lukewarmness in love, we retreat or flee like the Apostles on that first Good Friday.

It is good, therefore, to reflect on what we consider “the greatest grace that God can grant to our tiny Religious Family, which is persecution, especially that which reaches martyrdom”8, so that God may not come “to reprove us for not having been willing to carry the cross of Christ with patience”9.

1. The grace of persecution

“‘Blessed are you when all men speak well of you, when you are popular and the center of attention’; that is the beatitude according to the world”10, Ven. Fulton Sheen affirmed.

However, the Incarnate Word taught something very different: Blessed will you be when they insult you, when they persecute you, when they falsely say every evil against you on my account. Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you11.

This Beatitude is truly the beatitude of the blessing of being persecuted, or the happiness of suffering for Christ even to the point of martyrdom.

When our Lord spoke about the world, He was not referring to the physical world but to the spirit of the world, which was firmly positioned against Him and His followers; to a world that would one day kill His servants and think it was offering a service to God; to a world made up of men, organizations, systems, etc., which, under the influence of him who is “the prince of this world”12, are organized against the Divinity.

That is why it is interesting to note that our Proper Law asks for this great grace of persecution immediately after saying that our lordship with respect to the world is lived in two ways: one, by collaborating with the world of creation through work and with the world of the Redemption through the apostolate; but secondly—and this is what we want to emphasize—“by rejecting the world, whether out of loyalty to the world itself, which must be held as a means and not as an end, or out of loyalty to God, resisting the concupiscences, temptations and sins of the world; being independent in the face of the maxims, mockery and persecutions of the world, depending only on our upright conscience enlightened by faith; ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God, which constitutes the full and total rejection of the evil world”13.

“The spirit of the world,” Fulton Sheen affirmed, “is opposed to trial […]. God, on the contrary, is in favor of trials. Even at the beginning, Adam and Eve were not confirmed in their gifts until they passed the test of whether they preferred the fruit or the garden of Eden. The angels were tested […]. The forty years in the desert are called in Sacred Scripture a time of trial. Our Lord began His public life by undergoing the trial with Satan. In our time, the Church in Germany was tested by Nazism; the Church in Russia was tested by communism, and the Church elsewhere is tested by worldliness. What makes this trial so hard is that we are offered to go toward secularism without becoming secular, and to concern ourselves with the things of this world—ecclesiastical or not—without becoming worldly. In the days of [explicit and bloody] persecution the sides were well identified. The Church is ‘here,’ the persecutors are ‘there.’ But to draw that fine line of being in the Church ‘as a sign of salvation in the world’ and not abandoning the Church is a real trial”14.

“If the Church,” or a clerical Institute like ours, “is not a disturbing element, a leaven in the world, a storm center for sin, a ball of fire for its comforts, it is no longer the Body of Christ. The more loyal the Church is to the victimhood of Christ, the more hostility in the world its priests and its people will provoke, and, therefore, the greater the tribulation will be”15.

That is why Fulton Sheen will say in one of his books: “to be tolerated [or accepted] is sometimes a sign of weakness; to be persecuted is a compliment. The mediocre survive. The persecuted person shows that his belief is taken seriously and that the cause he defends must be eliminated if evil is to win. It is true that evil men are persecuted, but these do not enter into this Beatitude, because, as St. Paul said: If I were to hand over my body to the flames but did not have in my heart love of God and neighbor, it would profit me nothing. A martyr must die for the faith, not for his possessions, nor for his good name, nor for the good of the Party. Self-made martyrs are numerous, but they have no place in the ranks of those to whom the Kingdom of Heaven is promised for carrying the cross of Christ on their shoulders”16.

We, religious of the Incarnate Word, have been called “to be in the world17, ‘without being of the world’18; to go into the world to convert it and not to blend in with it19.

The world and the prince of this world will always wage war against those who fervently seek to cling to Christ. That is why persecution should not astonish us; rather, we must accept it as part of the program. Christ did not deceive us! Did He not warn us of it in the Gospel? They will seize you; they will persecute you, they will hand you over to the synagogues and to prisons, they will bring you before kings and governors because of my name… You will be handed over even by parents and brothers, and relatives and friends; and they will put some of you to death, and you will be hated by all because of my name20. For the disciple is not better than his teacher, nor the servant better than his master… If they called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more those of his household?21. Then many will be scandalized, and they will betray one another and hate one another22.

But that same Christ who with paternal care warned us about tribulation, with the same love also encourages us to courage in the struggle, telling us: Do not fear them23. For not a hair of your head will be lost. By your perseverance you will save your souls24. Truly, truly, I say to you, you will weep, while the world will rejoice. You will be sorrowful, but your sorrow will be turned into joy25. He who perseveres to the end will be saved26.

That is why we must realize that persecution itself, when it is unjust or based on lies, is in some way an indication that we are on the right track: “They will be carried from here to there, people will laugh at them and consider them clumsy, backward and even feeble-minded […] [However] no wisdom of the world will ever be able to deceive them. When the world says of us: Look at the madmen! stones are thrown at them and they kiss the hand that throws them; people laugh at them and mock them, and they laugh too, like children who do not understand; they are beaten, persecuted and martyred, but they thank God who found them worthy. When the world says that, it is a sign that we are on the right track”27. To the point that we might say that the greater our configuration to Christ, the greater the ‘discomfort’ the world will experience with us and, therefore, the greater the setbacks and attacks will be. Simply because the disciple is not above his Master, nor the servant above his master28.

That is why the grace of persecution is the greatest grace, and even more so if it is martyrdom, because it allows us to imitate more perfectly Christ slandered, suffering, despised and condemned to death. For we are convinced that after this momentary and light tribulation an incalculable weight of glory awaits us29. And because, moreover, we are given the occasion to complete in our flesh what is lacking in the passion of Christ on behalf of His body, which is the Church30.

The Blessed martyr whom we quoted at the beginning, and so many others whom we could quote, bear witness to us by their examples of that wisdom of the cross which made them set everything aside, even their very life, so as to put nothing before Christ31. For that is what it is about. Is the Kingdom of God of so little value that it does not deserve some sacrifice, so that we put every trifle of this world before eternal treasures? Let everyone know that our struggle is for the eternal kingdom.

“If I did not have faith in the mercy of God, that God will forgive me all my sins, I could hardly have endured life in solitary confinement so calmly. What is more, even if people accuse me of a crime and I am condemned to death as a criminal, it comforts me to know that not everything the world considers a crime is a crime in the eyes of God. I have the hope that I need not fear the eternal Judge… If a person possessed all the wisdom of this world and could claim half of this world as his own, even so he could not and will not be happy like one of those who can claim practically nothing as their own in this world except their faith. I would not exchange my solitary cell for the most magnificent royal palace. No matter how grand or how beautiful it might be, I would set it aside, because the word of God remains forever…,” wrote Blessed Franz Jägerstätter32.

Definitively, the grace of persecution, and especially that of martyrdom, is the greatest grace that God can grant us, because it allows us to love God purely and freely, without self-interest, as He loves us; for, as the Mystic of Fontiveros teaches, “the lover cannot be satisfied if he does not feel that he loves as much as he is loved”33 and “until it reaches this, the soul is not content, nor would it be so in the other life if it did not feel that it loves God as much as it is loved by Him”34; since “love alone is what unites and joins the soul with God”35. For in this consists perfection: “in perfect love of God and contempt of self”36. And what is said here individually of a soul may well be applied to the Institute as one single body.

That is why it is fitting, and very much so, to educate our candidates so that they “understand with all clarity… that their destiny is not command or honors [so prized in this world], but total self-giving to the service of God and to pastoral ministry. With singular care let them be trained in priestly obedience, in a poor manner of life and in the spirit of self-denial, so that they may become accustomed to renouncing promptly those things which, although lawful, are not fitting, and to becoming like Christ crucified”37. That is, religious, priests, with the spirit of a prince38, who “are those who know at every moment the things for which one must die”39, and who walk in the footsteps of the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep40. Today more than ever there is need of religious, priests and contemplatives of the Incarnate Word with a disposition for martyrdom.

No less is expected of us: “one must be ready even for martyrdom, if necessary, knowing how to maintain an unshakable firmness in order to be faithful to God”41. And one must live permanently in that attitude, without diminutions or retractions, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without withdrawals or sluggishness42.

2. Counsels for times of persecution

Nevertheless, it is a reality of our fallen nature that in the face of persecution, and even in the face of martyrdom, one’s strength diminishes and one looks for some less uncomfortable option, tries to negotiate something easier or less painful, or simply wants to run away. That is why it is fitting to prepare oneself.

And although, speaking individually, the title of “persecution” may perhaps be too big for us, we must not think that it is something distant, much less foreign to us, for it may well happen that one day God gives us the grace of martyrdom. Why? Because God does what He wills; God is the one who chooses, and He chooses and destines His great graces for whom He wills, where He wills, when He wills and as He wills, because He is God. Consequently, and in spite of original sin and personal sins, through the mercy of the heavenly Father—the same who gave us His Son and made us His children—we can become heirs of His glory and of His kingdom through martyrdom.

Now, the martyrs of all times—with some exceptions—did not become martyrs from one day to the next; rather, martyrdom (besides certainly being a grace of God) came to be like the natural consequence of the spirit of renunciation in which they lived and the crowning of that immense love for Christ which they forged throughout their whole life. In the case of persecution, while it is true that in some cases it was bloody, for the immense majority of thoroughgoing Christians—many of them today members of the triumphant Church of Christ—it was part of daily life and of their path of sanctification, for they suffered defamation, infamous trials, unjust condemnations, lacerating abandonment, slander… and the list could go on endlessly.

Merely by way of illustration we mention here a few examples43:

In the third century: St. Athanasius was taken for a sorcerer.

In the fourth century: St. Basil was falsely accused of heresy before Pope St. Damasus. St. John Chrysostom was slanderously accused of being unchaste.

In the fifth century: St. Cyril of Alexandria was condemned as a heretic by a conventicle of forty bishops and at the same time deprived of his bishopric.

In the tenth century: St. Romuald, who at the time was more than 100 years old, was accused of an enormous crime, such that there were those who said he deserved to be burned alive, he being completely innocent.

In the sixteenth century: St. John of Ávila suffered persecution; he preached with great zeal and fruit for souls, but some impenitent ones, frenzied patients against their good physician, accused him before the tribunal of the Inquisition of Seville. Likewise St. John of the Cross, among many other false accusations and persecutions he suffered in his life, had to endure the one in which they said he had kissed a nun44, which was absolutely false.

In the seventeenth century, slanders were raised against St. Francis de Sales, accusing him of having illicit relations with a lady.

That is to say, the cross of persecution makes no distinctions among bishops, religious, founders, monks, missionaries or simple Christians. It is also very interesting, consoling and fruitful to observe how, in so many cases, the difficulties came from those from whom one would not have expected them, such as, for example, the religious and ecclesiastical authorities of the various periods. So it was in the time of Our Lord, so it has been throughout the ages (even Blessed Jägerstätter suffered the incomprehension of prelates), and so it is in our own days; it is the mystery of the “persecution from good people,” which deserves to be profoundly assimilated.

Therefore, analogously to the way an athlete prepares himself and places himself under the direction of a coach in order to perform better and better and win his race, we must be well aware of these graces and know how to prepare ourselves, train ourselves, “adjust our mind” and, in short, fight the good fight45 until we finish the race, so that we may then say with St. Paul: I have kept the faith46.

Let everyone understand that in this, not to fight is already to have lost. “That is why, just as a man who thinks only of this world does everything possible to make his life here easier and better, we who believe in the Kingdom of Heaven must risk everything in order to receive our reward there”47, said Blessed Franz. Blessed is that servant whom his master, on coming, finds doing so!48.

So then, just as the first Christians who were preparing for martyrdom encouraged one another before the moment of death or of facing the great tribunals with all their might, it has seemed to us that it may be of great fruit for all the members of the Institute, called to have “the soul ready to accept death, if necessary, for the good of the Institute in the service of Jesus Christ”49, to bring here certain counsels, maxims and ‘recipes’ of the saints, who with heroic faith and unshaken patience endured every kind of abuse and even death itself out of love for the Incarnate Word.

To want this Christ to cost us something

First things first; that is why St. John of the Cross recommended to the Carmelite nuns: “to take up anew the path of perfection in all humility and detachment, inward and outward, not with a childish spirit but with a robust will; let them pursue mortification and penance, wanting this Christ to cost them something, and not being like those who seek their own comfort and consolation, whether in God or outside of Him, but rather suffering in God, and outside of Him for His sake, in silence and hope and loving remembrance”50. And “when some unpleasantness or displeasure comes your way, remember Christ crucified, and be silent”51.

The Mystical Doctor also uses in his works the example of Job as the best typical figure of passive purification and of the dramatic experience the soul goes through in the night. Job knows the cathartic action in his whole being: from physical trial—nakedness, abandonment by friends, loss of possessions—to the heartrending cry of the afflicted and humiliated soul that “roars and bellows”52, as usually happens to souls afflicted by persecution. With this the Saint tries to explain that to arrive at full communication with God one must pass through a total catharsis. Persecution—whatever it may be and from whomever it may come—is undoubtedly one of those occasions that God presents to us to plunge us into His purifying work.

How are we to act in the face of such a trial? “Above all, unconditional acceptance of the divine trial that purifies through ‘knowledge of oneself and of one’s own misery’53, so as to treat God with more ‘restraint and courtesy’54. God did not reveal His greatness to Job ‘in the time of prosperity,’ nor in the ‘delights and glories which he himself recounts’55, but when He tested him, keeping him ‘naked on the dunghill, forsaken and even persecuted by his friends, full of anguish and bitterness, and the ground strewn with worms.’ Then, St. John of the Cross assures us, the Lord deigned ‘to speak there face to face with him’56. Job’s disposition of acceptance was like that of Abraham, of Moses, of David and of other figures of the Old Testament. A testimony of unshakable faith, not random fatalism”57.

“Aware of the divine trial and yielding to its purifying action, Job experiences it in all its harshness and in all its radicality. He is aware that God’s ways are mysterious: He leads to good and to happiness by painful paths and dark clouds. This is what, according to St. John of the Cross, the ‘prophet’ acknowledges58. In the face of Job’s submission and courageous disposition, God begins the trial with exquisite refinement”59.

In the end, we must have it firmly rooted in our mind that times of trial are the prelude to great happiness. For often, in the purifying night—whether through persecution or through any other affliction that God permits our soul to go through—one reaches the limit of hope, as happened to Job and to so many saints. “The great uncertainty that [the soul] has about its remedy” makes it believe that “its trouble will never end”60. “Everything becomes narrow for this soul… it is a waiting and suffering without the consolation of any certain hope of some light and spiritual good”61. The temptation to discouragement is very common. But as the Master of faith rightly points out, God “granted him the favor of sending him those great trials [to Job] in order to exalt him much more afterward”62, so that later he would go about “inwardly and outwardly as if at a feast”63, with “a great jubilation of God”64. In that happiness the soul’s fidelity ends.

Some recipes against the fear of persecution and tribulation

St. Manuel González, for his part, in his little book Nuestro barro, offers what he calls “Recipes against the fear of persecution and tribulation.” And in a tone that is always humorous, yet at the same time very much to the point, he gives us ten recipes in which nothing is wasted. It would be too long to transcribe here everything the Saint develops in each of them, so we present a brief summary which we trust may serve to encourage many to banish that so very human fear of the scourge of persecution.

1st Recipe: Three points for meditation: 1. St. Augustine said fifteen centuries ago: “If all of us were truly Christians, there would be no heretics.” 2. The same St. Augustine said: “God does not sustain the wicked in the world in vain: He lets them live, either so that they may become good or so that the good may be exercised (in patience, faith, charity) by means of them.” 3. Christians who are frightened and discouraged by the evils that may come upon them through the persecution of the wicked are so either because they have forgotten that they belong to the Church militant or because they have believed that they had already arrived at the Church triumphant… Brothers, we are of the Church militant, with a Captain who, having risen, dies no more!

2nd Recipe: Jesus is Jesus. On the days of rogations [we say]: Arise, O Christ, help us and deliver us for Your Name’s sake! And I said to myself: that, more than asking God, is commanding God; more than inviting, it is pushing…, and that imperative of commanding and pushing comes without any reason or motive on our part…; we do not say: by our merits, by what we have worked or suffered or earned…, but for Your Name’s sake! That is the whole and only reason given! For Your Name’s sake!, that is, by Your power, by Your goodness, above all, by Your mercy, the most gratuitous of all the mercies man receives… Oh! How that cry of faith and confidence, which Mother Church places on the lips of all the afflicted, however sinful and unworthy they may be, enlarges the soul! Christ, arise, help us and deliver us because You are Jesus! Our Jesus…!

3rd Recipe: Exercises in unconcern in honor of St. Now. ‘St. Now’ is the now of God’s will in the present moment; that is, what God wants of you at this instant. The exercises in unconcern? Very simple. Convince yourself of this great truth: What God does not ask of you now, what does it matter to you?, and resolve, consequently, to say to every fear about what came before and what comes after, to every fantasy and every worry and to everything that you should not be doing, wanting or thinking now: What does it matter to me?, or, more briefly, so what? I assure you that in proportion as the devotees of these exercises to ‘St. Now’ increased, the number of those anguished by neurasthenia and of those bent on bulldozing everything would decrease.

4th Recipe: Another practice to make the exercises in unconcern of St. Now easier. Frequently set yourself to hear these words as spoken by Jesus from the Tabernacle: You do your part now and I will do Mine. And let us be certain that, if we seriously devote ourselves to doing well—as far as our frailty allows, and in peace and without haste—at each hour our part, that is, what Jesus asks of us through our duty, and leave to Him the care of what came before and what comes after, and the glory of what is His and what is ours, we will soon become holy and happy unconcerned people…, which is what I wish for all, as for myself.

5th Recipe: The domains of the King of infinite mercy. A soul came to tell me: ‘[…] Is it not upon misery that mercy is exercised? And is not that of our crucified and sacramental King, Jesus, infinite? Then my consolation is neither vain nor unfounded when, from the ocean of mud and even of mire in which my miseries submerge me, I cry out: King of infinite mercy, come and reign in my infinite misery!’ I pass on that cry of hope and consolation here so that it may find an echo and be repeated in many souls that live under the weight of mistrust and under the persecution of fear because they have many miseries! Go, go with them to the Tabernacle and present them as His domains to the King of infinite mercy who awaits you there.

6th Recipe: Who is the one asleep? Some days ago a good friend was insisting on whether, in the present storm that the little boat of the Church is suffering, Jesus seems more asleep than on that night on the lake of Gennesaret. […] On that occasion the one who was sleeping was Jesus and those who were awake and crying for help were the others, the ones who were drowning; and now, now…, in the midst of so fierce a storm… one is tempted to think that the one asleep is not Jesus, but they, and being asleep they do not realize that their souls, their children, their interests and everything that is theirs are drowning… How could Jesus be asleep? If it is precisely He who has permitted the storms of hell to be unleashed, it is to see whether those who travel with Him in the boat, which is the Church, will finally wake from the sleep of lukewarmness in which some, and of death in which others, have long been plunged…! That is why, instead of, or together with, ‘save us, we are perishing!’ one must say: ‘Wake up, or else you will perish!’

7th Recipe: He who does not abandon the abandoned. A poor soul who passes through the earth alone, sick and deprived of hearing, writes to me [and this is] a great lesson for those who suffer alone: ‘I have received your little book, which is very suited to the needs and trials my soul is going through, because I suffer so much when I believe myself abandoned by God and think that everything that happens to me is the effect of His anger provoked by my infidelities!… We who know that no one loves us on earth have such need to know that God loves us…!’ Heart of Jesus, the one in the Tabernacle, the most abandoned and lonely of all hearts, may all the abandoned come to know that You love them…!’

8th Recipe: Two commands of Jesus for times of persecution. But to you who are my friends, I say now: Do not be afraid of those who kill the body, and after that can do no more65. The first command is not to be frightened of persecutors. Blessed will you be when men hate you, and exclude you from their synagogues, and insult you, and spurn your name as accursed, out of hatred for the Son of Man. Rejoice on that day and leap for joy: for a great reward is reserved for you in heaven66. The second command is: to rejoice and leap for joy in the face of persecution. And when Jesus gives these commands it is because He is ready to give the grace for them to be fulfilled: He does not command impossibilities! How much more than one frightened and terrified child of God needs to make acts of living faith and boundless confidence in the Beatitudes! Are they not as much a matter of faith as the creed…?

9th Recipe. Three forgotten truths. 1. It is certain that my heavenly Father does not give me a cross without a cushion to make it less painful and more bearable. 2. That for each cross my heavenly Father gives me and for each hour of duty He imposes on me, He gives me sufficient strength and actual grace to bear them with peace and with merit; and 3. That my heavenly Father does not send me or ask of me today the cross and the duty of tomorrow. Why, then, do I worry today about my cross of tomorrow, about the cross that perhaps will never be mine, and about the duty that comes later? For none of those crosses or hours of duty can I count on the strength and grace of my Father, because He has not yet given them to me, nor are they mine.

10th Recipe: A great recipe against the fear of persecution: the value of reparation. My mother the Church is suffering harsh persecution… it is my duty, as a Catholic, to make reparation to her. How?

– By my adherence, now firmer and more practical, to the Pope, to my prelate […] and by promising to believe with more living faith all her creed, to keep without human respect all her commandments, to pray with more filial confidence and united to the Heart of Jesus and to Mary Immaculate, […] and to see to it that those who depend on me receive her holy sacraments, especially the Holy Eucharist, which, received daily or frequently, heals all the weaknesses of the soul and infuses the fortitude of a hero.

– By my most energetic protest, through all lawful means, against the plundering of which my mother the Church is being made the victim, not only by depriving her of her material goods and of her most beloved institutions, such as the religious orders, but by casting her out of official and public life, through the atheism of the State; out of the family, through civil marriage and divorce; out of the school, through official secularism; out of the tombs of Christians, through the secularization of cemeteries; out of the ideas and sentiments of the people and out of social relations, through the unbridled oral and written propaganda against religion, morality, private property and even against the lives of citizens.

– By my love to the point of sacrifice. Is not one’s natural mother loved in this way? And is not the Church my supernatural mother? […] In this hour of so much plundering and sorrow for the mother, is not sacrifice the duty of the son, to remedy it? […] I want to sacrifice myself for my mother, lastly, by my courage, standing up manfully for Christ and for His doctrine, for His priests, His churches and His institutions, combating the press that combats the Church by depriving it of subscribers, readers, advertisers and every kind of support… One faithful and fervent Catholic gives more glory to God and more consolation to the Church than a hundred lukewarm ones.

A maxim for suffering calumny patiently

During the year 1864, St. Anthony Mary Claret suffered great calumnies and persecutions, to the point that he wrote to another priest: “You cannot form an idea of how much hell is working against me: the most atrocious calumnies, words, deeds, death threats; it puts everything into play to see how it can discredit me and frighten me; but with God’s help I pay no attention”67. It was in that same year that he wrote the booklet “The Consolation of a Calumniated Soul,” which includes eight maxims that, as he himself says, are “to encourage me to suffer persecutions and calumnies in silence, with patience and with resignation”68.

Depending on the affront, each of them was like the ‘driving idea’ that helped him ‘adjust his mind’ and his will so as to suffer in a Christian way. Many of the ideas expressed there we have already mentioned here, but it seems good to us to refer to the 6th maxim the saint writes, since many religious, although every year they meditate on the Principle and Foundation, asking for holy indifference in such a way as not to want “honor more than dishonor”69, are terrified that some contrary, defamatory, insulting or slanderous word might be raised against them. This maxim says: “one day, when there was talk of a certain religious who was held to be a saint, St. Bernard said: He may be a saint, but he lacks the best part, which is to be held as wicked.”

Two counsels of the Master of Ávila for the struggle

These are some counsels that St. John of Ávila includes in his letter number 58. “Some specialists affirm with certainty that this letter was written by the Holy Master from the prison of Triana, in Seville, when he was being investigated by the Inquisition in the year 1532, and that it was addressed to his disciples, who were distressed by their Master’s situation. It is, therefore, a letter that seeks to console, but that at the same time shows the reasons for this consolation”70.

1st Have confidence in the power of Christ: … Do not be troubled or astonished, as at something unusual or strange for the servants of God, by the persecutions, or the shadow of them, that have come upon us. For this has been nothing but a test or examination of the lesson we have been reading for five or six years, saying: ‘To suffer! To suffer for love of Christ!’ You see it here at the door; do not be sorry, like children who would not want to recite the lesson they have studied; but be strengthened in the Lord and in the might of His power (Eph 6:10), who loves you so as to want to defend you; and though He is one, He can do more than all, for He is almighty: so do not fear for lack of knowledge, for there is nothing He does not know; so see whether it is reasonable for one who is bound to God with these three knots to be shaken. Nor let the threats of those who persecute you frighten you, for as for me, I tell you that I do not care a hair for all they threaten, because I am in no hands but those of Christ.

2nd Firm, not retreating before the trial: … And be on your guard not to turn back, not even in a single point, from the good you had begun, because that would be an extreme evil; rather, settle it in your heart that He whom you have followed is the Lord of heaven and earth and of death and of life, and that, in the end (even though the whole world be unwilling), His truth must prevail, which you must labor to follow; for, following it, do not fear men, nor even demons, nor even angels, if they were against us. Make much use of keeping silent with your mouth when speaking with men, and speak much in prayer in your heart with God, from whom all good must come to us.

*****

In the end, everything comes down to resisting with all one’s strength the temptation to “be tributaries,” and to living to the full Christian and priestly royalty and lordship, without renouncing the truth in a single point, without retreating in anything on the path undertaken, without worldly negotiations that seek comfort and consolation, without shrinking before affronts, persecutions and calumnies, and at the same time doing, with a heart swelling with gratitude for the struggle, the most and the best one can for the cause of Christ. Until one can say: “I thank God that I have the privilege of suffering and even of dying for Him”71.

That is why may the words that our Lord repeats to us through our Proper Law resound with greater force today, as addressed especially to us and to the generations that will come after us:

“I (am) your Father, because I am God; I your firstborn brother, because I am man. I am your payment and ransom: why do you fear debts, if you, through penance and Confession, ask to be released from them? I am your reconciliation: why do you fear wrath? I am the bond of your friendship: why do you fear God’s anger? I am your defender: why do you fear adversaries? I am your friend: why do you fear to lack anything I have, if you do not depart from Me? Yours is my Body and my Blood: why do you fear hunger? Yours is my heart: why do you fear being forgotten? Yours is my divinity: why do you fear miseries? And in addition, yours are my angels to defend you; yours my saints to pray for you; yours my blessed Mother to be a careful and loving Mother to you; yours the earth so that you may serve Me in it; yours heaven, because you will come to it; yours the demons and the hells, because you will trample them as slaves and prison; yours life, because with it you gain the life that never ends; yours good pleasures, because you refer them to Me; yours sorrows, because you suffer them for my love and for your own profit; yours temptations, because they are merit and the cause of your eternal crown; yours is death, because it will be for you the nearest passage to life. And all this you have in Me and through Me; for I did not win it for Myself alone, nor do I wish to enjoy it alone; for when I took fellowship in the flesh with you, I took it in order to make you sharers in what I would labor, fast, eat, sweat and weep, and in my sorrows and deaths, if it does not remain undone on your part. You are not poor, you who have so much wealth, unless by your evil life you knowingly wish to lose it!”72.

Rise, let us go!73 Let us advance confidently “amid the persecutions of the world and the consolations of God”74, proclaiming the cross of the Lord until He comes75. Exercising ourselves meanwhile in “the virtues of mortification and patience, desiring to become, in suffering, somewhat like this great God of ours, humbled and crucified; for this life, if it is not for imitating Him, is not good”76.

May our battle cry, today and always, be: “neither Jesus without the Cross, nor the Cross without Jesus”77!

May the maternal presence of Our Lady encourage and comfort us, today and always, to carry our crosses with fortitude, and keep us always united to her Son, our Lord. Amen.

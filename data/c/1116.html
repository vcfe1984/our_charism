“The irreplaceable importance of the life of prayer”
Cf. Constitutions, 40

The Directory of Spirituality, quoting St. John of the Cross, says: “Let those, then, who are active, who think to encircle the world with their preaching and exterior works, take note here that they would profit the Church much more, and please God much more, if they spent even half the time in being with God in prayer”1.

The capital importance of prayer in the religious, priestly and missionary life of the members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word follows not only from the universal end for which God has called us together—namely, “to seek the glory of God and the salvation of souls”2—but also from its specific end, which is “to inculturate the Gospel”3. But even more notably, the irreplaceable importance of the life of prayer4 follows from our spirituality centered on the mystery of the Incarnation, within which our consecrated life is framed; as well as from “our particular way of imitating the Incarnate God starting from the mystery of the Transfiguration”5; since both mysteries—the Incarnation and the Transfiguration—were events of prayer.

Therefore, one cannot be a religious, priest or monk of the Incarnate Word without being a man of prayer who seeks “familiarity with the Word made flesh”6 whom he aims to imitate. That is more important than any other service we can render to the Church. And in this sense the Second Vatican Council said that religious should “seek God before all else and him alone” and “join contemplation with apostolic love”7. The Proper Law adds, moreover, a connotation derived from the demands proper to the religious state, mentioning “the urgency of unceasing prayer and contemplation, and the awareness of the need for the active and passive purifications of sense and spirit”8.

Indeed, given the missionary character of our Institute, we must admit that the proclamation of the Gospel presupposes that strength, courage and hope are obtained through the life of prayer, for it is especially in prayer that God communicates numerous spiritual graces, which not only serve as spiritual nourishment for the missionary, but there, in turn, he receives what he will later transmit to souls.

That is why Blessed Paolo Manna, that great formator of missionaries, said: “The Missionary who wishes to live and remain at the height of his vocation must nourish himself constantly on this spirit of faith, enlightening and inflaming himself with meditation […] He must receive from God, whose instrument he is, through continual prayer, the grace he needs for his ministry, and without which he can do nothing with regard to the eternal salvation of his own soul and of those he went to evangelize”9.

In this regard, the life of prayer in our Institute involves four ‘dimensions,’ namely: personal and communal, liturgical and private; and it constitutes a fundamental element of primary demand in our spiritual formation.

1. Private Prayer

Succinctly, the Proper Law sets down the following maxim: “The missionary must be a ‘contemplative in action’”10.

For “our spiritual commerce lies in doing our works well, and our holiness in doing them according to the rule. Perfection consists in doing them with a spirit of prayer”11, St. Peter Julian Eymard told his own. That is, “it is not enough to have forms of prayer (set times, ways, pious exercises…); rather, it is a matter of attaining a prayerful attitude (like the soul sinking its depths into God)”12. Achieving this prayerful attitude is fundamental for the religious of the Incarnate Word—whether seminarian, brother, priest or monk—to progressively attain holiness13.

Prayer must permeate all our acts. This is what the Mystical Doctor pointed out to a religious: “try to be continual in prayer, and in the midst of bodily exercises do not leave it. Whether you eat, drink, or speak or deal with lay people, or do anything else, always go about desiring God and attaching your heart to him, which is something very necessary for interior solitude”14.

Hence, from their entrance into religious life, our candidates are encouraged “to know and experience the authentic meaning of Christian prayer, as a living and personal encounter with the Father through the Only-begotten Son under the action of the Spirit”15. It is there that the seminarians and novices of the Institute learn to become familiar with his Person and plan of salvation, in order to make it their ideal of life and the inspiration of all their youthful enthusiasm16. Now, for this living and personal encounter with the One and Triune God, in Jesus Christ, silence is necessary as the indispensable spiritual atmosphere for perceiving the presence of God and letting oneself be conquered by it17.

“Another indispensable means for acquiring the spirit of prayer is to attend ‘diligently to the exercises of piety recommended by the holy custom of the Church’18, taking care that ‘spiritual formation not be placed in these alone, nor cultivate only religious affection’19”20.

Among the numerous exercises of piety that Holy Mother Church provides us, our rule prescribes (though without being restricted to these alone): “the daily recitation of the Holy Rosary and the Angelus, the Way of the Cross, the wearing of the scapular, etc.”21. And intrinsically linked to our spirituality and mission is the beloved presence of Our Lady, which flows over the whole sphere of our spiritual life and, to tell the truth, over our whole life: whether in the celebration of Mass, or in the hymn to Our Lady at the end of events or of eutrapelia, or in the missions, etc.

All the devotional practices mentioned above “have their meaning insofar as they are ordered to having a solid spiritual life; that is, [so that the members] live ‘according to the model of the Gospel,’ are grounded in the practice of the theological virtues, ‘in faith, in hope and in charity,’ acquire ‘the spirit of prayer,’ and thus may ‘strengthen and defend their vocation, obtain solidity in the other virtues and grow in zeal to win all men for Christ’22”23.

2. Personal Prayer

From the beginning it has been a practice of the discipline of the Institute to have “the exposition and adoration of the Blessed Sacrament for one hour daily”24, since “a while of true adoration has more value and spiritual fruit than the most intense activity, even if it were apostolic activity itself”25.

That great promoter of Eucharistic adoration—especially among his brother priests—Ven. Archbishop Fulton Sheen insisted that it must be “an hour” (stressing thereby that it was not to be cut short): “the holy hour is grounded in Christ and in Scripture” […] “Why an hour? Because our Lord asked for it so. And our Lord made few requests during his life”26. And he added that Eucharistic adoration should be made every day: because our crosses are daily, not once a week; because children, the sick, the missions, families, the dying need our intercession every day27; because the enemy does not rest28. Moreover, the venerable archbishop said that faithfulness in keeping the hour of adoration with perseverance and fidelity throughout one's priestly life is a sign that the priest is a true victim.

Of the members of the Institute, by reason of our specific vocation, besides the daily hour of adoration, there is asked: “assiduous and prolonged adoration of the Eucharist”29, “as well as perpetual adoration in each Province and distributively in each house, since to adore the Blessed Sacrament is ‘the most excellent act […] it is the most just act’”30.

Such a demand follows from what our Constitutions declare in their first lines, saying that it is our intention “to give the ‘witness that the world cannot be transformed and offered to God without the spirit of the beatitudes’31”32. For we will give this witness of the Gospel beatitudes only if we enter deeply into the contemplation of the word, into intimacy with Christ, and into community life as service and self-giving. Intimate dealings with Christ are, for every Christian and even more for us, like the air we breathe to stay alive. To refuse air is to die. To forget prayer, to let oneself be dragged along by the routine that cools the affection of God's nearness, is also to die33.

We now mention three ‘additional’ reasons, besides those already known, that Venerable Fulton Sheen gives for not failing to make the hour of adoration:

▪ The hour of adoration combats priestly weariness

We must admit, the North American archbishop affirmed, that “as in a marriage, after some years in the priesthood the sensitivity of love is lost. Therefore we must pay closer attention to the things we have heard, lest we drift away, as the Letter to the Hebrews says34. Our souls are lost not only because we do evil things, but also because we are negligent in doing good things: we bury the talent, we do not walk the extra mile, we pass by the wounded man the Samaritan helped. How often in the Gospel condemnation follows precisely from not doing: you did not give me to eat, you did not give me to drink, you did not welcome me, you did not visit me…”35. That is why he proposed the hour of adoration as a means of combating priestly weariness.

How many times have we witnessed this very thing: contemplating Christ in the Eucharist, as Model and as Strength, how many of our missionaries in remote places where fraternal consolation hardly reaches, experiencing the weariness proper to unconditional self-giving to the mission, keep the robustness and freshness of their self-giving unaltered, ready to give even more.

We have the enormous privilege of having in our houses the Divine Presence of our Lord, and therefore a greater responsibility to adore him and keep him company. However, two main reasons are often put forward to excuse negligence in making adoration, namely: lack of time and lack of willpower (to overcome laziness or tiredness).

“We say we have no time. How many hours do we spend on things that scatter us? The excuses we sometimes make are as foolish as those given in the Gospel to that king for not attending the banquet: I have bought a field and I have to go and see it (imagine, he bought it without seeing it); I have bought five yoke of oxen and I am on my way to ‘try them out’; I have just gotten married… and so why don't you bring your wife too? And so we argue that we have no time…”36.

In response to that first ‘excuse’ for not making the hour of adoration, the Spiritual Father of our Religious Family, speaking once to priests, told them: “The increase of work in the Lord's vineyard, precisely when the number of laborers is decreasing, can make us forget that we have been called above all to be with the Lord, to listen to his word, to contemplate his face. The contemplative dimension is inseparable from the mission, because, according to the famous definition of St. Thomas, also taken up by the Council, the mission is essentially ‘contemplata aliis tradere’, to hand on to others what we have first long contemplated.

Hence the need for long spaces of prayer, of recollection, of adoration […] As consecrated persons we must not only pray, we must be a living prayer. One could also say we must pray while apparently not praying. We must pray while apparently not having time to pray, but we must pray. It is another paradox. Humanly, this is something impossible. How can one pray while not praying? But St. Paul tells us: the spirit prays in us, and then the matter turns out somewhat differently”37.

“The second real reason for not making adoration is our weakness of will.

We live in an age in which our will collapses under emotion; we have no determination. How many have abandoned the self-discipline that is the condition for our priestly victimhood. We become like children: not more than a second must pass between our demands and their satisfaction.

How do we overcome the excuse of apathy? By taking control of a segment of time, of one hour, and redeeming it. It is for our Lord, it is for the Church, it is for the world, not for me. We must kill acedia by displacing it with a new love. We cannot expel our vices all at once, but we can hem them in by deepening our love for Christ”38.

▪ The hour of adoration is a sign of our victimhood in the work of redemption

Because it incorporates us into the intercessory work of Christ. We are bound to humanity, to the nations, to the missions, to our homeland, to our diocese, to our parish, to our Institute, to our friends and to our enemies. Our priesthood-victimhood commits us to intercede for them and for their salvation. The agony of Christ continues in marriages going through difficulties, in young people who have lost their purity, in religious who are tempted, in the children of addicted parents, in the abandoned elderly, in defections… The hour of adoration of the Blessed Sacrament allows us to practice an eminent act of charity, since “we adore our God and Lord with all our mind, all our heart, all our soul and with all our strength, and in adoring him we also practice perfect charity toward our neighbor, praying for him and imploring on his behalf the graces and mercies of the Savior”39.

That is why another motivation for the hour of adoration is also that of reparation. The Proper Law says it explicitly: we “adore Jesus Christ for those who do not adore him, who abandon him, forget him, despise him and offend him”40.

“To be a priest,” said John Paul II, “means to be a mediator between God and men, in the Mediator par excellence who is Christ. Jesus was able to carry out his mission thanks to his total union with the Father, because he was one with Him […] In order to be able to continue the mission of Christ effectively, the priest too must in some way have already arrived where he wishes to lead others: he arrives there through assiduous contemplation of the mystery of God, nourished by the study of Scripture […] Fidelity to the times and means of personal prayer contributes to sanctifying the priest and leading him to an experience of the mysterious and fascinating presence of the living God, enabling him to act powerfully upon the human environment around him”41.

▪ Finally, the hour of adoration is necessary as a form of authentic prayer

No one is unaware that our world is incredibly fast. To that must be added the noise that drowns out the voice of conscience. We live in a world where activity kills the knowledge of oneself and of God that contemplation brings. And precisely for that reason, today more than ever, “the priest and the religious must live in intimacy with their Master and strive to become saints as the rule asks, in order to be available to the intuitions of the Holy Spirit and respond better to the calls of the world. The life of prayer—St. John Paul II warns—does not distance one from men; on the contrary, it helps one to perceive more deeply their fundamental needs, which only Christ can reveal to us”42. It is true “that it is not easy, especially today when the pace of life has become frenetic and occupations absorb us to such a great extent. But we must be convinced—the Pope continued—that the moment of prayer is precisely when the unity of the priest with his own faithful is strongest, the moment when he is ‘most present’ and his ministry is most effective”43.

We must not forget what the Constitutions warn us of in this regard: “For us pastoral work is a cross, not a reason for escapism; that is why we must never fall into sterile activism: ‘activity for the Lord must not make us forget Him who is the Lord of activity’”44. Moreover—and this we would like to underline—the level of pastoral and apostolic yield will always be in proportion to the measure of our fidelity to Christ according to the commitments assumed in our profession of vows, a fidelity that rests on union with God through prayer and the sacraments in order to maintain the life of grace. If there were not a perfect balance between our life with God and the activities carried out in the service of others, not only the work of evangelization would be compromised, but also our personal condition as evangelized. Prayer is the soul of our work for the Kingdom.

“The priest must be insatiably hungry for God”45. For “the priest is the man of God, the one who belongs to God and makes people think of God…”46. The people who have been entrusted to us seek the solidity of the doctrine of the faith, absolute values, and that Absolute is God. That is why we must respond to this request by speaking of Him, communicating Him and Him alone. That is the greatness of our mission, and to carry it out fully we must nourish that faith and strengthen it every day in prayer. St. John Paul II said that priests must be the “professionals of faith,” and that for this assiduous prayer is necessary, living our days at a rhythm of prayer.

In this sense, “recollection is absolutely necessary in order to draw profit from what one does; otherwise, we are left with those sorts of oases [of intermittent acts] which are the spiritual practices, but outside of them, everything is arid. When, afterwards, we cannot keep our minds fixed on God, it is enough to refer our actions to him and everything becomes prayer. In this consists the spirit of prayer, which helps the interior life greatly. A missionary must be able to maintain recollection anywhere; to know how to pass from study or work to prayer; to remain united to God with a permanent, or at least frequent, lifting up of the heart; in short, to work with great diligence and, at the same time, to pray. If you do not have this spirit, you will never be good missionaries. You may believe you are, but in reality you are not. Happy are you if you always try to advance in the interior life, with the spirit of recollection and prayer!”47, said Blessed Allamano.

Benedict XV wrote in the encyclical Humani generis redemptionem: “The salvation of souls is not achieved by many words, nor by learned disquisitions, nor by fervent perorations. And if a preacher bases his preaching on these means, he is nothing more than a sounding brass or a clanging cymbal48. What really makes the human word capable of helping souls is the grace of God”49. And the grace of God is obtained through prayer and through a life in conformity with his supreme directives.

On the other hand, none of our members should be unaware that, just as the life of prayer has an irreplaceable importance, so too there is a great “need for the active and passive purifications of sense and spirit”50. When the soul passes through the “cure of the night,” it must not abandon prayer on that account; quite the contrary, it must remain in patience and persevere in it. For, as St. John of the Cross says, “very foolish would be the one who, lacking spiritual sweetness and delight, should think that for that reason God is lacking to him, and, when he had it, should rejoice and delight, thinking that for that reason he had God. And more foolish still would he be if he went about seeking this sweetness in God and rejoiced and lingered in it”51.

3. Communal prayer

Complementary to personal and private prayer is communal prayer.

“The words of the Lord, pray always without losing heart52, apply both to personal and to communal prayer53. Both require fidelity and perseverance. Both are supernatural means that help the growth of community life and will certainly help to overcome, in a creative and prudent way, the difficulties proper to some communities, such as the diversity of tasks and therefore of schedules, the absorbing overload of work and the various forms of tiredness”54.

In our Institute, communal prayer includes, besides daily Mass, adoration of the Blessed Sacrament and the recitation of the Liturgy of the Hours, the weekly penitential liturgy and the sacrament of reconciliation55, whenever possible and pastoral needs permit.

“Prayer in common is the response to and the basis of all community life. It starts from the contemplation of the mystery of God, great and sublime, from wonder at his presence, at work in the most significant moments of our religious families as well as in the humble daily reality of our communities. The religious community, as a response to the Lord's pressing invitation watch and pray56, must be vigilant and take the time necessary to care for the quality of its life. Sometimes the day of religious who ‘have no time’ runs the risk of being too busy and anxious, and for that very reason may end up tiring and exhausting them”57.

4. Liturgical prayer

“Most valuable in this regard is liturgical prayer. In no one may the conviction weaken that community is built up starting from the Liturgy, above all from the celebration of the Eucharist, of the other sacraments and of the common recitation of the Liturgy of the Hours”58.

In this sense, the priests of our Religious Family are asked to celebrate Holy Mass daily and, barring pastoral commitments, to try to concelebrate as often as possible. Likewise, a moment of the day must be found for communal Eucharistic adoration59. And, thirdly, “all due importance must also be given to the pious and devout recitation of the Liturgy of the Hours […] Ordinarily the major hours of the Office should be recited in community”60, the Proper Law establishes.

▪ On the Liturgy of the Hours

Regarding the recitation of the Breviary, it is important to highlight the “pious and devout recitation” mentioned above. That is, at no time should it be hurried or unthinking, like someone who instead of reading ‘scans’ the page and does not think about what he is saying. It must be a moment of true prayer, of asking God, of praising him with the very words with which He wants us to ask him and praise him. They are words of the Holy Spirit! In such a way that, together with Eucharistic adoration, the recitation of the Liturgy of the Hours may help to cultivate and maintain throughout the whole day the spirit of prayer of which we spoke at the beginning. The recitation of the Breviary must truly be a “source of piety and nourishment for personal prayer”61.

Archbishop Fulton Sheen, however, writes with great realism in his autobiography: “Few priests like vocal prayer. It is a fact. This is not because priests are not men of prayer. It is because their prayers are sighs, aspirations and inspirations. […] They have few petitions. Rarely do they make a novena for something they need; they ask people to make them. […] And few want to admit that they are bored with something they are supposed to like. The Breviary belongs to that category. Priests are expected to rave with love for the Breviary, but many of us are like those people who go to an opera for the sake of appearances but neither enjoy it nor understand it. […] Perhaps the Breviary was meant to be difficult for any average priest. Could it not be like the wrestling between Jacob and God? If we look at it from this point of view, it may still be a struggle, but it would fall into the category of prolonged and unceasing intercession. For we would be praying it as our Lord prayed in the Garden of Olives, like the friend who kept knocking on the door at night asking for a piece of bread, like the widow who never ceased her pleas before the judge. Importuning is not relaxing in dreams, but sustained work”62.

“The Liturgy of the Hours is the sanctification of the day,” says the Laudis Canticum of St. Paul VI. That is why it is important that, before or after Mass, this be the first thing we do, that we make sure of it.

Blessed Joseph Allamano, for his part, told his own: “Let us observe the indications of the Church with regard to the time for praying the Liturgy of the Hours. Having a lot of work must not lead us to postpone it. Prayed in good time, it is a sweet burden. As for the place, if possible, pray it in the church, which is the house destined for prayer. The Liturgy of the Hours is the most excellent prayer after the Mass. May praising God be one of our principal occupations, as it will be for all eternity!”63.

We must add, moreover, that “the Liturgy of the Hours, like the other liturgical actions, is not a private action, but belongs to the whole body of the Church, manifests it and influences it”64. In such a way that each time we take up the Breviary to pray, we take up the millions of unbelievers in this world, the persecuted churches, the most distant missions, the thousands of Catholics in the world who do not regularly have a priest where they live, the thousands of priests in the world who must carry out their ministry covertly, etc., so that our prayer becomes a missionary work.

Archbishop Fulton Sheen even offers us some practical recommendations for praying the Breviary65:

  1. To pray it, as far as possible, before the Blessed Sacrament. A practice that also allows us to gain the corresponding indulgences.
  2. To notice that most of the psalms confront us with two figures: the Sufferer and the King. That helps us to interpret the psalms better.
  3. To have recourse to the Holy Spirit during the recitation of the Breviary. Not necessarily with a formal prayer, but as a gesture asking for help to do it well, with understanding, unhurriedly.
  4. To offer certain Hours of the Liturgy for certain intentions.

Let us say, finally, that the Breviary is not a yoke and a burden; rather, it is an obligation, but an obligation of love. If the priest is selfish, the recitation of the Liturgy becomes merely an obligation; if the priest is a person aware that it is the prayer of the Church, that obligation carries love contained within it; if the priest is a victim, love turns that obligation into such ardor that he feels no obligation at all.

▪ On Holy Mass

One of the great loves of every member of the Institute is the Eucharist66. In accordance with this, we read in our Constitutions: The principal thing, the most important thing we must do each day, is to take part in the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass67. “We must be characterized by the importance that must be given to the celebration of Holy Mass, as well as by the reverent way of celebrating it”68, we read in one of the acts of the 2007 General Chapter, which points to this very thing as an element attached to the non-negotiable charism.

That is why it can never be sufficiently stressed that it belongs to us “to be masters of the ars celebrandi, and to our major seminarians, our brothers, etc., to strive for their part to live the ars participandi in the most perfect way”69.

“It must be kept in mind that the celebration of the Mass is a thermometer of the priestly life”70. So much so that one can say that “a priest is worth as much as his Eucharistic life is worth; his Mass above all. Mass without love, a sterile priest: fervent Mass, a priest who conquers souls. Eucharistic devotion neglected and without love, a slack priesthood, even more, a priesthood in danger”71.

The Eucharist, St. Leonard Murialdo recalled, is not a rite to be performed but a mystery to be lived72.

And if this is a mystery to be lived, then we must not forget that our “style of liturgical celebrations as part of our charism [consists in] celebrations in which the Word becomes incarnate and in which he appears—sacramentally—Incarnate, in which the principal presence and action of the principal Priest73 is always highlighted, in which it is perceived that the essential attitude of the secondary priest is the prayerful attitude—proper to one who knows himself to be a mere instrument, and a deficient instrument, subordinate to the principal cause and subject to its ends—, in which all the visible elements contribute to the splendid knowledge of the Invisible”74.

Concretely: “our liturgical celebrations must be exemplary: ‘in their rites, in their spiritual and pastoral tone, and in the fidelity due both to the prescriptions and texts of the liturgical books and to the norms issued by the Holy See and the Episcopal Conferences’”75.

* * * * *

In short, to pray is necessary in order to live well. Whoever prays responds to his vocation and is faithful to it. A priest of the Incarnate Word will never be able to be “another Christ” for men if he is not first a “man of God.”

That is why we must feel as personally addressed to us the exhortation of the Spiritual Father of our Religious Family, who said: “Learn to pray! …so that you may become ‘masters’ of prayer and may also teach those entrusted to you to pray!”76. For, being contemplatives of the Incarnate Word, we will draw strength from the mystery we contemplate to prolong generously “the Incarnation in all reality”77.

Consequently, all of us members of the Institute must not only respect the prescribed times of prayer, but also know how to find time to be alone with God, listening in silence to what He has to tell us. For prayer allows us in a certain way to place ourselves within the dimensions of God, inserting ourselves humbly but courageously into the very heart of God, from which we must “not want to leave”78. We must be souls of prayer, souls of the Eucharist79.

Wherever we carry out our mission, our spiritual mission is the same everywhere: to illuminate with the “resplendent light that dawns from on high” all those who “dwell in darkness and in the shadow of death”80. This is truly our mission, whether we are priests in a parish in one of the great cities or serve a small rural community; whether we carry out our activity as pastors, chaplains, teachers, assisting in a home, or have been sidelined for reasons of health. “To pray is to acknowledge that Christ is risen and deserves unconditional dedication”81.

In other words: “Take care of your life of prayer and goodness, so as to be exemplary ministers and bearers of joy and serenity to all! Cultivate intimacy with Christ through a sincere and deep interior life, always remembering that your mission is to be witnesses of the supernatural and heralds of Christ to the men of our time, who are becoming ever more aware, even when appearances may sometimes suggest the contrary, of the call of God and their need for him”82.

Let us not forget that one must love prayer in order to be zealous apostles of the kingdom of God. […] One must find the time to pray ‘well,’ since from the heart immersed in God springs the spiritual energy for an effective apostolate83.

We conclude by paraphrasing the Mystic of Fontiveros, who wrote in a letter to one of his directees: “Do not let the soul cling to anything; for, as long as prayer is not lacking, God will take care of its affairs, since it belongs to no other master, nor ever shall”84.

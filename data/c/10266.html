Study in the religious life

The study of the religious, chiefly the knowledge of the truths of the faith together with the consequent penetration of them, is necessary for the formation of the religious. This is explicitly commanded in the legislation of the Church (CIC 587-589), where formation is required. Thus Pope Pius XII taught in his apostolic constitution Sedes sapientiae, addressed to the religious of the whole world: “Religious, whose principal obligation is the contemplation of divine things, seeking God alone and uniting themselves intimately with Him and transmitting these things to others, must remember that they can in no way carry out this most holy mission fittingly and fruitfully, nor rise to a lofty union with Christ, unless they possess in abundance that profound and ever-perfectible knowledge of God and of His mysteries which is acquired through sacred studies.

“The first concern of religious superiors must be to watch over the solid and

complete formation of the intellect, taking into account the natural development of the young and the distribution of studies. And although it is true that the same is not to be required of all religious nor of all orders, nevertheless it must never be forgotten that a thorough instruction in the subject of Religion and in the principles of Philosophy and Theology is absolutely incumbent upon all.

Study in the religious of the IVE

The words of the Magisterium—more could be cited—have greater force if we consider them in our own case, that is, as religious of the Incarnate Word, whose principal charism, which illuminates our religious life and reflects our longings, is the evangelization of culture.

Indeed, we read in one of the directories: [14] “Every religious of the Incarnate Word knows that he must apply himself to the knowledge of the truth and to its transmission beyond the concrete place where he carries out his apostolate. He knows that in order to evangelize the culture he must devote himself thoroughly to this task”1. Why study? What is the principal end of our study? There are two, but one is merely a consequence of the first. We study in order better to know and love Jesus Christ, and in order better to make Jesus Christ known and loved. For a thing is loved insofar as it is known.

“The supreme rule of religious life is the following of Christ2. And Christ is the supreme truth: I am the truth (Jn 14:ó). But to follow Christ as Truth means applying the intellect to know Him more, which will have to be something permanent in the disciple, for the riches found in Christ are inexhaustible. This is the foundation of the intellectual formation of monks”3.

One could cite a great many texts, both from our Constitutions and from the directories, in which

it is affirmed that all, to a greater or lesser degree, must apply themselves to study, at least as part of basic formation and of ongoing formation. In our Congregation, so to speak, no one is exempted from this: “The brothers shall make the Studentate, in which they will take some treatises of Philosophy and Theology, ‘attending to the needs of the Church and to the circumstances of men and of the times, as the end and character of the Institute require’4. The religious brothers will continue their formation until perpetual profession, in the houses to which they are assigned and under the direct authority of the local Superior, and attention will be given to their ongoing formation”5.

Study in the contemplative life of the IVE

And this duty applies not only to those in the active life, nor only to priests who by their office will have to exercise intellectual activities, but evidently also to contemplatives: “The contemplative sisters of the Institute of the Servants must be firmly convinced of the ‘need for an initial and ongoing formation suited to the vocation and life of contemplative search for God in solitude and silence, in continual prayer and in intense penance,’ and that a formed and enlightened faith cooperates more with the end of the Institute”6.

In the directories of monastic life this express will is particularly stressed, as is the fundamental importance of ongoing intellectual formation for monastic life, which finds its special vigor in relation to the spirituality proper to our religious family: “All the more so because of our particular spirituality in relation to our specific end, which ‘requires a faith enlightened by continual reflection that measures itself against the sources of the Church’s message, and a constant spiritual discernment sought in prayer’7, that is, the special need of study and contemplation for the apostolate of evangelizing the culture”8.

For: “Study will be useful for the contemplative life itself [as St. Thomas says]“directly, by assisting contemplation, that is, by enlightening the intellect…, indirectly, by removing the dangers of contemplation, that is, the errors which frequently occur in the contemplation of divine things for those who are ignorant of the Scriptures…”9.

Likewise we read in the Directory of Monastic Life that “historically it was the monasteries that saved for humanity all that was most important of what the human spirit had produced through the centuries; becoming, moreover, sources of culture by teaching within their walls the works of the Fathers, those of pagan Greek and Latin authors, science and the liberal arts such as painting, music, etc. Therefore, in the monasteries cultural activities will be carried out which contribute to their proper end—union with God—and to the end of the Institute—the evangelization of culture—10. This will be regulated in each monastery”11. This requires, as we have already mentioned, “A formed and enlightened faith is necessary for the monk who is a priest and spiritual father, of whom St. Teresa said: see that he be learned’12”13.

Therefore we must not neglect this fundamental aspect of our formation, so characteristic of

our institute. Formation according to the measure of our possibilities and abilities.

The virtue of studiousness

Studiousness. It is the most specific virtue of the one who studies. Study implies above all a strong application of the soul to something, and this application is regulated by the virtue of studiousness.

This virtue must be joined with three other virtues:

  • The first and fundamental one, the virtue that informs all the acts of the other virtues

and gives them, as it were, a new form, a new beauty, a new order. And this holds also for study: our study must be ordered by the love of God and by the love of neighbor.

  • The Recognizing our limitations and possibilities is fundamental in the counsels of St. Thomas. Moreover, because without humility research engenders proud men and, worse still, intellectually proud men14. Study requires effort and therefore perseverance in that unceasing search for the truth, which must go together with humility, recognizing that all gifts come from God and must return to Him. Such study will be done in depth, avoiding a merely textbook-bound and formalistic study, in such a way that it helps the progress of faith and the increase of charity.
  • The that is, the tenacity that keeps the student from falling prey to sloth, discouragement, apathy. It is the quality that crowns with success the undertakings begun. It is the quality that brings to completion the undertakings begun. Sloth, sensuality, pride, envy and the other vices are enemies of knowledge. It is necessary to practice the contrary virtues.

This formation, which moreover must be ongoing, has a penitential aspect that counters the threefold concupiscence and therefore helps toward the fulfillment of the evangelical counsels themselves, “since it serves to avoid the lust of the flesh… to remove the desire for riches… (and) it is useful moreover as a lesson in obedience.” Moreover, only through persevering study can one attain Wisdom, with which all good things come. I rejoiced in all these goods, because Wisdom is the one who directs them, though I did not know that she was their mother15.

Certainly the good practice of study will require effort and struggle.

Vices

There are two vices opposed to the right use of this virtue. As a moral virtue, one can sin against it both by defect and by excess.

  • By defect, negligence16. That is, neglecting precisely this aspect of formation. That is, through negligence not forming oneself, not studying, not making the most of study time. Not fulfilling our duty to form ourselves. There can be many temptations of many kinds: such as sloth, loss of interest, excusing oneself with other duties, etc., etc.

By excess, vain curiosity17, which is also a defect, no longer of the one who does not study but of the one who studies badly. Curiosity consists above all in devoting oneself to the study of purely superficial things, or else of important things but in disregard of one’s fundamental duties. Opposed to the virtue are both those who neglect the study required by the obligations of their state and profession, and those who devote themselves only to the study that satisfies their desires. The curious squander their real faculties.

Practical helps for before, during and after study

– Before, rectify the intention of doing it exclusively by and for the glory of God. There are four

virtues that must influence intellectual work so that it may be completely sanctified:

. studiousness, which is the most specific virtue of the one who studies;

. religion, offering the hour of study as an act of praise to God;

. obedience, studying as a duty of one’s state;

. charity, turning the intellectual act into an act of love for God.

– During study, it must possess the same qualities as prayer, since it is prayer. That is:

worthily, attentively and devoutly.

– After study one must give oneself to prayer. Let prayer prepare for study and study

for prayer. Meditating before the tabernacle on what has been the object of our study. The counsel of St. Thomas: «do not cease to devote yourself to prayer. The true intellectual vocation is sustained by the spirit of prayer, which must not only precede and conclude intellectual work, but accompany it, permeate it and in some way constitute it: the work itself, especially when it is devoted to theological inquiry, is prayer. Since the object of theology is the truth, the living God and His plan of salvation revealed in Jesus Christ, the theologian is called to intensify his life of faith and always to unite scientific research and prayer.

“For the saints, study was prayer and contemplation”18. Why, then, did some who apparently studied a great deal go astray? St. Augustine says: because “they did not know the way, your Word, by whom You made the things they number, the very ones who number them, the sense by which they perceive the things they number, and the mind by virtue of which they number them; […] they did not know this way, by which, descending from themselves, they might go down to Him and through Him ascend to Him; they did not know, I say, this way, and they thought themselves loftier and more radiant than the stars, and so they came tumbling to the ground, their foolish heart being darkened. They do not seek with a spirit of piety the Maker of the universe, and therefore they do not find Him … for it is vanity to make profession of these worldly things, but it is piety to confess You”19.

Characteristics

St. Thomas said in general terms: “the exercise of the moral virtues, by which the passions are mastered, is of the greatest importance for the acquisition of knowledge”20.

Someone might say: ‘no, I don’t have the gifts, I’m no good.’ It is worth recalling the letter of St.

Thomas to his disciple John, who asked him for advice on how to study better. His counsels do not deal above all with intellectual conditions but with human conditions.

“Dearest: since you have asked me in what way you should devote yourself to study in order to acquire the treasure of knowledge, here is my advice:

Do not wish to enter the sea all at once, but try to reach it by way of the rivers, because it is through the easier things

that one must arrive at the more difficult ones.

This, then, is my advice, which will serve you as a rule:

– I want you to avoid useless talk.

-Have purity of conscience.

– Do not neglect prayer.

– Love recollection

– Be kind to everyone.

– Do not be curious about the affairs of others.

– Do not be overly familiar with anyone, because this breeds contempt and gives occasion to neglect study:

– Avoid wandering about in all things.

– Seek to imitate the example of upright persons; do not look at who it is that speaks, but retain all the good that he says.

– Try to understand what you read and hear.

– Clear up your doubts and fix in your memory all the good you hear, without regard to who says it.

-Do not seek things beyond your capacity.

By following these norms, you will put forth great branches and produce useful fruit wherever the Lord has destined you to live. By putting these counsels into practice you will be able to reach the goal to which you aspire.”

Let us also recall here the example of St. Catherine of Siena, who, though illiterate, became a Doctor of the Church, and of St. Charles of Sezze, who, also illiterate, composed works of a most lofty spiritual tenor.

May the Virgin Mary teach us to practice this virtue, so very important for religious life and fundamental for our spirituality, so that, according to our possibilities, we may devote ourselves to study in order to possess a profound knowledge of the Incarnate Word, for His greater glory.

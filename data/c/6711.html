[Introduction]

In the first paragraph of our Constitutions we confess that Christ “remains in the Catholic Church governed by the successor of Peter and the Bishops in communion with him”1, and a few paragraphs further on, in number 7, we say that “we want to love and serve, and to make others love and serve, Jesus Christ: his Body and his Spirit. Both the physical Body of Christ in the Eucharist, and the Mystical Body of Christ, which is the Church.” From this it follows that another of our great loves as priests of the Incarnate Word is love for the Church. For our love and service of Jesus Christ are identified with our love and service of the Church, because they are not two loves but only one. And that is what we are going to speak about now.

By way of preamble we must say that the Church is inseparable from Christ.

In the divine order there is never a kenosis without a pleroma. That is why Archbishop Fulton Sheen expressed this reality with his exquisite pen, saying: “if the kenosis was the emptying of Christ as Victim, the pleroma2of Christ is the Church. […] The Church without Christ would be like an empty chalice; Christ without the Church would be like a rich wine that cannot be drunk for lack of a chalice. […] Just as there is no Messiah without Israel, nor birth of Christ without the Virgin Mother, nor Christ without his Church, so neither is there fullness of Christ outside his Mystical Body… The Church is the personification of Christ, just as Christ is the incarnation of God. He is the Bridegroom, the Church his Bride”3.

Hence “there exists an intimate bond between Christ, the Church and evangelization”4. And this is what our Directory of Spirituality teaches when it says that “the reality—hierarchical and at the same time mystical, visible and spiritual, earthly and heavenly, canonical and charismatic, human and divine—which is the Church, by a profound analogy ‘is compared to the Mystery of the Incarnate Word’5, since ‘Christ himself is Incarnate in his Body, the Church6”7. And our Directory of Consecrated Life also points out: “love for Christ the Head includes love for his body, the Church, with which he is mystically identified”8. That is why, from the Novitiate9 onward, “love for the Church and for her sacred pastors”10 has been instilled in us as parts of one and the same reality.

Our Constitutions, in turn, declare with great force our clear intention “to abase ourselves at the feet of the Church … and to obey out of love for Christ … those whom the Holy Spirit has placed to govern the Church of God”11, and we affirm that “submission to the ecclesiastical hierarchy” is a “title of honor of our Religious Family”12. For—as Blessed Paul VI said—“one cannot love Christ without the Church, listen to Christ but not to the Church, be in Christ but on the margins of the Church”13.

That much, just to begin.

1. The Church as the Mystical Body of Christ

“Since his resurrection and forever, the sacred humanity of Jesus Christ lives a glorious and immortal life, which since the moment of the ascension is in heaven and which will return when his second coming takes place”14.

“But in the span of centuries between the ascension and the parousia he lives his mystical life here on earth. Indeed, just as the Word assumed a human nature to fulfill the plan of salvation, so, to prolong that plan through the ages, he chooses other human natures so that salvation may reach all men, of all times”15. Christ himself said: Behold, I am with you always, until the consummation of the world16.

Seven truths then follow concerning this New Body of Christ, his Mystical Body17:

  • To be members of his Mystical Body, we must be born in its womb. It is not a birth in the human manner, but a rebirth through the Spirit in the waters of Baptism, which makes us children of God. Therefore, our belonging to the Church is through faith and baptism18. St. John of the Cross, with all the depth and sharpness that characterize him, writes in the Spiritual Canticle: “This is the adoption of the children of God, who will truly say to God what the Son himself said through St. John to the Eternal Father, saying: all my things are yours, and your things are mine19. He by essence, being the natural Son; we by participation, being adopted children. And so he said it, not only for himself, who is the Head, but for his whole Mystical Body, which is the Church. Which will share in the very beauty of the Bridegroom on the day of her triumph, which will be when she sees God face to face”20. This is perhaps an aspect that we sometimes forget or that passes into the background, but it is a reality: because we are adopted children of God, all things are ours, and one day we will share in the beauty of God.

  • The unity between this, his Mystical Body, and him is not achieved by singing hymns in His honor, nor by holding charitable gatherings in His name, nor by watching Mass on the same Catholic channel, but by sharing in His life: I am the Vine, you are the branches; whoever abides in me and I in him bears much fruit21.

  • The Mystical Body of Christ, like all living beings, was small at the beginning, as he said: Like a grain of mustard seed, but “it will be gloriously perfected at the end of time”22.

  • Another aspect: a house is built starting from the outside and finishing on the inside, brick by brick; human organizations are formed by the union of men among themselves, but always from the external to the internal. The Mystical Body of Christ, on the other hand, is formed from the inside to the outside, in the same way as an embryo is formed in the human body. Just as Christ received Life from the Father, we receive Life from Him. As Christ himself affirmed: That all may be one, as You, Father, are in Me and I in You, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you sent me23. Here the Holy Spirit is manifested as the Life-giver, ‘the Lord and giver of life,’ as we profess in the Creed24. “He makes us new creatures, built by God: You are built together in the Spirit into a dwelling place of God25, and through this renewal and purification, and in Him, we can have access to the Father: both of us have access to the Father in one Spirit26”27

  • Our Lord said that he would have only one Body. It would have been a spiritual monstrosity for him to have had many bodies or a dozen heads. To keep it united, it had to have only one shepherd, who, as he said, would feed his lambs and his sheep. …and there will be one flock and one shepherd28. Therefore, we can say that his Mystical Body “is an organically structured community29; ‘a people gathered by the unity of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit’30”31. And if it is structured, then it has a hierarchy to be respected.

  • Jesus Christ proclaimed that this, His new Body, would not be manifested to men before the day of Pentecost, on which he would send His Spirit of Truth. …if I do not go away, the Counselor will not come to you32. Anything else begun twenty-four hours after Pentecost, or twenty-four hours ago, may well be an organization, but it will be a human organization, with a human/worldly spirit, not a divine one; that is to say, it would be like an isolated wire, not connected to the Motor (with a capital M), and therefore it will not be the depository of the truth. John of the Cross was very serious about this, and that is why he stressed this same doctrine, saying: “inasmuch as there are no more articles to reveal concerning the substance of our faith than those already revealed in the Church”33.

  • The most interesting observation the Lord made regarding His Body was that it would be hated by the world, just as He himself was. The world loves the things of the world, but hates what is divine. But because you are not of the world, but I have chosen you out of the world, therefore the world hates you34.

Those, then, are seven truths about the Mystical Body of Christ. Now, we must also say that Christ relives his life in the Church. And just as Christ was Priest, Prophet and King and had the threefold power of teaching, governing and sanctifying, he communicates these same powers to his Mystical Body. Which has its consequences:

  • He is the infallible Teacher who said: I am the Truth, and the same one who says to his Mystical Body: I will send you… the Spirit of Truth, and he will guide you into all truth35. And Christ is identified with his Church to such a degree that whoever hears the word that comes from his Church will hear His own. Whoever hears you hears Me; whoever rejects you rejects Me; and whoever rejects Me rejects Him who sent me36.

That is why Pope Paul VI denounced: “We do not accept the attitude of those who seem to ignore the living tradition of the Church… and interpret in their own way the doctrine of the Church, even the Gospel itself, spiritual realities, the divinity of Christ, his Resurrection or the Eucharist, practically emptying them of their content and in this way creating a new gnosis…”37.

The truth of Christ is the truth of his Mystical Body, and that is why it is infallible; it is divine truth.

St. John of the Cross in his Ascent of the Mount, after having pulverized the pretensions of those who seek more revelation outside, or on the margins of, or beyond Christ, concludes by teaching: “that in all things we must be guided by the law of Christ made man and of his Church and ministers, humanly and visibly”38. And in one of his best letters he says to one of his directees: “What is there to get right but to go by the level road of the law of God and of the Church?”39.

  • On the other hand, our Lord, who is King, said: All power has been given to me in heaven and on earth40. And he has transmitted that power to his Mystical Body, to the point that its precepts are His; its commands, His, as He himself affirmed: Truly I say to you, whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven41.

This is precisely what St. Ignatius prescribes in his 1st rule for thinking with the Church, saying: laying aside all judgment, we must have our mind ready and prompt to obey in all things the true spouse of Christ our Lord, which is our holy mother the hierarchical Church”42.

  • Finally, our Lord is Priest, since he redeemed us before God by His Death on the cross. St. John of the Cross refers particularly to this mystery, the mystery that Christ gave his life for his Bride the Church. Thus the saint speaks, placing the following verses on the lips of Christ: “and so that she may have life, I would die for her”43. And in the Spiritual Canticle he goes on to say: beneath the favor of the tree of the Cross, […] [it was] where the Son of God redeemed, and consequently espoused to himself, human nature, and consequently each soul, giving it grace and pledges for this on the Cross”44. Do you see? The ecclesial substance lies in the reference to the human nature that he redeems and espouses to himself; and in it, consequently, he redeems and espouses each and every man, “each soul,” as the saint likes to say.

Therefore, Christ communicated to his Mystical Body that holiness and this power to sanctify. He charged his Mystical Body to baptize, to celebrate the memorial of His Death and to forgive sins. Whose sins you forgive, they are forgiven them, and whose sins you retain, they are retained45.

2. Love for the Church

Let us now say how this love for the Church that we say we profess is manifested.

  • By striving to be saints according to the spirit of our Institute:

Only love for Jesus Christ, living in his Mystical Body, in His Church, can overcome the evil that floods our age. And through this love for Christ and for his Mystical Body we must consecrate our “spiritual life to the benefit of the whole Church”46 and dedicate ourselves “to work according to our strength and according to the form of our own vocation, whether by prayer or by apostolic ministry, so that the kingdom of Christ may be established and consolidated in souls, and to spread it throughout the whole world”47.

St. Alphonsus Maria de Liguori, in a very beautiful letter of August 8, 1754, in which he recommended persevering in the first fervor of the congregation he had founded and denounced in some the lack of that spirit, said: “I do not know where these men will end up, because God has called us to this Congregation to make us saints and to save us as saints. He who wants to be saved in the Congregation, but not as a saint, I do not know whether he will be saved”48.

By striving to be saints we will contribute to the holiness of the Church. For our vocation “never has personal sanctification as its end. Even more, an exclusively personal sanctification would not be authentic, because Christ has very intimately united holiness and charity. Thus, those who tend toward personal holiness must do so within the framework of a commitment of service to the life and holiness of the Church. Even the purely contemplative life… carries this ecclesial orientation”49.

St. John of the Cross, a contemplative like no other, was a tireless apostle; we have already mentioned the magnanimity and variety of his apostolic labors. But in his time, too, he saw around him people who were excessively busy and who never quite understood where the efficacy of the apostolic and ministerial work they were carrying out lay. That is why he denounced ‘activism’ and presented the apostolic value of the purest love, and so he affirmed: “a little of this pure love is more precious before God and the soul, and does more good to the Church, even though it seems to be doing nothing, than all those works together”50. You also know that phrase of the saint which our Proper Law quotes: “Let those, then, who are very active, who think to encircle the world with their preaching and exterior works, take note here that they would do much more good to the Church and be much more pleasing to God if they spent even half of that time remaining with God in prayer. Certainly they would then accomplish more, and with less labor… otherwise it is all hammering and doing little more than nothing, and at times even harm”51.

We must resolve firmly—with the will of the third class of men; or with robust courage, as St. John of the Cross said—to attain holiness, particularly through an ever deeper and more consistent practice of the religious vows, most faithful to the spirit of our Institute, always persevering and aware that “if holiness is attainable, it is above all because it is the work of God”52. In this way much more is gained, because of how greatly that little bit of holiness benefits and matters to the Church53.

  • Thinking with the Church:

For this, it is imperative to think and act “always with her, in accordance with the teachings and norms of the Magisterium of Peter and of the Pastors in communion with him”54, since we have been called to be witnesses of ecclesial communion (sentire cum Ecclesia) through “adherence of mind and heart to the Magisterium of the Bishops, and to live it with loyalty and bear witness to it clearly before the People of God”55.

And this is precisely what St. Ignatius exhorts us to do in the 13th rule: To be right in everything, we must always hold that the white that I see, I will believe to be black, if the hierarchical Church so determines it, believing that between Christ our Lord, the bridegroom, and the Church his bride, there is the same spirit that governs and rules us for the salvation of our souls, because it is by the same Spirit and Lord of ours who gave the ten Commandments that our holy mother Church is ruled and governed”56. And it is exactly the same thing that the Directory of Spirituality prescribes for us in number 312.

“Thinking with the Church implies rejecting sectarianism. There must be a disposition of mind and heart in the baptized person, always ready to follow the one whom Jesus Christ himself placed as the visible head of his Church, that is, Peter”57.

All of you know that this has been and continues to be the spirit that has been bequeathed to us: sentire Ecclesiam and sentire cum Ecclesia58. On this depends the supernatural efficacy of all our apostolic activity, and if this is lacking “we would most gravely betray our charism”59. (Give examples urging docility to the bishops, cooperation, supporting their initiatives, participating in the events of the diocese, etc.)

Docility to the living Magisterium of the Church, of all times, as you know very well, is one of the elements attached to the charism that are non-negotiable, and if non-negotiable, it must be something that distinguishes and characterizes us.

We must be aware that “the apostolate is a supernatural reality”60 and that therefore its “fruitfulness will depend on our union with God and with the Church”61. All “our apostolate we always carry out ‘in the name of the Church and by her mandate; we exercise it in communion with her’62”63. To proceed otherwise is contrary to the Will of God.

Now a clarification. “Communion in the Church is not uniformity”64; our little Religious Family will be all the more useful to the Church and to her mission, the greater the respect for its identity65, which—like every gift of the Holy Spirit—has been granted to us so that it may bear fruit for the Lord66. This must be clear. Because we would not be loving the Church if, for false reasons, we got rid of the essential elements that make up our charism, which God himself has inspired for the good of his Church; if we cut back the demands implied in having professed our consecration in this beloved Institute; if we change—under the cloak of zeal—something of the very rich patrimony of our Institute. Call that whatever you like, but it is neither love for the Church nor love for the Institute.

The Constitutions explicitly point out, quoting the Code of Canon Law: “In the exercise of the external apostolate religious are also subject to their own Superiors and must remain faithful to the discipline of the Institute”67.

  • By practicing charity toward God and neighbor in concrete ways

Joining, then, “the perfect love of God with perfect charity toward neighbor,” we must know ourselves to be “totally consecrated to the needs of the Church and of all those in need” and consequently impelled to mission.

If we say that the Incarnate Word loved the Church and gave himself up for her68, and that we want to be like other Christs, then we must dedicate ourselves to the propagation of the Church, “totally, as to our supreme love”69. Witness is key. Without it, evangelization is superficial, in name only, because it does not draw people, it does not convince. Fidelity to God, humble and hidden sacrifice, the many priestly anguishes one keeps to oneself in silence, abandonment to the will of God, and serene fidelity even as one's strength runs out and people forget about one—that is luminous witness.

And in this sense we must insist, saying that one of the most effective ways in which we collaborate with the mission of the Church, and particularly with the evangelization of culture, is precisely by striving, through the love we profess for God, to attain holiness, and by loving God, uniting our will to his, as we have said in so many ways in these Exercises.

The other is by making that love for God concrete in love for neighbor. And in our Institute, because of the missionary life we lead, because of the community life and the family spirit with which we have been blessed, we have countless ways of putting into practice the love we say we have for God.

Likewise, remember that one of the elements attached to the charism, and non-negotiable, is the works of mercy, precisely so that we may be credible witnesses of the love of Christ70. And probably none of you, or most of you, do not work in a Hogarcito with disabled children, but all of you do encounter the poor. That is, people in need in the spiritual, psychological or material sphere, lonely people, who depend on the charity of the Church, whose representatives we are. In this field we have an irreplaceable mission, which derives from the supernatural virtue of charity. These are works that must be supported and in which we must get involved. “Almsgiving is an act of love of neighbor for the love of God, and I believe that here too there is an essential element for having a healthy «sensus Eclesiae»”71. Almsgiving understood not only as something monetary, but rather understood as that readiness to help others and to give oneself; it is the attitude of interior openness toward the other.

As the connecting thread between love of God and love of neighbor, there is the apostolic zeal of which we have spoken earlier, apostolic creativity, and magnificence in works. We must not have a little-kiosk mentality, forgetting that what is precisely ours is “to prolong Christ in families, in education, in the media, in men of thought and in every other legitimate manifestation of human life” 72 and that it also falls to us to evangelize in the “modern areopagi.”

  • By defending the Church

St. Ignatius says in his 9th rule for thinking with the Church: Finally, to praise all the precepts of the Church, keeping the mind ready to seek reasons in her defense and in no way against her73. And in the 11th rule: to define or declare for our times the things necessary for eternal salvation, and the better to refute and expose all errors and all fallacies.

Fulton Sheen said: Today the Mystical Body of Christ has become the target in a game of theological darts. While the secularists say that ‘God is dead,’ the necro-ecclesiologists proclaim that the ‘Church is dead.’ For some the Church is too holy; for others, too human; like Caiaphas, who rejected Christ because He called himself the Son of God, and Pilate, who crucified him because he was not sufficiently ‘one with Caesar’74.

He was writing in the 1960s and 70s. But the situation has not changed today. Therefore, out of love for Christ and for the Church, depository of the truth of Christ, we must preach the truth, define, declare for our times—as St. Ignatius says—the things necessary for eternal salvation, and refute and expose errors, “without fear of the executioners who lie in wait for the Church in every age”75.

St. John Paul II, the lover of the Church, said: “We must bear witness to the truth, even at the price of being persecuted, even at the cost of blood, as Christ himself did […] We will surely encounter difficulties. There is nothing extraordinary about that. It is part of the life of faith. Sometimes the trials are light, at other times very difficult and even dramatic. In trial we may feel alone, but divine grace, the grace of a victorious faith, never abandons us. That is why we can hope for the victorious overcoming of any trial, even the most difficult”76.

On the other hand, there is no love for the Church if one “sacrifices the truth and one's own conscience, claiming to maintain a false peace, not to cross a friend, to avoid some problem or, at times, to gain an advantage through silence or applause”77, because in the end that is negotiating with the world. We must be convinced that our holy religion is truly a help to the world if it contradicts it.

The true life of a Catholic, of a Catholic priest, is not made up of routine and isolated acts of devotion, but of crises, when great choices are set before us. Because then we have the possibility of being one with the Truth of Christ or of being against it. That is why these are wonderful days to be alive.

No one denies the general confusion, the lack of lucidity of some, and the need for those whose task it is to pronounce clearly and forcefully on certain points. But look, out of that same love for the Church, St. Ignatius recommends to us “to support and praise the customs of our superiors […] because, granted that some are not or were not such, to speak against them, whether preaching in public or conversing before the simple people, would engender more murmuring and scandal than profit; and so the people would become indignant against their superiors, whether temporal or spiritual.”

That is why our Proper Law explicitly commands us to “remain deaf when someone speaks to us leaving the Pope aside, or not explicitly in favor of the Pope and of the sound and exact doctrine of the Church: such people are not the planting of the Heavenly Father, but evil offshoots of heresies that produce deadly fruit78”79.

3. Scandals

The last rule that St. Ignatius gives for thinking with the Church says: “Above all, the much serving of God our Lord out of pure love is to be esteemed”80.

Unfortunately, this—for various reasons—does not always happen. That is why St. Joseph Calasanz warned: “Woe to those who destroy by their example what they preach by their word!”81. The risk is always there: like the one who seeks himself, or “the one who seeks to please men more than God”82, the one who seeks praise, recognition, to secure his position, and compromises with the world, or like the one who “chooses apostolates according to their financial return”83, “the one who sees the priesthood as a career ladder” 84, the one who sees and treats people as customers85 and dispatches them without much concern, quickly, etc. And this is not something new, for St. Paul already told the Ephesians: do not grieve the Holy Spirit86.

But it is true that perhaps one of the marks of the Church most attacked in recent times is precisely that of her holiness. That is why I would like to say a word about scandals.

The scandalous behavior of not a few of her members has certainly helped to disfigure in people's minds the beauty of the holiness of the Church, or rather, it represents a trial for people's faith concerning the Church, even for us, precisely because that principle of St. Ignatius is not observed: to serve God our Lord out of pure love. And so we have seen priests, religious, missionaries, even some of high rank in the hierarchy, who are guided by worldly criteria, who seek themselves and not the good of souls, who run after the fashions of the world and its riches, who do not burn with zeal for Christ but live comfortably like idlers, who instead of gathering with Christ seem to scatter87, who mix Christ with pagan deities… and consequently they attack that holiness which is expected of us as ambassadors of Christ and members of his Church.

As early as 1950 Fulton Sheen said: there are too many scandals in the Catholic world. Husbands and wives who are unfaithful to each other; Catholic politicians who are more dishonest than others without religion; Catholic children who steal; young Catholic women who worship the divinities of the pagan world […]; Catholic businessmen who are selfish, hard and completely indifferent to social problems and the rights of workers; etc.88 How many more examples could we add today!

What does this mean? That the Church is now less holy because the malice of sin stands out more, because there is more variety and gravity in sins?

When today we see some who leave the Church, what—does that perhaps prove that the Church is not the Mystical Body of Christ? As if Christ had been less divine when in the gospel we read that they all forsook him and fled.

You know well that this is not so. Today we probably do not see the holiness of the Mystical Body of Christ, just as we would not have noticed the glory in his Physical Body when he was thirsty, for example, at Jacob's well, or when the soldiers ‘played’ with Him in Pilate's Praetorium and insulted him and spat on him. If there were scandals associated with the Physical Life of Christ, to the point that he prayed that his disciples would not be scandalized by Him, why are we surprised that there are scandals in his Body, which is the Church?

We can never expect his Mystical Body, the Church, to be exempt from scandals, because He himself was a stone of stumbling89. It was an enormous scandal for those who knew him to be God to see him crucified and experiencing an apparent defeat, at the moment when his enemies challenged him to give proof of his divinity by coming down from the Cross.

If the human nature of our Lord could endure such physical annihilation as to become a scandal, why think that his Mystical Body, being made up of poor mortals, should remain untouched by scandals? If He allowed hunger, thirst and death to afflict his physical body, why should He not allow mystical and moral failings, such as the loss of faith, sin, heresy, schisms, sacrileges and defections, to wound his Mystical Body? That these things happen does not deny the intimately divine nature of the Church, just as the crucifixion of Christ did not deny that He was God. If our hands are dirty, that does not mean that our whole body is contaminated. Analogously, scandals cannot destroy the “substantial” holiness of the Mystical Body, just as the crucifixion could not destroy the integrity of the physical body of Christ. The Old Testament prophecy, fulfilled on Calvary, said that not one of his bones would be broken. Yes, it said that his flesh would hang from Him like bloody shreds; that the wounds, as if they were sorrowful mouths, would express his sufferings with blood; that the pierced hands and feet would let flow torrents of life and redemption, but his “Substance,” his bones, would remain intact. The same happens with the Church. Not one of her bones will be broken; the substance of her discipline will always be just, notwithstanding the rebellion of some of her disciples; the substance of her faith will always be divine, notwithstanding the carnality of some among her faithful. Her wounds will never be mortal, because her soul is holy and immortal, with the immortality of divine love, which, in tongues of living fire, descended upon her Body on the day of Pentecost.

I quote Fulton Sheen here: “And now, to speak of one of the scandals, let me ask you: ‘How is it possible that a man as wicked as Alexander VI could have been the infallible Vicar of Christ and Head of his Church?’ The solution is in the Gospel. The Lord changed Simon's name to Peter and made him the rock on which he would build that which He called his Church. He made, at that time, a distinction that very few have ever noticed: He distinguished between ‘infallibility,’ or immunity from error, and ‘impeccability,’ or immunity from sin. Infallibility is the impossibility of teaching evil; impeccability is the impossibility of doing evil. The Lord made Peter infallible but not impeccable.

Immediately after having confirmed that Peter held in his power the keys of Heaven and the authority to bind or loose, the Lord told the Apostles that He had to go to Jerusalem and die there90. And poor, weak Peter, so human and proud of his recently received authority, approaches Jesus and begins to rebuke him: Far be it from You, Lord! This shall not happen to you91. And then Jesus, turning, said to Peter: Get behind me, Satan; you are a scandal to me; for you do not understand the things of God, but those of men92.

So we see how a little before, Peter was called “Rock” and now “Satan.”

With this Christ is saying to Peter: “In your condition as the rock on which I build my Church, whatever you say with the help of God will be like rock preserved from error; but as Simon, son of John, as a man, you are so frail in your flesh and so prone to sin that you can become like Satan. In your ministry you are infallible, but as a man, Simon, you are a sinner. As Peter, the power you possess is my handiwork; as Simon, your weakness for sin is due to yourself.” And we too must keep this very much in mind.

Is it so difficult to grasp this distinction between the man and his function? If a police officer, while directing traffic, raises his hand to stop us, we would stop even knowing that he beats his wife at home. Why? Because we distinguish between his functions as a representative of the law and his person.

I am sure—says Fulton Sheen—that the Lord permitted Peter's fall, after having granted him the gift of supreme power, to remind him and all his successors that infallibility was necessarily part of his function, but that virtue he had to win by his personal effort, supported by the grace of God.

Whether Peter's voice be sweet or monotonous or persuasive, or even mispronounced or ungrammatical, we do not take into account the tone of his voice but the message he announces to us: “speak, O Lord, for your servant is listening!93That must be our attitude.

It can be said that surely those who believe they know everything about the few bad successors of Peter know nothing about the many good pontiffs the Catholic Church has had. The infamy of one man who occupies a position of authority can overshadow millions of saints. How many of those who investigate the Vicars of Christ during the Renaissance, stopping there, delve into the history of so many others during the remaining two thousand years? How many of those people who take it upon themselves to exploit the figure of those few unworthy popes have admitted that, of the first thirty-five successors of Peter, thirty-two of them were martyrs and three were exiled? How many of those who concentrate their attention on the bad example of a few ever admit that of the 266 successors of Peter (including Pope Francis), 82 have been canonized as saints and each of the first 35 popes was canonized? How many are there who can match St. John Paul II, for example, in wisdom and prudence? Whoever attacks so numerous a legion of martyrs and saints and scholars must be completely convinced that he is without sin to dare to raise his hand against the few who revealed the human side of so high an office.

To all these critics, Fulton Sheen says that, if they are holy, pure, and without fault, let them throw their stones. The Lord said that only one who is free of sin may throw the first stone. But if they do not find themselves in that situation, then let them leave judgment to God. And if they are indeed free of sin, then they belong to a race different from ours, because we feel welling up from the depths of our heart a cry that says: Lord, have mercy on me, a sinner.

Returning to the scandal of bad Catholics, let us remember that the Lord did not expect every member of the Church to be more perfect than the apostles were. That is why He said that on the last day he would cast the rotten fish out of his net. Some Catholics may be bad, but that does not prove that the Mystical Body of Christ is rubbish, in the same way that the fact that some compatriots have sold themselves to another country does not make all our countrymen a race of traitors. Our faith increases the sense of responsibility, but it does not force us to obey; it increases guilt, but it does not prevent sin. If some Catholics are bad, if some priests, bishops, missionaries are bad, it is not because they are members of the Church, but on the contrary: because they do not know how to live under the light and grace of the Church.

The psychology of those who are scandalized by bad Catholics is interesting. Because it means that, deep down, they expected something better from us. If those who are bad take delight in scandal, it is because they think that they have more authority to sin than anyone else who falls. One never hears it said: “he is a perverse relativist,” or: “he is a scandalous humanist,” or “he is an adulterous gnostic,” because to begin with no one expects anything better from them. The horror one feels at the fall of some is proportional to the height (or degree) of virtue that was expected of them. So we must be grateful for the recognition they give us in the face of the fall of some members of the Church, and for their not tolerating in us the things they tolerate in others.

May we ask those who are scandalized by the weaknesses of the Church what point of perfection they expect the Church to reach for them to become members of the Church? Because evidently, some agree with the teachings of the Church but are horrified by the behavior of some. Do you realize that if the Church were as perfect as they wish, there would be no place for them? And Fulton Sheen presents us with the following situation: Imagine for a moment that the Mystical Body of Christ had no moral weakness; imagine that a monk had never broken his priestly vows to marry a nun and start a new religion, as has already happened; imagine that a bishop had never been anything other than a skillful administrator, that no priest had ever given an unedifying witness, that no friar were obese, that no Sister were ever impatient with a child, and that holiness were automatic like an odometer; suppose that no one had ever been a cause of scandal, so that those outside the Church might justify the way they live… would that have been the Church the Lord had in mind, if He said that the weeds would be reaped with the grain, and that some of the children of the Kingdom would be cast out of its borders? If the Church were perfect, as those who are scandalized would like her to be, her perfection would condemn us, who are not saints. So lofty an ideal often repels instead of attracting. If the Church were holy in that way, she could not attract poor mortals like us. Hope would be lost for those who are not saints or who are in sin. The Mystical Body with all its members perfect would be a stumbling block. And then, instead of our being scandalized by the Church, the Church would be scandalized by us, which is much worse.

If the life of the Mystical Body had been triumphal, a luminous transfiguration on the mountaintop but far removed from the sorrows and ills of humanity, she could never be the comforter of the afflicted and refuge of sinners. The Church is not a distant and abstract idea, but a mother, and although she is stained with the dust of her long journey through the centuries, and although some among her children have wounded her in body and afflicted her in soul, she has kept joy in her heart for all her children. There is joy in her eyes for the faith she has preserved; there is understanding in her soul, because she understands human frailty and knows how to nourish us to bring us back to life. And these things reveal to us why our Lord chose as his first Vicar, not a holy man like John, but a weak man, prone to error, like Peter, so that through his frailty he, with the Church of which he was Head, might understand the weakness of his flock, be the Apostle of mercy, and, in the fullest meaning of the word, the true Vicar of the Savior and Redeemer of the world, who came not to save the just but the sinner.

Yes, it is true, in general the world is right. We Catholics are not truly as we should be, and the world is as it is because we Catholics are as we are. The Lord said: If salt loses its taste, how shall it be made to recover its flavor?94 . We have not disappointed the world but Christ; and failing Him, we fail the world.

[Peroratio]

We must always be convinced of what the Incarnate Word himself assured us: You are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it95. And “just as what Peter believed in Christ endures forever, in the same way what Christ instituted in Peter will endure forever”96.

This means that we need not fear that infallibility will vanish, nor that God could be dethroned, nor that transubstantiation will perish, nor that the sacraments will disappear. What should concern us is that the world may be led by the hand of error, that barbarism may reign, that the family may perish, that the moral law may disappear. And so that this does not happen we must work as men whose vocation has no meaning apart from God and his promises, with great courage and firmness in our faith, to make Jesus Christ reign. He is the fullness of all authentically human life and culture. And it is for this that God has gathered us in this Institute.

Therefore, ours will always be: neither Christ without the Church, nor the Church without Christ. And that other saying: Christ yes, the Church too.

May the Mother of the Church find us each day more dedicated to loving and serving, and to making others love and serve, Jesus Christ, in his Mystical Body, which is the Church.

During Mass, Saint John Paul II says, a priest “lends his voice to Christ, repeating the sacred words of institution.”1 That is why the priest’s voice—whether loud or soft, intelligible or not, whether the words are spoken with unction and clarity or not—is of great importance in the liturgy.

The Institute’s proper law expresses it this way: “Visible elements are of great importance in the liturgy. Speech must have its proper rhythm, neither fast nor slow, with a voice neither harsh nor lifeless. It must be spoken expressively, without theatricality, with sincerity, clarity, precision, originality and unction … without forgetting that through it God makes himself present and manifest.”2 The priest’s voice is so important that, if the words are pronounced in the way just described, the Directory says that Holy Mass will have been fruitful. It then cites Dominicae Cenae, whose published English text begins: “Eucharistic worship matures and grows”. The passage calls for humble, intelligible and reverent delivery, without haste, so that those present respond with recollection and devotion to the mystery being celebrated.3

In keeping with this, GIRM 32 requires the priest’s presidential prayers to be audible and intelligible, with everyone listening attentively; other prayer, singing and instrumental music must cease while he speaks.4 See the linked published English paragraph.

Fr. Buela knew that, when spoken in persona Christi, the words of consecration are divine words and receive their power from divine power. Thus, by divine power, as he speaks he also acts: the words are efficacious because they accomplish what they signify.5 He therefore attached the greatest importance to the priest’s voice, not only in preaching—a subject we leave for another article—but above all when pronouncing the parts of the Mass, especially the consecration. He was convinced that an authentic liturgical celebration expresses the sacredness of the Eucharistic Mystery, and that this is reflected—or ought to be reflected—in the words and actions of the celebrating priest as he intercedes before God both with and for the faithful.6 Yet “affectation”7 must always be avoided, whether in the solemnity of the words or in the slowness with which they are pronounced; that is, one must not fall into theatricality. For this reason, and in complete accord with what the Church prescribes, our Founder left us practical, concrete counsels that all the Institute’s priests can follow. For example:

“The words that must be pronounced: They must be pronounced clearly, for example, in the Canon.”8

“In concelebration, when it is their part, the concelebrants all speak together in a low voice, so that the principal concelebrant can be heard.”9

“There are also parts that are said ‘secretly,’10 not mumbling, not whispering, but without any sound at all.”11

“Ordinarily, do not say the ‘offertory’ prayers aloud. If there is no offertory chant, its prayers may be pronounced aloud.”12

We recall here that the Preface is sung or said by the principal celebrant alone; the Sanctus, on the other hand, is sung or said by all the concelebrants together with the people and the singers.13

The Church also excludes assigning portions of the Eucharistic Prayer to a deacon, lay minister or the assembled faithful. Redemptionis Sacramentum 52 supplies the published English instruction; see the related reference in note 15.

It is well known that the Eucharistic Prayer forms the central high point of the celebration.14 Its proclamation belongs to the priest in virtue of ordination.15 The following identifies passages assigned to the principal celebrant, using short excerpts of the published English incipits:

  • In Eucharistic Prayer I, the passages beginning “To you, therefore …”, “Therefore, Lord …”, and “Through whom …” belong to the principal celebrant.16
  • In Eucharistic Prayer II, the passage beginning “You are indeed Holy …” belongs to the principal celebrant.17
  • In Eucharistic Prayer III, the passage likewise begins “You are indeed Holy …” and belongs to the principal celebrant.18
  • In Eucharistic Prayer IV, the principal celebrant says the passage beginning “We give you praise …” through the words ending “… sanctify creation to the full”. The final doxology belongs to the principal celebrant, with the other concelebrants if desired.19

The Directory of Liturgical Life states: The priest, “thanks to the faculty granted by the sacrament of Holy Orders, accomplishes the consecration. With the power that comes to him from the Christ of the Upper Room, he says: ‘This is my body, which will be given up for you … This is the chalice of my blood, which will be poured out for you.’ The priest pronounces these words or, rather, places his mouth and voice at the disposal of the One who pronounced them in the Upper Room and willed that they be repeated from generation to generation by all those who, in the Church, share ministerially in his priesthood.” The Directory also cites the teaching of Dominicae Cenae 8: the priest’s sacramental action identifies him with Christ, the eternal High Priest, whose sacrifice cannot be replaced by anyone else’s. The full published English passage is linked in note 20.20

“That is why we must always,” our Founder advised us, “strive to please God with our interior dispositions, since it is of no use to praise him with our lips if our minds and interior dispositions are far from him. […] Our principal dispositions must be: ‘The creature’s complete submission to the Creator, the conformity of our will with God’s will, and the fullest possible identification with the sentiments of Jesus Christ.’21”22

“Never limit yourself simply to saying Mass.”23

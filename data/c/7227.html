“The devil you do not know”
Part II

We publish here the second part of the Conference published in April. Here we present the first strategy that the devil ordinarily uses in his way of acting, which is deception. Later we will go on to set out the other three strategies. For this whole work we draw on the excellent book by Louis J. Cameli, The Devil You Know Not, which we follow freely to a large extent in developing the argument. [The translation from the English original is ours].

His strategies: the ordinary work of the devil

Let us now see concretely what the common strategies of the enemy of our souls are. We can identify four of them: deception, division, diverting or distracting the soul from its purpose (from its objective, in short: from the end for which it was created) and finally, discouragement. Today we will speak of the first of them:

  • Deception – Jesus says: He (the devil) was a murderer from the beginning, and did not stand in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he utters a lie, he speaks from what is his own, because he is a liar and the father of lies1.

The devil inverts reality, that is, he turns things around, he presents them upside down. And then he proceeds to present that “inversion of things” as a “truth come from God.” In other words: he lies. Very often that lie has to do with promises which, of course, he will not keep, nor does he want to keep, either now or ever.

The devil uses very deceptive promises to tempt us, to derail us. That is nothing new. It has been so since Adam and Eve: The serpent said to the woman: ‘No, you will not die. God knows very well that when you eat of that tree, your eyes will be opened and you will be like gods, knowing good and evil.’ There the lie worked. You will remember that well-known temptation of Jesus, steeped in deceptive promises, with which the devil had no success at all. It is in the Gospel of St. Matthew: The devil then took him to a very high mountain; from there he showed him all the kingdoms of the world in all their splendor, and said to him: ‘I will give you all this, if you fall down and worship me.’ Jesus answered him: ‘Begone, Satan, for it is written: You shall worship the Lord your God, and Him only shall you serve.’ Then the devil left him, and angels came to minister to him”2.

Notice how all these promises are rooted in the future. The devil seems to say: ‘there is something further ahead that I can assure you you are going to like and that will suit you really well.’ Promises about the future, positive results guaranteed, the promise that it will be a “suffering free” experience, consequences controlled or controllable… all these things are quite attractive. One feels much better knowing how things are going to turn out, even when that feeling is based on something improbable and, definitively, false. One feels much better moving forward having some kind of control over things and circumstances than walking only in trust and hope, without any control over what is going to happen.

That is why the first lesson here is to realize that very often the devil's deception is joined to a promise that has to do with the future. That must alert us and put us on guard when we look toward the future, when we make plans or when we are daydreaming about what ‘could be.’ Listen to what C. S. Lewis says in “The Screwtape Letters”: “Nearly all vices are rooted in the Future. Gratitude looks to the Past and love to the Present; fear, avarice, lust and ambition look ahead.” All this means that we expose ourselves to being deceived when we want to anticipate what is going to happen. The deceptions can be about various things, of course: “how happy I'm going to be when I no longer live with this one and live with that one”; “how great it's going to be when I work in such and such a diocese”; some imagine ‘all that they will do’ and ‘all that they are going to enjoy’ if they leave the Institute, if they are promoted to some office that gives them ‘renown’; they imagine how secure and satisfied they are going to be if they are assigned this mission and taken out of where they are, if they are sent another superior, if suddenly the Institute did not have ‘the drawbacks’ it has…

There are also other assurances about the future, equally deceptive, which falsely promise to hide what we prefer to keep secret. The desire to maintain privacy in one's life has an absolutely positive tone. We think of privacy as a form of legitimate reserve that keeps for oneself what would otherwise be something discourteous or rude in front of others. Although an earlier age might have equated privacy with refinement, today privacy has a less attractive aspect. For many, privacy means blocking off parts of their life that they simply want to keep in reserve, generally because they are not exactly proud of their actions or because they fear that these actions may have negative repercussions and disrupt their way of life. There are many things that people in general prefer to block off: the use of pornography, the propensity to take things from work that do not belong to them, etc. But it happens to us too: we want to hide that we have the regular habit of ignoring requests for help, that we have bad thoughts, that we accept ‘credit’ for things we do not do, that deep in our heart—and even though we do not want to admit it—we harbor a certain ‘hope of glory,’ of being vindicated… And where does the devil come into these private matters? The devil is active in various ways. Blocking off or bracketing areas of our life in order to keep them hidden, and not seeking help or a solution, is a deception that belongs in a notable way to the evil one.

St. Ignatius describes this phenomenon very well in rule of discernment number 13, which is found in [326] of the book of the Exercises and which you know very well:

[Our enemy] acts like a false lover in wanting to remain secret and not be discovered; for just as the false man who, speaking with evil intent, solicits the daughter of a good father or the wife of a good husband, wants his words and insinuations to be kept secret; and the contrary displeases him greatly, when the daughter reveals to her father or the wife to her husband his vain words and perverse intention, because he easily infers that he will not be able to succeed in the undertaking he has begun; in the same way, when the enemy of human nature brings his wiles and insinuations to the just soul, he wants and desires that they be received and kept in secret; but it weighs heavily on him when the soul reveals them to its good confessor or to another spiritual person who knows his deceits and malice: because he infers that, his deceits being laid bare, he will not be able to succeed with the wicked plan he had begun.

That is the strategy of deceptive concealment, to which the devil adds the promise (again deceptive) of mitigating the evil he offers as a temptation. The promise is simply that the evil will not be known by others, and the mitigation is that ‘in reality it does not matter much in this case.’ The promise is false and the mitigation is false. If it were advantageous for the devil that our fault be known, of course he will make it known! On top of that he can falsify, confuse others, scandalize, and make the person who is exposed become engulfed in intense sorrows of depression. This is exactly the case with the stories of sexual abuse of minors by priests. What seemed to be sealed forever, out of public view and a closed case, through personal shame and institutional connivance (collusion), at a certain point ‘that secret’ came to the surface and quickly, and in some cases forcibly, caused confusion among people about the Church, provoking scandal after scandal. That shameful truth, now public, unleashed waves of destructive depression among the perpetrators, the victims, and countless good souls in the Church.

The mitigation promised by the devil, “it really doesn't matter much,” in this case of clerical sexual abuse, but also in other instances in which the devil hides evil, is clearly revealed as false. Whether the evil is public or apparently private and hidden, it has an impact and exacts a price. The truth is that we are so interrelated with others and so interdependent that we can never ‘isolate’ ourselves completely from the rest of humanity, nor from the impact of others on our life and of our impact on the lives of others.

To put it in a few words: the devil does not keep his promises because he is the Liar. Identifying this reality, however, does not automatically free us from the influence of the one who habitually inverts the truth and seeks to entrap us with his distortions. We are vulnerable to this and therefore we must be alert.

Another aspect of the strategy of deception on the part of the Liar with a capital L: the devil deceives us by entangling us in all sorts of complexities. Be careful: this has nothing to do with something that is in itself difficult to understand. Since I am not a mathematician, I may find it very difficult to understand number theory and graphs and things of that kind. By this I mean that there does exist a difficulty that is inherent in the subject matter, which is studied precisely because it has an intelligibility, a capacity to be known and understood with the appropriate tools. In the case that now concerns us, what the devil tries to do, through the complicatedness of his presentation, of his logic, of his argumentation, is to hide a very simple truth. He seeks to confuse us and to hide the fundamental truth from us. All those complicated tangles are related to deception and have to do with the free choice to complicate things so that they may remain obscure and, in the end, part of the deception. In this one sees the hand of the devil: intelligence joined to a malicious will.

The devil is more than capable of bringing confusing complexities into our lives and trying to hide the truth about who we are and about the call God has made to us. Various layers are superimposed, for example, questioning our motivations, raising worries about how others perceive us or what they think of our projects, setting before us aaall the possible difficulties we might come across… all this serves only to block us or at least to make us stumble on our way, injecting deception by means of complications. And he uses this strategy especially when it comes to projects that are going to do a great deal of good; a concrete example is when it comes to works of mercy or when many souls are going to benefit.

We must open our eyes and realize that the devil is a sworn enemy of the plain and simple truth. Precisely because it is plain and simple, the truth will be known and will be the path that leads us to God, who is the Truth, and the devil does not want that. The enemy—listen carefully—will always be interested in hiding the truth by entangling it in complexities that do not allow us to see it, or use it, or even speak of it.

That the devil is a liar and his task is to deceive is also shown in expressiveness. In fact, a style of expressiveness in language (loquacity) and in gesture can be masking deeper realities. Put another way: that a person is loquacious, exceedingly expressive, is no guarantee that he is revealing an interior truth. The devil can use this for deceptive purposes. And one of the most interesting ‘circumstances’ is that he uses it to hide a religious truth or one that has to do with religion. The devil can, and in fact does, orchestrate religious observances, not for a positive end, of course. How many times have we seen that the devil uses intense expressions of religiosity that have little or nothing to do with an interiorized and genuine faith. How much fanaticism some priests can show regarding certain religious practices, but afterwards they fail in charity and, without any pastoral tact, ridicule, criticize, get angry when someone does not do as they like, which greatly contradicts the interior faith that must animate us.

The devil's deceptive tactic includes, then, a pernicious encouragement of religious practices as purely external observances without a corresponding interior commitment.

Another area in which the devil uses the tactic of deception is the ambiguity of sexual relationships. We are not speaking here of lust, which clearly speaks the language of selfishness. The more subtle attraction, and the direction in which the devil is wont to lead souls, is to make them think that they have an apparently intimate relationship, or at least that is how it appears outwardly. This is the case of those who cohabit. They are not promiscuous, and therefore they do not see themselves as lustful. They believe they are experiencing great intimacy, but such an assessment is based only on the external, on the physical. In reality, that physical closeness neither represents nor expresses a deeper intimacy, because it lacks the full commitment to be faithful and to lead a life in communion; that relationship remains confined only to the provisional and limited. And so we see in this example how an external expression replaces the truth of the substance.

Another manifestation of the strategy of deception turns out to be: “information instead of truth.” We live in a world driven by information (information-driven society). It is obvious: just think of the computers that store and process such an enormous amount of information divided into millions of little pieces. Information well handled is a marvel: business is done more efficiently, schedules are better synchronized, people have the possibility of being more in communication with others. Now, the truth of our lives is not tied to little pieces of information that can be shuffled (as cards are shuffled) and dealt out in different directions. The truth of our life is manifested in our identity, which is stable, and began when we were born and accompanies us throughout our whole life until our last breath. And that truth of our lives is manifested in our freedom. A freedom that allows us not only to choose, but to give ourselves to others out of love (or not to do so) and to affirm our existence as a gift received (or not to do so). The truth that makes us free leads us to truth itself, to a truth beyond our imagination, a transcendent truth that is grounded in the Truth with a capital T. And that is God.

Information that supplants the truth or ignores the guidance of the truth is deceptive and dangerous. When all those little pieces of information mask the truth, disfiguring it, things take on a dangerous color, and that is the devil's terrain. We see it in so many who show themselves on Facebook or on “Talk shows,” presenting themselves in a way that does not correspond to real life. They offer information about themselves, but not the truth about themselves. They tell you the country they live in, what they do, how old they are, their particular interests… but the truth about the person never emerges from those little pieces of information. And you will be saying to yourselves: ‘Well, Father, but we neither have a personal Facebook page nor go around checking other people's Facebooks.’ Perfect. However, notice how this also affects us in another sphere. The devil can easily, and for his own convenience, use the confusion between information about persons and the truth about those same persons to sow seeds of discord and even of great antagonism. How many times have we heard things said about other religious, and then he is sent to the community where one is, and right from the start one has a certain mistrust of that person, or underestimates him, or even avoids him… and worse if they are superiors, because one does not even take the time to get to know the person, gravely wounding that priest in some cases and even causing great division and turmoil in the community.

St. John of the Cross has a magnificent teaching about this in his first precaution against the world: “Think nothing of them [of the other religious/priests], deal with nothing of theirs, neither good nor bad… and if you do not observe this, you will not know how to be a religious, nor will you be able to reach holy recollection or free yourself from imperfections.” And further on he says: “The third precaution is very necessary so that you may know how to guard yourself in the convent from all harm regarding the religious; and many, by not observing it, not only lost the peace and good of their soul, but came, and ordinarily come, to fall into great evils and sins. It is this: that you guard with all care against setting your thought, and still less your word, on what happens in the community; what it is or has been, nor on any religious in particular, not on his character, not on his manner, not on his affairs, however grave they may be, neither under the color of zeal nor of remedy, except to the one to whom it rightly belongs, saying it at the proper time; and never be scandalized or amazed at things you see or come to know, striving to keep your soul in forgetfulness of all that.

For if you want to look at anything, even if you live among angels, many things will seem to you not good, because you do not understand their substance (it is our keeping hold of that little piece of information which does not tell me the whole truth about that person). […] So that you may understand that, even if you live among devils, God wants you to live among them in such a way that you do not even turn the head of your thought toward their affairs, but leave them entirely, striving to keep your soul pure and whole in God, without a thought of this or that hindering you.

And for this, take it as certain that in convents and communities there will never be lacking something to stumble over, since there are never lacking devils who try to bring down the saints, and God permits it in order to exercise and test them.

And if you do not guard yourself, as has been said, as if you were not in the house, you will not know how to be a religious, however much you do, nor reach holy nakedness and recollection, nor free yourself from the harms that are in this; because if you do not do so, however good your aim and zeal, the devil will catch you in one thing or another, and you are well caught already when you give room to distracting the soul in anything of this”3.

Another manifestation of this strategy of deception that the tempter uses is to use lies and deceptions as remedies for pain. The devil leads souls to lie either to avoid suffering or to find relief in their suffering. There are thousands of examples, and they can range from the smallest to the greatest: like women who lie about their age as a way of coping with the fact that they are growing old and all that this entails; or they can be terribly greater, like the lie of an entire government. But we religious also fall into this trap: like the one who lies about his achievements to relieve himself of the pain of disappointments and lack of self-esteem. Like the one who exaggerates his ‘spiritual sufferings’ to console himself with the thought that he is advancing through the nights of the soul, when in reality those sufferings are due to his lukewarmness. And it sometimes happens that we spend more energy trying to twist the truth, to accommodate it to our liking, than in trying to accept or resolve a difficult reality. The devil strives to instill in us, by suggestion, the idea of an “easier” way instead of the truth, to make us believe that lying is a more attractive option. How many times—even with religious—has he made them think that it is better to lie, even if there are painful consequences, because the raw truth, just as it is, is even more painful. And even worse, the devil makes us believe that the lie works, that is, that we can create another version of reality or soften the real circumstances of our life. That in itself is false! In the long run, lies do not work! Lies are fictitious constructions of reality. How many religious ‘pretend’ to be one thing before their superiors only to obtain an office of greater importance, to be sent to one place or another, or before their spiritual directors to obtain this or that permission, to obtain approval… and afterwards they themselves cannot cope with what they obtained from their superiors! Because they built on air, on soap bubbles, because they do not have the solidity that comes from the truth. At some point, my dear friends, all that has been built on fallacies will come crashing to the ground.

And there is no lack of religious who lie to ‘protect’ themselves. Listen carefully: the lie protects no one! Neither oneself nor others. The devil's deception lies in the fact that he always offers the lie as a remedy for suffering. He makes us follow an incredible logic of convenience, until he convinces us that it will be easier this way. And that is the real temptation, the real offer.

Without doubt, the possibility of sparing ourselves some pain captures our attention and makes us doubly vulnerable to the devil's suggestions. And another thing about this is that the devil's most effective attacks are not direct, but oblique and evasive; those are the ones that work best.

Another criterion for identifying the presence of the evil one and his deceptions is that the presence of Christ is always marked by love and truth. The absence of love, and of its correlative, which is truth, is a clear sign of the enemy. Where love is lacking, truth is lacking. On the other hand, truth—whatever that truth may be—cut off from love ceases to be truth and becomes an apparent truth or pure deception. St. Edith Stein expresses this very well, giving us this great advice: “do not accept anything as truth if it lacks love, and do not accept as love anything that lacks truth; one without the other becomes a destructive lie.” Something to keep in mind, isn't it?

Sometimes we ourselves say to someone, “I'm going to give you my honest opinion,” and we do it without any hesitation, without charity, without any concern for how what we say is going to affect that person. Perhaps I am giving reliable information, but information that is also debatable, that is based on what I see externally and may be deceptive and not really true. This kind of “honest feedback” insinuates that what I say defines the person. It insinuates that your identity, what you are worth, your place in the world is reduced to what I present to you in my very narrow understanding. If someone is told: “look, your sermon was a disaster, people were falling asleep,” perhaps he will never want to preach again. If that ever happens to you, realize that this information that is thrown at me like that, and that falls on me like a bucket of cold water, does not define me as a person. Part of the devil's deception is to make the part look as if it were the whole. Losing a match is not losing the championship!

At an even more personal level, I cannot, in my eagerness to be truthful, say to someone, “you're in a bad way,” because I saw him cry and then carry on through life as if nothing had happened. That can be crushing for the soul. Very probably that person already knows that he is in a bad way. The question is what I do to help that person. The Jew who was lying on the road and was helped by the Samaritan already knew that he was in a bad way and that if no one helped him he was going to die there. There was no need for anyone to tell him. What was needed was for someone to help him get better, to recover. And that is where charity comes in. Do you see? Truth always goes together with charity; otherwise it becomes cruelty. And if it catches someone at a bad moment, it can be deadly.

Think of the case in which a doctor tells a patient that the results of the biopsy were not good, that he has stage-four cancer and that the chances of a cure are minimal or nonexistent. The doctor is a specialist in the field, and routinely reports this type of news to his patients. To bear the emotional burden that this kind of news implies, the physician abstracts himself from the human context and simply reports the facts, what they found in the examination. It does not matter to him whether the patient has children or not, whether he is a newly ordained priest, or whether he is young or old. Obviously there is no loving concern or solicitude on the part of the doctor for the whole person, and that is why the patient receives the news as decisive, as if what now defines him is the cancer he suffers from. And that is not true! That is disconnecting the illness from the rest of the picture and keeping only that little piece. Is that clear?

I think we realize that the work of the father of lies in our lives is not only ordinary (in the sense of routine) but extraordinary, because of how intricate and refined it can become and also because of the scale of his work. What we have considered here in no way represents the totality of the manifestations of his strategy of deception, but it helps us to be alert to the work of the enemy in our lives.

Now, everyone here knows that one cannot speak of the devil without speaking of the victory of Jesus Christ over the devil in all his forms. In Christ, who is the Way, the Truth and the Life, we find the hope and confidence to keep going forward. In Him we find the specific means and the grace we need to free ourselves from the stratagems of deception that we may encounter.

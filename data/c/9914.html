NATURE

What, then, is humility? Although it may not seem so to us, this virtue consists in the right consideration of what we are; that is why St. Thomas defines it as the virtue that inclines us to restrain the disordered appetite for our own excellence, giving us a just knowledge of our littleness and misery before God. A just knowledge, neither more nor less: what we really are. That is why, when we contemplate the considerations the saints made in order to foster it, we must understand, strictly speaking, that it is not that they were exaggerating, but rather that they were the ones who understood the truth of the creature before the Creator. St. Francis Borgia devoted two hours of meditation each day to self-knowledge and self-contempt, because he had learned that he must never cease to consider his miseries; and so, even before these examples, how much the knowledge of our pride ought to grow.

St. Bernard: It is a true knowledge by which one knows oneself and holds oneself in low esteem. That is why the best definition is the well-known formula of St. Teresa: humility is walking in truth. Man, St. Thomas said, can be summed up in two things: what he has from God, and what he has from himself; from God he has everything that pertains to goodness and perfection, since, as we know, all created goodness or perfection is a participation in the divine and uncreated; and from himself −he says− he has only imperfection and defect. This is what we are, this is what we contribute to the work of God: imperfection and defect; and this is no exaggeration, it is the plain truth. Hence St. Augustine says in the Soliloquies: “If there is any good, small or great, it comes from You, for from us nothing but evil can proceed,” and elsewhere: “Whoever counts his own merits, what does he do but count Your gifts?” That is why to be humble is to be wise, because it is to consider the truth about man. Whoever does not understand that he is misery and nothingness −said St. Teresa− understands nothing and lives in a lie; and further on she will say: “if humility is not considered, all is lost.”

In order to learn, we must always repeat that prayer of St. Augustine: “May I know myself and may I know You”; which is what St. Francis of Assisi always asked of God: “Who are You and who am I?”, because he was amazed at the greatness and goodness he saw in God, and at the same time at the unworthiness and misery he discovered in himself. This is why the saints humbled themselves to the very depths: the more they knew God, the poorer and the more full of defects they saw themselves. The proud, by the very fact of being deprived of light, lose the enormous grace that God grants to the humble, that is, the grace of recognizing their lowliness and their truth.

The holy man, however many virtues he may have, will always have to recognize, first, that he possesses all those goods because God has given them to him, what do you have that you have not received1; and second, that all those created gifts are nothing beside the majesty of God; created gifts are like grains of dust before the infinite perfection of God.

EXCELLENCE

Now, despite its greatness, humility is not the greatest of all the virtues. Above it are the theological virtues, the intellectual virtues, and even justice. But St. Thomas calls it the fundamental virtue, because it lays the foundations for the moral edifice. That is why it is an indispensable virtue, the greatest remedy against pride. A man who neglects to practice it may as well renounce the life of heaven right now. God resists the proud and gives his grace to the humble2, says the apostle St. James. The whole edifice of the virtues has holy humility as its foundation. That is why without humility one cannot take a single step in the spiritual life. God is the supreme Truth, and He cannot tolerate anyone voluntarily placing himself outside of it. St. Augustine said: “there is no higher way than the way of charity, but only the humble set foot upon it.”

The higher the edifice of the spiritual life that we wish to raise with the grace of God, the deeper must be the foundations of humility upon which it is to be built.

Let us listen to some texts of St. Teresa:

And since this whole edifice is founded on humility, the closer one has come to God, the further this virtue must advance, and if not, all is lost.

This whole foundation of prayer is founded on humility, and the more a soul lowers itself in prayer, the more God raises it up. I do not remember His having granted me any very signal favor except when I was undone at seeing myself so wretched.

And if they do not resolve upon this, have no fear that they will make much progress, because, as I have said, the foundation of this whole edifice is humility, and if this is not very genuine, the Lord, even for your own good, will not wish to raise it very high, lest it all come crashing to the ground.

If all this is true for any Christian, it is all the more so for us religious. According to St. Bernard, the more elevated one is in dignity −as is the case with religious− the more humble one must be; otherwise, should he fall into sin, his fall will be all the deeper for being hurled from a higher place. That is why St. Augustine said: placed in the most sublime office, you need a more excellent humility. And before that our Lord had declared: Let the greatest among you become as the least3. More than anyone, we must follow the examples of Our Lord, the humble one par excellence, practicing all the degrees.

THE PRACTICE OF HUMILITY

The saints speak of the degrees of humility in different ways. For St. Anselm it is necessary to recognize oneself as worthy of contempt, to grieve over it, to confess it simply, to persuade others of it, to bear patiently being told so, to bear patiently being treated as vile, and to rejoice in it. The substance of humility −said St. Thomas of Villanova− is the definitive death of our self-love.

There are four things, says St. Alphonsus, that we must strive for in order to be truly humble, not in name but in reality.

1° To abhor pride: it is the queen and mother of all vices and sins, because it is the root and beginning of all of them. It was the sin of the angels and the sin of the first man. Pride is the prelude to great ruin4, says the book of Proverbs. St. Gregory says: “pride is a seedbed of impurities, because the flesh hurls into hell those whom haughtiness exalts”. In itself the devil does not fear the proud; St. Joseph Calasanz, speaking of the proud priest, said that “he is in the hands of the devil like a ball that he throws and casts to the ground whenever he pleases.”

St. Thomas points out four forms of it: (1) attributing to oneself the goods one has received from God, (2) believing that we have received them on account of our own merits, (3) boasting of goods one does not possess, and (4) wishing to appear as the sole possessor of such goods, with contempt for others.

2° Not to glory in the good one has done: St. Jerome says that the highest mountains are the most battered by the gales; that is why the more sublime our ministry, the more exposed we are to the attacks of vainglory…”. “How many religious, for lack of humility −says St. Alphonsus− for lack of humility fell miserably over the precipice. They rose to the point of working miracles, and afterwards ambition made them fall into heresy.”

The spiritual man dominated by pride is in himself a thief, who steals not material goods but spiritual ones, because he steals the glory of God. That is why St. Francis of Assisi said: “Lord, if You grant me any good, keep it for me Yourself, lest I steal it from You.” That is why St. Paul writes to the Corinthians: by the grace of God I am what I am5; and so for him, by ourselves we cannot do a good work, nor even have a good thought.

Hence it would always be a necessity to say We are unprofitable servants, we have done what we had to do6. For everything we do is nothing if we consider that it is for a God who deserves infinite love and who has suffered so much for love of us. “Who would not laugh at the clouds -says St. Bernard- if they boasted of the rain they pour down?” Each time St. Teresa did some good, she praised God for it, saying that all that good came from Him. St. Gregory said: “he who does many works of virtue, but without humility, is like one who throws dust into the wind.”

3° To remain in distrust of oneself: If God does not help us, we cannot preserve His grace: Unless the Lord guards the city, in vain do the watchmen keep vigil7. Let us never expect the fruit of our labor except from the hands of God. Indeed, what proportion is there between our efforts and the conversion of a sinner? Good works are more God's than ours; the rest is just talk. Without Him it is not that we can do little, but that we can do nothing. If we cannot conceive a thought by ourselves, how much less can we accomplish a good work. That is why the key here and always is to place ourselves in His hands: let us recognize our uselessness in order to become useful. And when some great work is imposed on us, let us not lose confidence by looking at our incapacity, but let us trust in God, who tells us in Exodus: I will be with your mouth and with his, and I will teach you what you must do8. The humble, trusting in God, work with His divine arm, and that is why they obtain from God what they desire.

4° To accept humiliations: In order to acquire humility we need to accept the humiliations that come to us from God or from men, and to repeat with Job: I had sinned and perverted what was right, yet he has not given me what I deserved9. That is true humility: to know that we are contemptible because of our sins. And to be so in truth. There are those who consider themselves the most deserving of contempt; but when they are corrected in something, right then and there they boil over like scalded milk.

HUMILITY AND MAGNANIMITY

But, as Chesterton often points out, Christianity is the doctrine of admirable paradoxes. We say that we must humble ourselves to the point of contempt, that this is the truth of man; but precisely in that lowering of ourselves our greatness is found; that is why whoever humbles himself will be exalted. That is why St. Augustine points out that “God is on high: if you exalt yourself, He flees and hides from you; if you humble yourself, He comes down to you.” God hears only the prayers of the humble, of those who give themselves to Him with all their heart. When St. Jerome speaks of the humility of St. Paul, he says:

“He attained glory by fleeing from it; for just as the shadow follows the one who flees from it, so glory follows the one who despises it and withdraws from the one who seeks it.”

And this is so because, necessarily, when we empty ourselves of ourselves, when we scrape away from ourselves what hinders our progress —which is our very selves— it is at that moment that we are filled with God. That is why St. John of the Cross says: “When he comes to be reduced to nothing, which will be the highest humility, the spiritual union between the soul and God will be accomplished”10. And furthermore, because when we let ourselves be led by the Spirit of God, we have the power of God at our disposal. And that is why we launch ourselves into great undertakings, toward great honors, because we can do all things in Him who strengthens us11. That is why, always, as St. Teresa said, “love your littleness.” Albino Luciani wrote:

Humility goes hand in hand with magnanimity. To be good is something great and beautiful, but difficult and arduous. So that the spirit may not aspire to great things in an immoderate way, there is humility. So that

it may not lose heart before difficulties, there is magnanimity. I think of St. Paul: contempt, scourgings, pressures do not depress this magnanimous man; ecstasies, revelations, applause do not exalt this humble man. Humble when he writes I am the least of the apostles. Magnanimous when he declares I can do all things in Him who strengthens me. Humble, but for that very reason he knows how to fight when the time comes: are they Jews? so am I… are they ministers of Christ? I speak like a madman, but I am more. He places himself beneath everyone, and that is why in his duties he lets himself be bent by nothing and by no one. The waves dash the ships in which he travels against the reefs; serpents bite him; pagans, Jews, false Christians expel and persecute him; he is beaten with rods and thrown into prison; he is made to die each day; they think they have frightened him, annihilated him, and he reappears fresh and full of vigor to assure us: I am certain that neither death nor life, nor angels nor principalities, nor things present nor things to come, nor spiritual powers, nor height nor depth, nor any other creature will ever be able to separate us from the love of God, made manifest in Christ Jesus, our Lord.

Therefore let us always live in this holy spirit of humility, and we will do enormous works. God chose what is weak in the world to combat what is strong, so that no mortal might glory in the presence of God, since Christ, for the conversion of the world, did not wish to choose the powerful or the wise but humble and unlettered fishermen who, far from trusting in their own strength, placed all their trust in God. St. Gregory said: “Nothing is difficult for the humble.”

All this has a special bearing on us, religious of the Incarnate Word. We must imitate with all our strength Jesus Christ in His hidden life, in His hidden life, in His passion, in His resurrection, His humility in the Eucharist; and let us ask Mary, the most perfect model of humility, who always lived in the attitude of a poor handmaid of the Lord, Ecce Ancilla Domini, who scarcely speaks, draws attention to herself in nothing, devotes herself to the tasks proper to a woman in the poor little house of Nazareth, who appears on Calvary as Mother of the ‘Great Failure,’ and who lives obscure and unknown under the care of St. John after the Ascension of the Lord, and who works no miracle — let us ask her to teach us that science which she lived from her conception, which is that of being humble, so that we may say with Her: My soul proclaims the greatness of the Lord, for he has looked upon the lowliness of his handmaid12.

[Exordium] The whole biography of Jesus of Nazareth as man can be summed up in this formula: “He was the Messiah and He fought against the Pharisees” —or perhaps more briefly still: “He fought against the Pharisees.” That was the task Christ personally assigned Himself; that was His campaign1.

The life of Christ was not an idyll but a drama, and there is no drama without an antagonist (without a villain in the story). Christ’s antagonist, the apparent victor, was Phariseeism. Without Phariseeism the whole history of Christ would have been different; and so would that of the entire world. His Church would not have been as it is now, and the universe would have followed another destiny, wholly unimaginable for us, with Israel as the head of the people of God, and not deicide and scattered.

Without Phariseeism, Christ would not have died on the cross; but without Phariseeism fallen Humanity would not be this Humanity, nor would religion be religion. Phariseeism is the worm of religion; and after the fall of the First Man it is an unavoidable worm, for in this mortal life there is no fruit without its worm, nor any institution without its specific corruption.

1. Religious pride

Phariseeism is religious pride. The moral portrait of the Pharisees that Christ paints in today’s Gospel is tremendous! To do all things in order to be seen by men; to broaden their phylacteries and lengthen their fringes (Mt 23:3); they are greedy for places of honor, for the first seats… they are of that generation that considers itself clean, yet is not washed of its filth (Prov 30:12).

That is why Phariseeism is the subtlest and most dangerous corruption of the greatest truth: the truth that religious values come first. But the moment we claim them for ourselves, we lose them; the moment we make our own what belongs to God, it ceases to belong to anyone, if it does not become the property of the devil. The religious gesture, once one becomes self-conscious of it, turns into a grimace.

The destiny of Jesus of Nazareth was to collide with Phariseeism; and once the collision occurred, the fight to the death followed inevitably. Christ came to fight against the false shepherds, against the wolves dressed as shepherds, those in sheepskin jackets. Humanity has never witnessed a sharper, more dangerous and more tragic conflict: living religion must live within dried-up religion without drying up or ceasing to be what it is, like a surge of sap that must move through a trunk turned to bark.

This was the difficult and delicate work of Christ. The chair of Moses is still the chair of Moses. And as we heard in the Gospel: one must do what those seated in it say without doing what they do; and one must say a great many things that they keep silent about, which must be said, and which will make them leap up like vipers: “to bear witness to the truth.” That must be done; and the other must not be omitted.

2. Spiritual vice

Phariseeism is a spiritual vice, that is, a diabolical one, for the corruptions of the spirit are worse than the corruptions of the flesh. It is a compendium of all the spiritual vices—avarice, ambition, vainglory, pride, blindness, hardness of heart, cruelty—which has come to hollow out from within, diabolically, the three theological virtues, thus constituting the “sin against the Holy Spirit.” The deviations of the flesh are corruptions; but the deviations of the spirit are perversion.

And why is it a sin against the Holy Spirit? Because the Pharisee sees himself as a holy man, a just man, almost divine, and therefore he sees all his actions as holy, just, divine. And in such a heart there is no room for the Holy Spirit to enter. That is why the words of the psalm apply to them: They have closed their heart with fat; arrogance speaks through their mouth [Ps 16(17):10].

Instinctively, the Pharisee smells out and hates true religiosity. He is its contrary, and contraries know each other. He feels certain that if he does not kill it, it will kill him. And that is why the Gospel says that the Pharisees went off and plotted how they might trap Him in some word (Mt 22:15). This whole drama unfolds in silence, in darkness, by means of cover-ups and intricate schemes. And every means is good, provided it is secret: calumny, bribery, fraud, distortion, false witness, threats.

Such was the drama of Christ. Thus did the Savior die. All His meekness, all His gentleness, all His docility, His benefits, His prudence, His eloquence, His pleas, His tears, His withdrawals, His warnings, His imprecations, His prophetic threats, His artistic talent, His blood, His silent imploring as the Ecce Homo, were to shatter against the heart of the Pharisee, harder than stone.

3. Drama of Christ and His Church

It is the drama of Christ and of His Church. If, in the course of the centuries, an enormous mass of suffering and even of blood had not been offered up by other christs in resisting the Pharisee, the Church would not be standing today.

Does Phariseeism exist in the church today? Of course it does! But that is no excuse for leaving her or for not entering the bosom of the Catholic Church.

Ven. Archbishop Fulton Sheen used to say: “[Jesus] came to place a prostitute higher than a Pharisee, the repentant thief higher than the high priest, and the prodigal son higher than his model brother. To all the frauds and pretenders who would claim they could never join the Church because His Church was not holy enough, I would ask: ‘How holy does the Church have to be before you will enter her?’ If the Church were as holy as they want her to be, they would never have been let in!”2.

[Peroratio]

None of us is exempt from falling into that spiritual vice. That is why it is supremely important to follow our Lord’s warning at the end of the Gospel we heard today: The greatest among you will be your servant. Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted (Mt 23:12). Humility and the fear of God are the antidote to Phariseeism.

Of Our Lady, the humble handmaid of the Lord, we ask these gifts in this Holy Mass: great humility and fear of God for ourselves and for all those in the Church who hold the office of shepherds of souls.

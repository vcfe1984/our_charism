1 Kgs 19:16b, 19-21/Gal 5:1, 13-18/Lk 9:51-62

[Exordium]All the readings we have just heard can make us feel a little uncomfortable. All the details we read in them are profoundly countercultural, against the current.

So, for example, in the first reading we heard the call of Elisha, whom Elijah chooses as his successor. That is to say, this reading from the book of Kings has to do with following one’s vocation.

Now, when we speak of finding our own path in life, we set a very high price on ʽour own judgmentʼ about what suits us or does not suit us to do with our life –for us it is like a fundamental right–: ʽnobody is going to come and tell me what I should do with my lifeʼ, ʽI decideʼ what to do with ʽmy lifeʼ. Well, that may be our perspective, our way of thinking in today’s culture, but it was definitely not the biblical perspective.

Notice that Scripture does not suggest that Elijah and Elisha knew each other. Suddenly Elijah appears, passing by him while he is plowing, and just like that, without consulting him, without asking his permission, without any discussion, without preparations, Elijah simply throws his mantle over him, and Elisha follows him. Choosing your own path? Deciding what to do with your life? It does not seem to be the biblical way.

Let us also note the change Elisha has to make in accepting his call. Elisha was in the field plowing with twelve yoke of oxen, that is, he was a wealthy person for the time, or at least of ‘high society’; twelve yoke of oxen is a lot of animals, and besides, he had sophisticated machinery, the plow; only people of the upper class of society could have such possessions. It is as if today we were to say that Elisha was driving his Lamborghini. What does Elisha do? Immediately, he took a yoke of oxen, slaughtered them, and with the oxen’s gear he cooked their meat and gave it to the people; then he arose, followed Elijah and became his servant. This is equivalent to saying ‘he sold his whole business’, ‘he gave away all his money’, ‘he gave away his house’. Just imagine! A person who is rich, who has people working for him, who has a big house, a very sophisticated way of life, suddenly leaves it all, gives it away.

Throughout the centuries we have seen many who, like Elisha, spontaneously, following God’s initiative, left everything to follow our Lord. Let us think, for example, of St. Anthony the Abbot, who one day went ‘by chance’ into a church and heard the Gospel where Jesus says to the rich young man: go and sell all that you have, give it to the poor and follow me. And he did it! even though he was rich. He spent the rest of his life living in the simplicity and austerity of the desert. Think of St. Francis of Assisi, who was the son of a very rich merchant; he gave it all away and even gave his father the clothes he was wearing and walked naked into the forest. Let us think of St. Katharine Drexel, an American saint, the sole heiress of an immense fortune, who in order to follow God’s call left everything.

Radical self-giving

I imagine that by now you are thinking: ‘well, we have in some way, more or less, already done that’; ‘we have left the comfort of our homes, our money –little or much–, our studies…’. However, and this is the point, as religious of the Incarnate Word we are called to a radical self-giving that does not end on the day of the profession of temporary vows, nor on the day of perpetual vows, nor on the day of our priestly ordination; we must “aspire to live with all radicality the demands of the Incarnation and of the Cross”1 always, that is, “to live poverty in the maximum and absolute degree in order better and more radically to imitate the poor Christ”2. And the major seminary must be a school in that sense.

Poor in the material sense, one can say that you already are… here I would rather refer to that degree of poverty of which our proper law speaks and which invites us to conquer “total detachment, not only from material goods –the proper object of the virtue of poverty– but from everything that is not God Himself, which presupposes the perfection of charity and complete and consummate holiness”3. This is very important for our life as missionaries.

Here you do not have twelve oxen, or a Lamborghini, but you are indeed surrounded by a great many consolations. If you stop to look, you have the good fortune of being surrounded by very good priests to whom you can turn whenever you want; you have access to the sacraments daily; you are surrounded by a great many brother seminarians; none of you –not even the one who lives at the end of the last block– has to walk kilometers to go to church; and we could go on listing many very consoling details. Careful! I am not saying those things are bad, all right? On the contrary, all those things are very good, extremely good, because they are conducive to formation. But one must be attentive and not set one’s heart on those things, because one day God is going to ask you, too, for a radical change, as He did Elisha, and God is fully within His rights. It is the deal we made when we took our vows in this Institute. That is why one has to be prepared for it.

It happened, for example, to St. Junípero Serra. At 56 years of age, and with the sole experience of having been a professor of Theology, very comfortable in the seminary, he had to leave for a mission that seemed impossible in the new world. He left his comfort zone and off he went. He founded 9 of the 15 missions that today give their names to cities in California. Was it hard for him? Of course it was hard for him. He was asthmatic and had a wound in his leg that never healed, and yet he walked the whole way (38,000 km). “Always forward, never back”, he used to say. It also happened to St. José de Anchieta: having barely finished the novitiate, and while still sick with tuberculosis, he was sent to the mission in Brazil, which had just begun, with all the hardships that implied at that time. He did his seminary in the mission. His companions were ordained at 24 or 25; he at 32. But before God’s call they did not shrink back, they did not look back; they threw themselves into what God was asking of them.

That is why, notice that the Directory of Missions Ad Gentes establishes as a most important condition for the mission that capacity to deny oneself, to renounce that selfish attitude that makes us seek our own interests, our own comfort, in order to work with zeal in the place and in the office that the Institute entrusts to us for the greater glory of God. The directory says: “The missionary is asked ‘to renounce himself and everything he had up to then and to make himself all things to all’: in the poverty that leaves him free for the Gospel; in detachment from the people and goods of his own milieu, so as to become a brother to those to whom he is sent and bring them to Christ the Savior”4. Notice what it says: to renounce himself and everything he had up to then… detachment from the people and goods of his own milieu… that is part of the program of all who want to be missionaries in the Institute, and you must begin to exercise yourselves in it from now on.

Let it be well understood that the missionary, in order to truly be one, must give himself entirely, and must give himself forever. This, and nothing else, is the apostolic ideal always promoted in the Institute from its very foundation5. Nothing is given to the Lord by halves; nothing is measured out to the Lord. What we can offer Him is already so little! So, not to give oneself forever is not to give oneself entirely.

Once St. Peter Julian Eymard was giving a sermon to those who were professing for the first time, and he asks them: “For how long are you signing this contract? Out of prudence the rule orders you to sign it only for a few years, one or three. Are you going to say on that account: Fine! I give myself for this time, but afterward we shall see? Certainly not! In your hearts you must act as if you were making perpetual vows. Because if you do not want to belong to God forever, you are not worthy to be His for one year. Stay here, the saint tells them, do not take one more step, because where God is concerned one does not make trial runs6.

However, there are souls who, as soon as a difficulty arises, when they have some clash in the apostolate, when they experience some rejection –like the disciples in the Gospel when they were not received–, when they are given a correction, when they hear some criticism of the Institute, immediately call into question the following of Christ, to the point that at times they behave as enemies of the cross of Christ7, as St. Paul says; they look back, as the Gospel we heard says. Sometimes, to avoid a renunciation, so as not to deny themselves, out of fear of sacrifice, they miss out on great graces. They are afraid that by giving something they will be left with nothing. Tell me something: why cling to a match when we can have the sun?

“Cowards –says our proper law– die many times before their death”8. Nothing could be further from us. That is why we read in another of our documents: “The religious vocation is to leave everything in order to obtain everything; it is to leave the things of this world in order to cling to the All, which is God. St. Teresa said: ‘This King does not give Himself except to the one who gives himself entirely to Him’9. Far from lamenting what we leave behind, we should consider the goodness of God who wishes to give Himself to us”10.

In this sense the witness of one of the North American martyrs, Noël Chabanel, is truly exemplary; he belonged to the same group as St. Jean de Brébeuf, Isaac Jogues… who are perhaps the best known. The point is that Fr. Chabanel was a successful professor in France, but he fervently desired to be a missionary in New France (Canada) and prayed that his martyrdom would be accepted, that is, his offer to go to those lands as a missionary. The fact is that when at last he was sent and arrived at the mission, once he had contact with the Indians and had the opportunity to experience in his own flesh the difficulties of the mission, he felt like running away, like going back to the comforts and securities of France, like looking back. They say that of all the Jesuits who went to New France, he was the one for whom the mission was hardest. He found the language incomprehensible and the way of life repugnant, and naturally all this saddened him. However, and here is the heroism that prepared him for martyrdom, even though his superiors gave him permission to return to France, he made a vow to remain firm in the mission no matter what happened. Why? Because he was fully aware of the words we hear the Incarnate Word pronounce today in the Gospel: Whoever puts his hand to the plow and looks back is not fit for the Kingdom of God11.

And do you know what? God took him at his word. He was the one who stayed longest in the mission, because he was the last to be martyred. One night, as he was fleeing from some warrior Indians and trying to reach the central mission post the Jesuits had, he came upon a very deep river that could not be forded, and while he was thinking how he would manage to cross, an Indian –in fact a Christian apostate– approached him and offered to take him across in his canoe. But halfway across, the man killed Fr. Chabanel, stole what he was carrying and threw his body into the river. The man took two years to confess what he had done, saying that he had acted out of hatred for the missionaries and for the faith. Do you see? If he had gone back to the comforts and securities of France, if he had gone back to his comfort zone, he would have missed the palm of martyrdom and eternal beatitude.

That is why it is important now, while you are in formation, that you load your saddlebags full of fervor, the fervor that withstands the great waves of trial that will undoubtedly come upon the missionary; that you load your saddlebags full of “apostolic charity”12 –as our proper law asks of us– which makes the missionary forget himself in order to work “even in the most difficult places and in the most adverse conditions”13, and this not in just any way, but with great zeal for the good of souls and the expansion of the Church of Christ; and that you be filled with that courage which makes the missionary –even though he no longer has the spiritual consolations he had before “and which one day God withdraws completely”14, and stripped also of “the supports and securities regarding the state of his soul”15– nevertheless trust “with unshakable firmness that even the most adverse events, those most opposed to his natural outlook, are ordained by God for his good”16.

That genius St. Alberto Hurtado used to say: “In life there are no difficulties, there are only circumstances. God directs everything, and He directs everything well. There is nothing to do but abandon oneself, and serve at every moment as far as possible”.

Looking forward

Therefore, listen to me carefully: there have been and there always will be difficulties; criticism, threats, crosses of every kind are not lacking and will not be lacking, because God does not want them to be lacking, either here or anywhere else. However, we have to look forward; we must put out into the deep, trusting in the word of Christ: Duc in altum! Jesus Himself warns us: Whoever puts his hand to the plow and looks back is not fit for the Kingdom of God17. In the cause of the Kingdom there is no time to look back, much less to let oneself be carried away by sloth. Much is expected of us: nothing more and nothing less than the sublime task of “claiming for Jesus Christ the lordship of all that is authentically human, even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”18. We must act with generosity19, with resolve20, “working always against the temptation of delay, against the fear of sacrifice and of total self-giving, and against the temptation to take back what we have given by seeking compensations or by settling down, making a ‘nest’ in things that are not God”21. It is the example of Christ Himself in today’s Gospel, which says that Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem22. And in that “attitude one must live permanently, without diminishing or retracting, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without retreats or sluggishness”23. For “he who is not capable of setting fire to his ships when he lands is not capable of building empires”24.

[Peroration] Let us ask in this Holy Mass for the grace that from this beloved house of formation may go forth missionaries who live lordship over themselves, over men, over the world and over the devil; who enjoy the ‘freedom’ of the children of God… who are courageous, who are accustomed to discipline, who know how to give each thing its value, and in hierarchical order; who love the Institute by living its proper charism; missionaries who say, with all the radicality and commitment that it implies: Lord, I will follow you wherever you go!25.

We ask this of the Incarnate Word through the intercession of the Most Blessed Virgin Mary.

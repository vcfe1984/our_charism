“The devil you know not” Part V

Throughout these Conferences we have presented the strategies the devil uses, according to what Louis J. Cameli’s book The Devil You Know Not sets before us. Thus we have spoken of deception, division and diversion. Today we are going to present the fourth and last strategy the author points out, which is discouragement.

  • Discouragement: This is the fourth strategy the devil uses so that in the end we do not advance on the path toward the summit of the Mount (which is Christ Himself). This is the worst of all, and we will see why.

First, it must be said that the devil is not the only one responsible for or the cause of discouragement. Other people, wearing circumstances, and our own sin and limitations are of course also causes of discouragement.

The tempter can, likewise, use discouragement for his own cause, which is always to frustrate our advance on the path toward union with God. Discouragement is a potent weapon in the devil’s hands. Discouragement, if it comes to invade us and is insistently present in us, can stop us or paralyze us on our path toward God. All spiritual authors, from the Desert Fathers until now, have seen discouragement as one of the most dangerous, if not the most dangerous, of the threats in the spiritual life. Discouragement, unfortunately, puts our whole spiritual life at risk.

Besides the spiritual or explicitly religious context—from my point of view—discouragement is a pandemic in our society. Everyone seems to have been touched by discouragement in one way or another. As a phenomenon in itself, discouragement becomes evident in the lack of enthusiasm and in the weariness that marks so many souls. It also shows itself in the joyless faces of the people who get on the train with us, of the people who walk through the city, and even of those who sit in the pews of the church every Sunday.

This has nothing to do with depression. At times something that is simply discouragement is diagnosed as depression. And it seems to me that more people are affected by discouragement than by depression. The two conditions are different, although the manifestations of the one and the other are often similar. A severe, acute depression, however, is a devastating sadness that comes upon us from certain chemical imbalances or from certain very painful situations. To such a point that it seems to the person that there is no way out.

The one who is discouraged is not necessarily depressed. The discouraged person sees clearly that there is a certain potential and some possibility of a way out. And precisely because he knows what could come to be, the frustration or the impeding (the obstruction, the delay) of those possibilities causes him grief. They throw out an idea, they have some initiative, they see all the good that could be done, they entrust it to someone, and that person delays, does it badly, does not give it importance… result = he becomes discouraged. In the case of the person who suffers from depression, on the other hand, the depression itself makes the decisions for him, limiting all his movement, activity and even interpersonal relationships. Discouragement, by contrast, leads people to decide “to stop trying,” “to give up,” to surrender, to stop trying or simply to stop (not to do something). It is that tiny bit of freedom to decide whether or not to fall into discouragement that interests the devil, who knows that our path toward God has to do precisely with freedom and that, when all is said and done, all that God wants of us is our free and loving response to Him.

Truly: the devil uses discouragement to harm us and even to derail us from the narrow path St. John of the Cross speaks of. This strategy is particularly insidious because it is not so noticeable, in the sense that the devil’s hand in all this is not easily perceptible, just as it is not evident that we are drawing away from God.

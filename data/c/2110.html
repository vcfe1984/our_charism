Jn 1:1-18 – Preface of the Nativity III

What existed from the beginning, what we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, what we have contemplated and our hands have touched concerning the Word of life (…) we proclaim to you1.

My dear all, on this solemn day, on which we celebrate the birth of Christ, the Incarnate Word, we feel the truth, the power and the joy of these words of the apostle St. John.

That God of whom no image must be made, because any image would only manage to diminish Him, and even to falsify Him, this God has made Himself visible in the One who is His true image, as St. Paul says (cf. 2 Cor 4:4; Col 1:15)2.

This is the mystery of Christmas, which allows us to touch –so to speak– the humility of God. He who, as we said yesterday, has entered into human history and dwelt among us, in order to give us the capacity to become children of God, to be like Him.

Each of the mysteries of Christ is not only a historical fact but contains special graces for the nourishment of our soul. What is that particular grace? The Church herself points it out to us in the collect we have just prayed: “…Grant us to share in the divine life of him who willed to share in our humanity.”

A petition that touches us, the members of the Institute, very closely, since being configured to Him3, wanting “to be other Christs, is central to our spirituality”4.

We are asking for the grace to share in that divinity to which our humanity was united. For in becoming incarnate God took our human nature and in exchange communicated to us a participation in his divine nature. This is the admirable exchange that God wants to make with us and with each and every man.

That is why St. John of the Cross, in his Spiritual Canticle, asked the Lord: “assail my intellect with your divinity, giving it divine understanding, and my will, giving and communicating to it divine love, and my memory, with divine possession of glory”5. That is what we must strive for! That “God may bring us into his divine splendors by transformation of love”6. As He did that night with the shepherds.

The Word who was in the beginning, with God7 and through whom all things were made8 invites us to be like Him. That is, to become like children, which is another way of saying to become humble.

For this it is necessary to lay aside our false certainties, our intellectual pride, which so often prevents us from perceiving the nearness of God. We must get down, go spiritually on foot, so to speak, in order to be able to enter through the door of faith and find God, God who hides himself in the humility of a newborn child9.

That going on foot, spiritually speaking, in order to enjoy to the full the wonderful exchange that God wants to make in each one of us, requires that one make progress in the practice of humility. Because only by being humble will we be saints10, that is, only by being humble will we imitate the Incarnate Word whom we contemplate today lying in a manger, surrounded by animals, in utter poverty, rejected by so many, and who, being so weak, is the very Infinite, Immense and Eternal God. In short, because only by being little can we discover something great, and only in that way can the meaning of the Incarnation be understood11.

The humble, simple souls, little enough to see the greatness of God in the littleness of a Child, are therefore the only ones who will understand the reason for His visit. He came to this poor world to bring us a proposal; to tell us as only God could say: “You give me your humanity, and I will give you my Divinity; you give me your time, and I will give you my eternity; you give me your weary body, and I will give you my Redemption; you give me your withered heart, and I will give you Love; you give me your insignificance, and I will give you My All.”

The world, so inclined to power, never seems to learn the paradox that, just as only the very little child discovers the greatness of what surrounds him, so only humble hearts will one day realize the greatness of God. You are not unaware that the world rejects this lesson. Why? Because it confuses littleness with weakness, the littleness of a child with childishness or immaturity, and humility with an inferiority complex.

But that Child whom the angels adore12 descended to the littleness of childhood as the first step toward his eternal triumph. And we must begin our eternal mission as He willed to begin His, that is, by the way of humility, which implies descending from our ignorant pride to the level at which we stand before His divine eyes.

Jesus himself taught us: … Unless you turn and become like children you will not enter the kingdom of heaven13. To become like little children means nothing other than humility or sincerity of judgment about ourselves, an acknowledgment of the disproportion between our poor life and the eternal life before us, an acknowledgment of our weakness, our frailty, our sins, the poverty of all that we are doing now, and also of the power and the wisdom that are to be ours, provided that we are humble enough to kneel before this Child in a manger of straw and confess to Him that He is Our Lord, the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father14, our Life and Our All.

Very particularly for us, this contemplation of Jesus in his birth, as we were saying a moment ago, must move us to practice the virtues of self-emptying15, among which humility is the first, and which our Proper Law translates “into the humility of a hidden life, into the acceptance of sufferings in order to complete in one’s own flesh what is lacking in the tribulations of Christ (Col 1:24), into silent sacrifice, into abandonment to the holy will of God, into serene fidelity even in the face of the decline of one’s strength and of one’s own influence”16. Something that is very proper to contemplative religious, since it is the fruit of the life of prayer17 and of the true prayerful attitude that must permeate the whole life of the monk. That is why Fulton Sheen said that the closer we are to God, the more we become like children18, the more we become humble.

Now, this humility of children is not simply a spiritual metaphor, nor is it a pious external pose. “To attain humility we must love and desire humiliations. Without that there can be no humility,” said Blessed Joseph Allamano. These humiliations may come to us from our own deficiencies, from our defeats or even from our mistakes, if not also from the ill will of our neighbor. Without this serene, even joyful acceptance of humiliations there is no authentic humility.

But our calling is to imitate the Incarnate Word, and in order to enjoy to the full the wonderful exchange that God wants to make in each one of us, that is, to transform us into Him, it is necessary to practice humility actively and effectively.

So that, by exercising ourselves along that path, we may attain the humility of the truly spiritual, which the mystical Doctor of Fontiveros describes as follows: “not only do they consider their own things as nothing, but they are very little satisfied with themselves; they regard all others as much better, and usually have a holy envy of them, with a desire to serve God as they do; because, the more fervor they have and the more works they do and the more delight they take in them, since they walk in humility, the more they know how much God deserves and how little is all that they do for him. And so great is what they would like to do for him out of charity and love that all they do seems nothing to them; and this loving care so urges, occupies and absorbs them that they never notice whether others do or do not do; and if they do notice, it is all, as I say, believing that all the others are much better than they. Hence, holding themselves in little esteem, they also want others to hold them in little esteem and to belittle and disparage their things. And even if others want to praise and esteem them, they can in no way believe it, and it seems strange to them that such good things should be said of them.

These, with great tranquility and humility, have a great desire to be taught by anyone who can benefit them. Far from wanting to be anyone’s teachers, they are very ready to walk and set off on a path other than the one they are following, if they are ordered to, because they never think they are right in anything. They rejoice when others are praised; they only grieve that they do not serve God as those others do.

They have no desire to speak of their own things, because they hold them in so little esteem that they are ashamed to tell them even to their spiritual masters, thinking that they are not things worth talking about. They are more eager to tell their faults and sins, or to have them known, than their virtues; and so they are more inclined to deal about their souls with those who think less of their things and their spirit.

These will give the blood of their hearts to whoever serves God, and will help, as far as lies in them, so that He may be served.

In the imperfections into which they see themselves fall, they bear with themselves with humility, and with meekness of spirit and loving fear of God, hoping in Him”19.

On this beautiful Christmas morning, on which we celebrate the incalculable mystery of the humiliation of the Son of God, let us ask the Blessed Mother, who bent down to adore her Child-God, for the gift of humility, so that we may contemplate in the most tender breast of the Child Jesus the Heart of this God who “wants to be our ‘joy,’ our ‘salvation,’ our ‘hope,’ our ‘happiness,’ and our ‘only Sovereign’”20. May She teach us how to serve God, how to love God, how to pray to God.

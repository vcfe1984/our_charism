[Exordium] Our Constitutions, in number 20, say that “in the name of Christ we want to constitute a Religious Family… loving one another in such a way because we are children of the same Father.”

That is to say, Christ has called us individually, certainly, but in order to form a family, a particular family, the Religious Family of the Incarnate Word.

This is an aspect on which St. John Paul II insisted a great deal every time he spoke to religious. Thus, for example, in ’86 (perhaps you had not even been born yet) he said: “The preparation of candidates for religious life is not limited only to imparting knowledge. It is above all the delicate task of leading them to give a deep and personal response to God’s call… with a generous and sincere love for their own Religious Family”1. This is key.

1. Love for the Religious Family

What does it mean to have love for one’s own Religious Family?

Our Proper Law says it very clearly: a true religious keeps fidelity and shows a great love not only when he appreciates the gift of religious life but also when he loves and defends his own Institute, which includes in a particular way the founder and the Constitutions. That is in the Directory of Consecrated Life2.

This love for the Religious Family is key for our mission. Because it is what will make us soak up the spirit of our beloved Institute, in such a way that its interests are our interests, its projects are my projects; if the Institute is attacked, I am attacked. Do you understand?

Moreover, if there is charity among us, if there is unity, that gives our religious witness a power that nothing and no one else can give it. No matter how many popular missions we preach, no matter how pure our doctrine, no matter how much we expand into many countries of the world, if there is no charity and unity among us, our efforts are in vain because, as John Paul the Great also says, “division among brothers, among sisters, is a stumbling block for evangelization”3. Word for word.

On the other hand, if we are solidly united in love for our Religious Family, that is a constant, intelligent preaching, even without words, of the message of Christ, which is none other than a message of charity. And this love for the Religious Family –which naturally includes our brothers in this Family– is also key for the future of the Institute. It is in the seminary that one begins to live the spirit of priestly fraternity4.

For notice what our Spiritual Father says: prayer and the apostolate –it is true– help us to grow in charity, but “even more important for a deep spirit of brotherhood is ‘the unity of soul and heart,’ which is strengthened by your shared commitment to holiness, your common charism and your lifelong commitment to follow Christ by observing the Gospel and the Constitutions of your Institute”5.

All the holy founders placed great emphasis on the love that religious must profess for their Institute. I quote here two examples, just to illustrate: St. John of the Cross, for example, gave the following counsel to a religious: “By no means desire to know anything except how you will better serve God and better keep the things of your Institute”6. St. Peter Julian Eymard, for his part, calls his Congregation “mother” and tells his religious that “the first duty you have toward her is to love her with filial love”7. And he adds the following: “The rule is for you what the gospel is for the Church” –notice how important– therefore be careful, because “some come who say: If this were modified, if that were added…, it would be better if things were done this other way… It may be that such men have talent, experience and influence, but I tell you [–says the saint–] that, willingly or not, they are traitors to the first grace, to the grace of the foundation, to the ideas of the Founder, and that whoever listens to them will ruin the Institute”8.

Let us be, then, good children of our beloved “mother,” the Congregation, which is the place where God the Father has called us to consecrate our existence to the greater honor and glory of his Name.

2. The servant religious – the son religious

St. Luigi Orione, founder of the Sons of Divine Providence, insisted a great deal that his congregation live in an authentic family spirit, in which the bond of love for the Incarnate Word would be the secret of harmony and mutual collaboration. But of course, he realized that not everyone loved the congregation as they should; that is why he distinguishes “two different types of religious: there is the servant religious and the son religious”9, and in a fantastic good night talk before a profession of vows he explains them. Let us see what it is about.

The saint begins by clarifying that these are not two categories of his own religious, but that he is referring to religious in general, according to the love they have for their mother, the Congregation. And therefore what he teaches here applies to us as well.

“I have traveled a great deal,” says Don Orione, “I have visited many religious congregations; I know many religious; I know the Trappists, the black Benedictines, the white Benedictines, the Olivetans, I know the Lateran canons, the various Franciscan families, the Jesuits, the Piarists; I know the religious of this part of the world and also of the other part of the world…; the Salesians of Italy and the Salesians of America, the Philippines, the Somascans10, etc. Well, among religious there are ‘servant’ religious and ‘son’ religious: two types of religious”11.

“What is the ‘servant’ religious like? [Pay attention] He is the one who seeks his own interests in everything, who takes advantage of the Congregation to achieve his personal ends. He is the one who obeys his Congregation only with fear and out of fear. The ‘servant’ religious is the religious who serves the Congregation and works with indifference and reluctance. One could say that piety and love for his mother, the Congregation, are not in his heart. He lives in the schools, in the convents, in the abbeys, in the educational institutes, in the communities, more as a stranger than as a brother.

His soul seems closed toward his superiors; the ‘servant’ religious always has a closed heart. He is like a book open only in part, with many parts still untouched, in which the superior cannot read how and when he wishes.

The ‘servant’ religious,” Don Orione goes on to say, “as soon as the superior turns his eyes away, feels free. The first thing he does in the morning is to find out whether the superior will be at home or will go out. When the ‘servant’ religious has occasion to go out, if he is away from home, he never finds the moment to return to his religious house. He enjoys living his own life: he acts like lay people. His conversations are mainly worldly, rather than the conversations of a good religious. He is inclined to criticism; among the brothers, always criticism; for him all the brothers have a weakness; even in the best he knows how to find the weak side, and he always takes care to point it out.

In his judgments he always leans to the left and never to the right. He is always with the most lukewarm, with the professional jokers. The superior, before making even the slightest observation to him, must make the sign of the cross three times, for fear of a bad reception.

When it comes to giving him an assignment, the superior has to examine: will he accept or not? And when he is in that house to assist, to say Mass, to preach, how will he behave? Will he behave like a good religious or like a ‘servant’ religious? And above all, when it comes to working, to tiring himself out, the ‘servant’ religious does this much (he stretches out his hand as if to measure a span) and nothing more.

The ‘servant’ religious has his scheme, his confidants. Even at table one must be careful with one’s words, because the ‘servant’ religious makes public the most confidential news of his religious family.

Love for the Congregation is not in his heart. If he speaks outside his Congregation, it is a lot if he does not throw stones at it. If he knows that the Congregation has enemies outside, setbacks, he remains apathetic, he remains indifferent; on the contrary, he behaves in a way that even seems as if he were inwardly enjoying it”12. How sad, isn’t it?

Let us now see how Don Orione describes the religious who knows himself to be a ‘son’ of his religious family.

“There are also, thanks be to God, the ‘son’ religious, who feel themselves to be ‘sons’; nothing is dearer to them, after God, than their Congregation. He desires nothing other than to see it prosper, to see it spread its tents over the face of the earth for the greater glory of God.

He sees in the Congregation his mother and, after holy things, nothing is dearer than she. He prays, suffers, works, wears himself out for his Congregation. Who are religious like this? They are the ‘sons’… Whatever the office in which they are occupied, the ‘son’ religious is always content. Whether you tell him to work as a nurse, or put him in the school, or in an agricultural colony, he is always content; he is content to serve, with love, in any office of his Congregation. [Moreover,] he strives to perfect himself in the performance of his duties, and he does not squander his health with imprudence in work, but works with moderation and constancy without always thinking about his health. He takes care to nourish himself in order to live and to do good; he does not eat much, so as not to feel unwell: if he needs something, he goes with an open heart to the superior; he does not abandon himself to the sense of pessimism and melancholy that does so much harm to the Congregation…

Who are religious like these? …They are the ‘son’ religious. If at some time the superior of the ‘son’ religious says or does something that is not to his liking, thus leading him to renounce himself, well then, the ‘son’ religious blesses God in holy joy; because he knows that the ‘son’ religious must above all deny himself… and so he takes up his cross and follows Jesus to Calvary…

Unlike the ‘servant’ religious, the religious who knows himself a son of his congregation is like a book open to his superiors, in which one can read whenever one wishes.”

Then Don Orione concludes with the following exhortation: “Dear sons, the ranks of the Congregation are growing in number, and the hour has come for us to understand each other well, because I know that you are on the eve of making or renewing your vows.

If you feel yourselves to be ‘son’ religious, go forward!, but rather than be ‘servant’ religious, it is better to stop. Because Christ told us: I will no longer call you servants, but friends and sons.” And here I want to paraphrase what Don Orione says: “Let whoever is not clear about being a son [of the Incarnate Word] know that it is not Paul or Apollos whom he serves, but the Incarnate Word; he serves the Congregation, and the Congregation belongs to no one but the Incarnate Word”13.

[Peroratio] That is why tonight, with the hymn to Our Lady, while we thank her for all the Brazilian vocations she has been pleased to call to grow and be nourished in the lap of this mother, our beloved Congregation, we ask her very especially for two graces:

  1. That in all our houses, especially in the houses of formation14, a family atmosphere may always be lived, with a family spirit. So that, just as a “family is a community of persons in love, a communion in love, our houses may be communities of charity, to such a degree that it may be said that Christ himself lives in them”15.
  1. That in this beloved major seminary religious may be formed who know themselves to be ‘sons’ of our beloved mother, the Congregation. Religious who venerate her, who are available for the service of God in the Congregation, religious who are holily proud of her, who defend her, and who desire to spread to countless souls this marvelous spirit with which God has blessed us.

So be it. And may God always bless you.

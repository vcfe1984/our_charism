St. Thomas teaches that Christ in His Baptism plunges into the waters, burying the old man in them (for He had the flesh of sin), and purifying the waters so that they might have the power to sanctify. That is why we, in our Holy Baptism, die with Christ to the old man and rise with Him to the new life of grace. The letter to the Romans says: we were, through baptism, buried with Christ into death, so that as Christ was raised from the dead … so we too might walk in newness of life.

That is, through Baptism we Christians are configured to Christ; we receive from Him the enormous grace of sharing in the divine life and of being able to be called, properly speaking, Children of God. That is why the Baptism of Christ is celebrated with such solemnity in the Christian liturgy; St. Thomas himself says that on the day of the baptism the fact that the heavens were opened allegorically represents the fact that on that day too the gates of heaven were opened for all men. That is why, through baptism, we are Sons in the Son and temples of the Holy Spirit.

Now, by the grace of God, we too are consecrated, and if in baptism we already gave ourselves to God by the renunciation of Satan and the profession of the faith of the Church—that is, we belong to God, we are God’s, and in some way we are God, insofar as we have the divine life and are one spirit with Him—in religious consecration we do something similar, insofar as in it we wish to give ourselves totally to the things of God; but there is something more in the self-giving we make when we take our vows.

Relationship of baptism with religious life

Baptism has an intimate relationship with religious life, for strictly speaking religious life is nothing other than an attempt to achieve a perfect development of Holy Baptism. It is to carry death to the world and life in God to their ultimate consequences. This is what the Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches: The religious state thus appears as one of the ways of living a more intimate consecration “which is rooted in baptism and is totally dedicated to God. In consecrated life, the faithful, under the movement of the Holy Spirit, propose to follow Christ more closely, to give themselves to God loved above all things, and, pursuing the perfection of charity in the service of the Kingdom, to signify and announce in the Church the glory of the world to come.”. The Second Vatican Council already affirmed this in Perfectae Caritatis: “Let the members of any institute remember above all that, by the profession of the evangelical counsels, they responded to a divine call, so that, not only dead to sin but also renouncing the world, they might live for God alone. They have indeed handed over their whole life to the service of God, which undoubtedly constitutes a special consecration, which is intimately rooted in the consecration of baptism and expresses it more fully.”

Hence this continual union, to such a point that the Christian tradition is in perfect agreement that religious profession constitutes, as it were, a second baptism. The number of Holy Fathers—both Eastern and Western—and of theologians of all schools who use the formula secundum baptismus is overwhelming; nor is it merely as a simple renewal of the promises of baptism, nor even because of its sanctifying effect: what is proper and characteristic of religious profession as a second baptism consists in this, that through it the demands of baptism are carried to their utmost fullness and perfection. Those same baptismal demands—death to the world and life for God—are ratified and affirmed so fully and so consummately in religious profession that this ratification not only produces in the soul of the one who makes it the same effects as baptism—remission of the guilt and of the punishment due for previous sins—but also constitutes him in a state of perfection, something that the simple reception of the first baptism did not produce.

A second baptism—says a theologian (S. Aguilar C.M.F.)—insofar as in profession the sacrament of baptism is administered and received in the whole integrity of its baptismal being; this is a

grace granted by the Church and voluntarily accepted by the Christian as a means of entering entirely into the kingdom of God without voluntary attachments to this world that would diminish the perfection of his new life as a child of God. Religious life fulfills these demands, that is, death to the world and life for God, and this because, as the Church teaches:

  1. Religious profession, as a state of perfection, includes the voluntary commitment, under vow, to live the evangelical counsels with the aim of truly living death to the world and giving oneself totally to the things of God; thus the Council says: Therefore religious, faithful to their profession, leaving all things for Christ, must follow Him as the one thing necessary, listening to His words and devoting themselves with solicitude to the interests of Christ.
  1. Religious profession is a second baptism because it can have the same effect as baptism of renewal to a new life of perfection, precisely because the will of the one professing constitutes an act of perfect charity which produces—here ex opere operantis (that is, not as in baptism, whose effects are produced ex opere operato, by the very fact, whether Peter baptizes or Judas baptizes; but ex opere operantis means that the effect is produced according to the efforts put forth by the one making the profession)—produces the same effect as baptism: total remission of the guilt and of the punishment of all past sins. Hence St. Thomas teaches: If a man can make satisfaction for his sins by almsgiving, according to that word of Sacred Scripture, redeem your sins with alms , how much more does he make satisfaction for all his sins who gives himself totally to divine things by entering religious life, which exceeds every kind of satisfaction, ... just as the holocaust exceeds the sacrifice, as St. Gregory says. Thus is understood what the fathers say, that those who enter religion obtain the same grace as the baptized. Thus a religious—however great a sinner he may have been—can become, through profession and vows, holy and innocent before God.

Consecration to Our Lady

This intimate relationship and this splendid communication found between the effects of baptism and those of religious profession is also found in something very much our own and very dear to us, which is our fourth vow of consecration to Our Lady. This consecration is intimately united to Holy Baptism. St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort teaches this in the Treatise on True Devotion, saying that it is precisely for this that we make the present devotion, for through it we renounce the devil, the world, sin and ourselves, and we consecrate ourselves totally to Jesus Christ; it is simply that we add to baptism not only that we do it voluntarily, offering even the value of our good actions, but also that we

expressly consecrate ourselves to our Lord through the hands of Mary.

To say that we want to be one with Jesus Christ, to say that we want to live to their ultimate consequences the promises of our baptism in order to belong more fully to Jesus—in short, to say that we wish to do this with total perfection—means to say that we want to be faithful to God in religious life, because in it the ends of baptism are fulfilled in an admirable way,

by which we are united to God and sanctify our souls through the renunciation of sin and participation in a new life.

Today, as we celebrate the baptism of the Lord, all of us must recall with joy the day of our baptism and that of our perpetual vows, and in the case of those of you who have not yet made them, look with hope to that day when you will give yourselves totally to God, so as to obtain in fullness the effects of holy baptism.

That is why the apostle St. Andrew, when the executioner wanted to make him deny Jesus Christ, answered him: Oh, if you knew the mystery of the cross! If you knew the love that Jesus Christ showed you by dying on the cross to save you, you would not weary yourself trying to persuade me to deny Him, but you yourself would abandon all you have in order to consecrate yourself entirely to the love of Jesus Christ.

How much they will console us at the hour of death if during life we meditate on them assiduously with love! The Kempis said: “Nothing is so profitable as to meditate on the Passion of the Lord.” When we see the crucifix and the bleeding face of Jesus, may we know how to say: “Lord, how much You have loved me, and how small is the poor love I return to You.”

The Incarnation was the greatest work of God’s love; let us ask today for the grace to return to God, day by day, something of the love God had and has for us. May we know how to sing the love of God, as St. Augustine said, with our love. Since God has given us the grace to suffer for Christ. Or even more, as St. Francis of Assisi asked: “may I die for love of Your love, since for love of my love, You deigned to die.”

Introduction

1. “The risen Christ, as the four evangelists indicate, gives us the mandate of the mission: As the Father has sent me, so I send you (Jn 20:21)…In order not to shy away from the missionary adventure, and to move many others to it, one must have something of the poet, since peoples have never been moved by anyone but poets, and what greater poet than Jesus Christ!, and what greater poetry than His crying out to us: Put out into the deep!Duc in altum! (Lk 5:4)…It is an invitation to accomplish great works, extraordinary undertakings in which there is much of adventure, of vertigo, of danger, where the waves toss the boat, salt water sprays the face, the prow cuts its way for the first time, where there are no tracks and the only points of reference are the stars, where the keel is shaken by crosscurrents, the unfurled sails receive the fury of the wind, the masts creak… and the soul trembles… Into the deep!, far from the shore and from the firm ground of merely human thoughts, calculating and cold… where the water seethes, the heart beats fast, where the soul knows heavenly inebriations and fascinating joys. To put out into the deep is to take seriously, thoroughly, the demands of the Gospel: go, sell all that you have… (Mt 19:21); it is the yearning of our restless heart, which longs to possess the Infinite; it is the impetus of the Saints and of the Martyrs, who gave everything for God. It is what is proper to fishermen: humble, hardworking men, who do not fear dangers, watchful, patient in the long night watches, persevering in going out to sea again and again, prudent in drawing in the fish, weathered by salt and sun. It is to be ready to die, like the grain of wheat, in order to see Christ in all things. For this reason, we make our own the ardent words of St. Luigi Orione: ‘Whoever does not want to be an apostle, let him leave the Congregation: today, whoever is not an apostle of Jesus Christ and of the Church is an apostate’”1.

1.

Nature, origin and end
of the mission ad gentes

2. The mission ad gentes is a work specifically consecrated to the evangelization of peoples who do not yet.. know God or Jesus Christ. The mission of the CatholicChurch is to reveal Jesus Christ and His Gospel to those who do not know them: this is the fundamental program of the Church which, since Pentecost, she has taken up as received from her Founder.

3. “For the Church, evangelizing means bringing the Good News into all the strata of humanity and, through its influence, transforming humanity from within and making it new”2.Indeed, the Lord Jesus sent His disciples to proclaim the Gospel to the whole world and to baptize all nations3.

4. “The universal mission of the Church is born from the command of Jesus Christ and is fulfilled in the course of the centuries in the proclamation of the mystery of God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and of the mystery of the Incarnation of the Son, as the saving event for all humanity”4.

5. The whole New Testament, and in a special way the Acts of the Apostles, bears witness to the privileged and, in a certain sense, exemplary moment of this missionary effort, which would later be carried out throughout the whole history of the Church, precisely in obedience to the mandate of her Founder.

a) Mission of the Son, mission of the Church

6. The missionary mandate of the Lord has its ultimate source in the eternal love of the Most Holy Trinity: “The pilgrim Church is missionary by her very nature, since it is from the mission of the Son and the mission of the Holy Spirit that she draws her origin, in accordance with the plan of God the Father”5.

7. We confess that “the Church is the depositary of the Good News to be proclaimed. The promises of the New Covenant in Christ, the teaching of the Lord and of the Apostles, the Word of life, the sources of grace and of God's loving kindness, the path of salvation—all these things have been entrusted to her. It is the content of the Gospel, and therefore of evangelization, that she preserves as a precious living heritage, not in order to keep it hidden, but in order to communicate it”6.

8. “There is thus a profound link between Christ, the Church and evangelization. During the period of the Church that we are living in, it is she who has the task of evangelizing. This task is not accomplished without her, and still less against her”7.One cannot claim “to listen to Christ but not to the Church, to be in Christ but outside the Church. The absurdity of this dichotomy is clearly evident in these words of the Gospel: whoever rejects you rejects me (Lk 10:16).How could one love Christ without loving the Church, given that the most beautiful testimony given in favor of Christ is that of St. Paul: He loved the Church and gave himself up for her(Eph 5:25)”8.

b) Ultimate end of the mission

9. “The ultimate purpose of missionis to enable people to share in the communion which exists between the Father and the Son: the disciples are to live in unity with one another, remaining in the Father and the Son, so that the world may know and believe”9.

10. The Church, mindful of her duty to preach salvation to all, knowing that the Gospel message is not reserved to a small group of initiates, privileged or chosen, but is destined for all, makes her own the anguish of Christ before the wandering and abandoned multitudes, like sheep without a shepherd, and often repeats His words: I have compassion on the crowd (Mt 9:36).

c) The motive of the mission

11. “From God's love for all men, the Church in every age has drawn both the obligation and the vigor of her missionary dynamism”10.

12. We do not insist on the missions on our own account:

– It is the Holy Spirit who urges us to proclaim the great works of God: Preaching the Gospel is no reason for glory for me; rather, it is a duty incumbent upon me: and woe to me if I do not preach the Gospel! (1 Cor 9:16).

– It is the Church who, through the Second Vatican Council, wished to renew her life and activity according to the needs of the contemporary world, and has underlined her “missionary character,” basing it dynamically on the very mission of the Trinity. The missionary impulse therefore belongs to the intimate nature of the Christian life, and also inspires ecumenism: That all may be one… so that the world may believe that You have sent me (Jn 17:21).

– It is the life that we carry within us and which desires to communicate itself: indeed, in the history of the Church, missionary drive has always been a sign of vitality, just as its lessening is a sign of a crisis of faith.

13. It is always true that mission renews the Church, strengthens faith and Christian identity, and gives fresh enthusiasm and new incentive. Faith is strengthened when it is given to others!

14. We are urged on by the need and the religious and moral destitution of so many people. Truly, the missionof Christ the Redeemer, entrusted to the Church, is still very far from completion. At the beginning of the third millennium after His Coming, an overall view of the human race shows that this missionis still only beginning and that we must commit ourselves wholeheartedly to its service11.

15. “The urgency of missionary evangelization lies in the fact that it is the primary service which the Church can render to every individual and to all humanity in the modern world, a world which is experiencing great achievements but seems to have lost its sense of ultimate realities and of existence itself”12.

16. “The number of those who do not know Christ and do not belong to the Church is constantly on the increase; indeed, since the end of the Council it has almost doubled. When we consider this immense portion of humanity which is loved by the Father and for whom He sent His Son, the urgency of the mission is obvious”13.

17. “On the other hand, our own times offer the Church new opportunities in this field: we have witnessed the collapse of oppressive ideologies and political systems; the opening of frontiers and the formation of a more united world due to an increase in communications; the affirmation among peoples of the Gospel values which Jesus made incarnate in His own life (peace, justice, brotherhood, concern for the needy); and a kind of soulless economic and technical development which, nevertheless, stimulates the search for the truth about God, about man and about the meaning of life”14.

d) Unicity and salvific universality
of Jesus Christ and of the Church

18. The Church is faithful, and always will be, to the mandate of the Lord: Go, therefore, and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And know that I am with you always, until the end of the world (Mt 28:19-20).

19. This fidelity to the mandate of Jesus Christ implies, as its foundation, fidelity to the truth about Jesus Christ. “The Church's universal mission is born from faith in Jesus Christ”15. If this faith in Jesus Christ is falsified, the mission will be adulterated and will no longer be “the mission of the Church.” On the contrary, right faith in the Incarnate Word is the milestone upon which the whole dynamism of the mission ad gentes rests.

20. For this reason, at the beginning of the third millennium, the Church wished to reaffirm the truth about Jesus Christ, setting it within the context of the mission ad gentes. She did this in the Declaration Dominus Iesus, which“must be for every Catholic and for each of the members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word like the magna carta of the witness to the Word made flesh, in the century and millennium now beginning,” according to the wish of our Founder16. This Declaration clearly denounces certain errors concerning the mystery of Christ which consequently harm the missionary proclamation:

21. “The Church's constant missionary proclamation is endangered today by relativistic theories which seek to justify religious pluralism, not only de factobut also de iure (or in principle). As a consequence, it is held that certain truths have been superseded; for example, the definitive and complete character of the revelation of Jesus Christ,the nature of Christian faith as compared with belief in other religions,the inspired nature of the books of Sacred Scripture,the personal unity between the Eternal Word and Jesus of Nazareth,the unity of the economy of the Incarnate Word and the Holy Spirit,the unicity and salvific universality of the mystery of Jesus Christ,the universal salvific mediation of the Church,the inseparability—while recognizing the distinction—of the Kingdom of God, the Kingdom of Christ, and the Church,and the subsistence of the one Church of Christ in the Catholic Church”17.

22. In the face of all the errors of relativistic theories, which endanger the content of the mission ad gentes, we wish to profess clearly the perennial doctrine of the Church:

23. “Concerning the unique and universal salvific mediation of Jesus Christ.

– It must be firmly believed that Jesus Christ, Son of God made man, crucified and risen, is the one and universal Mediator of the salvation of humanity.

– It must also be firmly believed that Jesus of Nazareth, son of Mary and the only Savior of the world, is the Son and Word of the Father. For the unity of the plan of salvation centered on Jesus Christ, it must further be held that the salvific action of the Word is accomplished in and through Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Son of the Father, as mediator of salvation for all humanity. Therefore, it is contrary to the Catholic faith not only to assert a separation between the Word and Jesus, or between the salvific action of the Word and that of Jesus, but also to maintain the thesis of a salvific action of the Word as such in His divinity, independently of the humanity of the Incarnate Word.

24. Concerning the unicity and fullness of revelation in Christ.

-It must be firmly believed that Jesus Christ is the mediator, fulfillment and fullness of revelation. Therefore, it is contrary to the faith of the Church to hold that the revelation of or in Jesus Christ is limited, incomplete and imperfect. Although the full knowledge of divine revelation will be had only on the day of the Lord's glorious coming, the historical revelation of Jesus Christ already offers everything necessary for man's salvation, and has no need of being completed by other religions.

– It is in keeping with Catholic doctrine to affirm that the seeds of truth and goodness that exist in other religions are a certain participation in the truths contained in the revelation of or in Jesus Christ. On the contrary, it is an erroneous opinion to consider that those elements of truth and goodness, or some of them, do not derive, ultimately, from the source-mediation of Jesus Christ.

25. Concerning the universal salvific action of the Holy Spirit.

–The faith of the Church teaches that the Holy Spirit, at work after the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, is always the Spirit of Christ sent by the Father, who acts in a salvific way in Christians as well as in non-Christians. Therefore, it is contrary to the Catholic faith to consider that the salvific action of the Holy Spirit could extend beyond the one universal salvific economy of the Incarnate Word.

26. Concerning the orientation of all men to the Church.

– It must be firmly believed that the Church is the sign and instrument of salvation for all men. It is contrary to the faith of the Church to consider the different religions of the world as ways complementary to the Church with regard to salvation.

– According to the doctrine of the Church, the followers of other religions too are oriented to the Church and are all called to become part of her.

27. Concerning the value and the salvific function of religious traditions.

– According to Catholic doctrine, it must be held that everything that the Spirit works in men and in the history of peoples, as well as in cultures and religions, serves as a preparation for the Gospel (cf. Lumen Gentium,16). Therefore, it is legitimate to maintain that the Holy Spirit accomplishes salvation in non-Christians also, through those elements of truth and goodness present in the various religions; but it has no foundation in Catholic theology to consider these religions, as such, as ways of salvation, all the more so because there are in them gaps, insufficiencies and errors regarding the fundamental truths about God, man and the world.

– Furthermore, the fact that the elements of truth and goodness present in the various religions can prepare peoples and cultures to receive the salvific event of Jesus Christ does not mean that their sacred texts can be considered complementary to the Old Testament, which is the immediate preparation for the event of Christ itself”18.

e) The mission ad gentes and the Kingdom of God

28. The mission ad gentes is directed toward establishing throughout the whole world the Kingdom of God, already prepared by the Old Covenant and brought about by Christ and in Christ. In recent years there has been no lack of certain conceptions of the Kingdom of God which do not correspond to the teaching of the Church and which weaken her missionary spirit. It is fitting here to recall some essential elements of that teaching.

29. It is Jesus who progressively reveals the characteristics and demands of the Kingdom through His words, His deeds and His person.

30. The Kingdom is destined for all men, since all are called to be its members. To emphasize this aspect, Jesus drew near above all to those who were on the margins of society, giving them His preference, when He proclaimed the “Good News.” The liberation and salvation brought by the Kingdom of God reach the human person in both his physical and spiritual dimensions. Two gestures characterize the mission of Jesus: healing and forgiving.

31. But the Kingdom also aims at transforming human relationships, and it grows gradually as people slowly learn to love, forgive and serve one another. In fact, Jesus refers to the whole law, summing it up in the commandment of love.

32. The Kingdom is the concern of everyone: individuals, society and the world as a whole. Working for the Kingdom means acknowledging and promoting the divine dynamism which is present in human history and transforms it. Building the Kingdom means working for liberation from evil in all its forms. In a word, the Kingdom of God is the manifestation and the realization of His plan of salvation in all its fullness.

33. In certain theological circles there have arisen conceptions of salvation and mission that we may call “anthropocentric,” in the reductive sense of the term, since they are centered on man's earthly needs. However, such conceptions are reduced to the confines of a kingdom of man, cut off from its authentic and profound dimensions, and easily translate into ideologies aimed at a merely earthly progress. The Kingdom of God, by contrast, is not of this world, it is not from here19.

34. On the other hand, there are certain conceptions which deliberately place the emphasis on the Kingdom and present themselves as “Kingdom-centered.” They describe the task of the Church as if she should proceed in only two directions: on the one hand, promoting the so-called “values of the Kingdom,” such as peace, justice, freedom and brotherhood; on the other, fostering dialogue between peoples, cultures and religions, so that, through mutual enrichment, they might help the world to be renewed and to journey ever closer to the Kingdom.

35. Together with some positive aspects, these conceptions often reveal negative aspects as well, as happens when they say nothing about the mystery of the Redemption. Moreover, the Kingdom, as they understand it, frequently ends up marginalizing or undervaluing the Church.

36. Now this is not the Kingdom of God as we know it from revelation, which cannot be separated either from Christ or from the Church. Christ not only proclaimed the Kingdom, but in Him the Kingdom itself became present and was fulfilled. The Kingdom of God is not a concept, a doctrine or a program subject to free interpretation, but it is before all else a person with the face and name of Jesus of Nazareth, the image of the invisible God.

37. Likewise, the Kingdom cannot be detached from the Church. Certainly, the Church is not an end unto herself, since she is ordered toward the Kingdom of God, of which she is the seed, sign and instrument. The Church is effectively and concretely at the service of the Kingdom. This is seen, first of all, in her preaching, which is a call to conversion; this is the first and fundamental way of serving the coming of the Kingdom in individuals and in human society. The Church, then, serves the Kingdom by establishing communities and founding new particular Churches, administering to them the sacraments of salvation and guiding them to mature faith and charity. The Church, moreover, serves the Kingdom by spreading throughout the world the Gospel values which are an expression of the Kingdom and which help people to accept God's plan.

f) End of the missions and their reason for being

38. Needless to say, many objections will be raised against missionary work. In fact, this has happened. While people often speak of a springtime of the Church or of Christianity, one cannot, however, conceal a certain negative tendency in many Catholics according to which the specific mission ad gentes would be fading and disappearing—a way of thinking which is certainly not in harmony with the directives of the Council and of the subsequent Magisterium. Internal and external difficulties have weakened the Church's missionary thrust toward non-Christians, a fact which must concern all who believe in Christ.

39. Today some once again call into question the necessity of the mission ad gentes and ask: Is missionary work among non-Christians still valid? Has it not perhaps been replaced by interreligious dialogue? Is not human development an adequate goal? Does not respect for conscience and for freedom exclude every proposal of conversion? Is it not possible to attain salvation in any religion? Why, then, mission?

40. Today we must answer with St. Peter: there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved (Acts4:10.12). This statement, addressed to the Sanhedrin, has a universal value, since for all, Jews and Gentiles alike, salvation can only come from Jesus Christ.

41. It was necessary to confess one God and one Lord in contrast to the multitude of “gods” and “lords” that the people admitted. Thus St. Paul reacts against the polytheism of the religious milieu of his time and highlights the characteristic feature of the Christian faith: faith in one God and in one Lord, sent by God.

42. “Christ is the one mediator between God and men[…who] cannot enter into communion with God except through Christ, under the action of the Spirit. Christ's one, universal mediation, far from being an obstacle on the journey toward God, is the way established by God Himself, a fact of which Christ is fully aware. Although participated forms of mediation of different kinds and degrees are not excluded, they acquire meaning and value only from Christ's own mediation, and they cannot be understood as parallel or complementary to His”20.

43. Some maintain that mission is not valid because there is no right to impose one's own way of thinking, thereby annulling freedom. We answer that, instead of taking away freedom, it strengthens it, for it makes man more responsible for the dignity to which he is called. “God offers man a new life: Can Christ and everything He has brought into the history of man be rejected? Of course it is possible. Man is free. Man can say no to God. Man can say no to Christ. But the fundamental question remains. Is it legitimate to do so? On what grounds would it be legitimate?”21.

44. Moreover, and in relation to freedom of conscience, the Second Vatican Council says: “The human person has a right to religious freedom… all men are to be immune from coercion on the part of individuals or of social groups and of any human power, in such wise that in matters religious no one is to be forced to act in a manner contrary to his own beliefs, nor is anyone to be restrained from acting in accordance with them, whether privately or publicly, whether alone or in association with others, within due limits”22.

45. The proclamation of and witness to Christ, when carried out in a way that respects consciences, do not violate freedom. Faith demands a free adherence on the part of man, but it must be proposed, because “the multitudes have the right to know the riches of the mystery of Christ—riches in which we believe that the whole of humanity can find, in unsuspected fullness, everything that it is gropingly searching for concerning God, man and his destiny, life and death, and truth… This is why the Church keeps her missionary spirit alive, and even wishes to intensify it in the moment of history in which we are living. The Church feels responsible before all peoples”23.

46. Some ask: Is this not imposing a Church, thus limiting the possibilities of salvation?

47. The Council has amply asserted the role of the Church in the salvation of humanity. While acknowledging that God loves all people and grants them the possibility of being saved24,the Church professes that God has established Christ as the one mediator and that she herself has been established as the universal Sacrament of salvation.

48. It is necessary, then, to keep these two truths together, namely, the real possibility of salvation in Christ for all mankind and the necessity of the Church for this same salvation.

49. “Since salvation is offered to all, it must be made concretely available to all. But it is clear that today, as in the past, many people do not have an opportunity to come to know or accept the Gospel revelation or to enter the Church. They live in social and cultural conditions which do not permit this, and frequently they have been brought up in other religious traditions. For such people salvation in Christ is accessible by virtue of a grace which, while having a mysterious relationship to the Church, does not make them formally part of the Church but enlightens them in a way which is accommodated to their spiritual and material situation”25.

50. Finally,“to the question, Why mission? we reply with the Church's faith and hope: opening ourselves to the love of God is true liberation. In Him, and only in Him, are we set free from all alienation and doubt, from slavery to the power of sin and death. Christ is truly our peace (Eph 2:14), and the love of Christ urges us on (2 Cor 5:14), giving meaning and joy to our life. Mission is an issue of faith, an accurate indicator of our faith in Christ and His love for us”26.

51. “The present-day temptation is to reduce Christianity to a merely human wisdom, almost a science of good living. In a heavily secularized world, there has been a ‘gradual secularization of salvation,’ so that people certainly strive for the good of man, but of man truncated, reduced to his merely horizontal dimension. We, however, know that Jesus came to bring integral salvation, one which embraces the whole person and all mankind, opening them to the wondrous horizons of divine filiation”27.

g) Specific end of the missions ad gentes

52. The mission which the Church received through the Apostles is a universal mission that knows no boundaries and that concerns salvation in all its aspects, in keeping with the fullness of life which Christ came to bring.

53. This mission is one, since it has the same origin and purpose; but within the Church there are different tasks and activities. First of all, there is the missionary activity which we call mission ad gentes; it is a primary activity of the Church, essential and never completed. Indeed, the Church “cannot withdraw from her permanent mission of bringing the Gospel to the multitudes—the millions and millions of men and women—who as yet do not know Christ the Redeemer of humanity”28.

54. To say that the whole Church is missionary does not preclude the existence of a specific mission ad gentes, just as saying that all Catholics must be missionaries does not exclude the existence of “missionaries ad gentes for life” by a specific vocation.

55. In this way three situations can be distinguished within the Church:

– First, that toward which the Church's missionary activity is directed: peoples, groups, and socio-cultural contexts in which Christ and his Gospel are not known, or which lack Christian communities sufficiently mature to be able to incarnate the faith in their own environment and proclaim it to other groups. This is properly the mission ad gentes.

56. – There are also Christian communities with adequate and solid ecclesial structures; they are fervent in their faith and in Christian living; they bear witness to the Gospel in their surroundings and feel a commitment to the universal mission. In them the Church carries out her pastoral activity or care.

57. – Finally, there is an intermediate situation, particularly in countries with ancient Christian roots, and occasionally in the younger Churches as well, where entire groups of the baptized have lost a living sense of the faith, or even no longer consider themselves members of the Church, and live a life far removed from Christ and his Gospel. In this case what is needed is a “new evangelization” or a “re-evangelization.”

58. The specific missionary activity, or mission ad gentes, is addressed to “peoples or groups who do not yet believe in Christ,” “who are far from Christ,” those who “have not yet received the Gospel message for special reasons or who have rejected it until today”29, and whose culture has not yet been influenced by the Gospel. This activity is distinguished from the other activities of the Church because it is directed to non-Christian groups and settings, owing to the absence or insufficiency of the proclamation of the Gospel and of the Church's presence. It is therefore characterized as the task of proclaiming Christ and his Gospel, of building up the local Church, and of promoting the values of the Kingdom.

59. Without the mission ad gentes, the Church's very missionary dimension would be deprived of its fundamental meaning and of its exemplary realization.

2.

The Holy Spirit,
Soul of the mission

a) The Holy Spirit,
principal agent of the mission

60. The Holy Spirit is indeed the principal agent of the whole of the Church's mission; his work shines forth eminently in the mission ad gentes, as can be seen in the early Church in the conversion of Cornelius30, in the decisions made about emerging problems31, and in the choice of regions and peoples to be evangelized32. The Spirit works through the Apostles, but at the same time he is also at work in those who hear them: “through his action the Good News takes shape in human minds and hearts and extends through history. In all this it is the Holy Spirit who gives life”33.

61. The coming of the Holy Spirit transforms the disciples in a wonderful way, so that neither fear, nor weakness, nor material frontiers will be able to hinder the action of the Holy Spirit. From now on they will be fearless envoys and witnesses of the risen Christ.

b) Sent forth in the Spirit

62. As the Father has sent Me, so I send you (Jn 20:21). The moment had come when the mission of Christ and of the Holy Spirit would be carried out in the Church, the Body and Temple of the Holy Spirit. And we Christians, members of the Church which Christ founded, are associated with this joint mission, in communion with the Father in the Holy Spirit.

63. In them are the presence and power of the Spirit, and the assistance of Jesus: They went forth and preached everywhere, while the Lord worked with them (Mk 16:20). The coming of the Holy Spirit makes them witnesses or prophets34. The Spirit gives them the ability to bear witness to Jesus with all boldness (Acts 28:31). Paul and Barnabas feel themselves impelled by the Spirit toward the Gentiles35. At the first Council, which brings together in Jerusalem members of various Churches around the Apostles, a decision is made which is acknowledged as coming from the Spirit: it is the Spirit who urges them to go ever further, not only in a geographical sense but also beyond ethnic and religious barriers, for a truly universal mission. Through him, the group of the Twelve, as a single body guided by Peter, proclaims the Good News. This community perceives that the Holy Spirit has chosen Paul and Barnabas to be sent forth36. A reading of the Acts helps us to understand that, at the beginning of the Church, the mission ad gentes already had missionaries “for life, dedicated to it by a special vocation”37.

c) The Holy Spirit, guide of the mission

64. “The Spirit manifests himself in a special way in the Church and in her members. Nevertheless, his presence and activity are universal, limited neither by space nor time. The Second Vatican Council recalls the activity of the Spirit in the heart of every person, through the ‘seeds of the Word,’ even in religious initiatives and in the efforts of human activity aimed at attaining truth, goodness and God himself”38.

65. The Spirit's presence and activity affect not only individuals but also society and history, peoples, cultures and religions. Indeed, the Spirit is at the origin of the noble ideals and undertakings which benefit humanity on its journey; “with marvelous foresight he directs the course of the ages and renews the face of the earth”39. It is also the Spirit who sows “the seeds of the Word” present in various customs and cultures, preparing them for full maturity in Christ40.

66. It is the Holy Spirit who guides the Church, who leads her along the paths of mission, who makes the whole Church missionary, who is present and active in every time and place.

3.

Those to whom Evangelization is addressed

67. The last words of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark confer on the evangelization which the Lord entrusts to the Apostles a universality without boundaries: Go into all the world and preach the Gospel to every creature (Mk 16:15). Evangelization, therefore, is destined universally for all humanity.

a) Spheres of the mission ad gentes

68. By virtue of Christ's universal mandate, the mission ad gentes knows no boundaries. Nevertheless, various spheres in which it is carried out can be outlined, so that one may have a real view of the situation.

Territorial limits

69. Missionary activity has normally been defined in terms of specific territories, especially in Asia; but in Africa, Latin America and Oceania, too, there are vast areas still to be evangelized.

70. These are regions entrusted to the special regime of the mission ad gentes, as well as unevangelized groups and areas. It is particularly toward the Asian continent that the mission ad gentes ought to be chiefly directed in our time.

New social worlds and phenomena

71. “The rapid and profound transformations which characterize today's world, especially in the southern hemisphere, are having a powerful effect on the overall missionary picture. […] Today the image of the mission ad gentes is perhaps changing: privileged places ought to be the large cities, where new customs and styles of living arise, together with new forms of culture, which then influence the wider population”41.

72. Moreover, it is true that individuals or small groups cannot be evangelized if we neglect, so to speak, the centers where a new humanity is emerging with new models of development. The future of the younger nations is being shaped in the cities.

73. In speaking of the future, we cannot forget the young, who in many countries already make up more than half of the population.

74. Among the great changes taking place in the contemporary world, migration has produced a new phenomenon: non-Christians are becoming very numerous in traditionally Christian countries. Among the migrants, refugees occupy a prominent place and deserve the greatest attention.

75. Pointing out the new particular features which the mission ad gentes involves in our times, Pope Benedict XVI declares: “From its origins, the Christian people clearly perceived the importance of communicating, through unceasing missionary activity, the riches of this love [of God the Father] to all who did not yet know Christ. Even more, in recent years the need has been felt to reaffirm this commitment because—as my beloved predecessor John Paul II observed—in the modern age the missio ad gentes at times seems to be passing through a slower phase owing to the difficulties of humanity's new anthropological, cultural, social and religious framework. Today the Church is called to face new challenges and is ready to dialogue with different cultures and religions, seeking to build with every person of good will the peaceful coexistence of peoples. Thus the field of the missio ad gentes has expanded considerably and can no longer be defined solely on the basis of geographical or juridical considerations; indeed, the true recipients of the missionary activity of the People of God are not only non-Christian peoples and distant lands, but also socio-cultural spheres and, above all, hearts”42.

Cultural sectors or modern areopagi

76. The Areopagus to which St. Paul went to preach represented at that time the center of the culture of the learned people of Athens, and today it can be taken as a symbol of the new settings in which the Gospel must be proclaimed.

77. The first Areopagus of modern times is the world of communications, which is unifying humanity and turning it into what is known as a “global village.” The means of social communication have become so important that for many they are the chief means of information and education, of guidance and inspiration in their behavior as individuals, families and within society at large.

78. There are many other areopagi in the modern world toward which the Church's missionary activity ought to be directed. For example, commitment to peace, development and the liberation of peoples; the rights of individuals and peoples, especially those of minorities; the advancement of women and children; safeguarding the created world—these are all areas which need to be illuminated with the light of the Gospel.

79. We must also remember the vast Areopagus of culture, of universities, of scientific research, and of international relations which promote dialogue and open up new plans for life.

80. Missionary activity still represents today the greatest challenge for the Church. It is ever more evident that the peoples who have not yet received the first proclamation of Christ make up the majority of humanity. Yet it happens that the ends of the earth, which the Gospel must reach, are drawing ever farther away, and Tertullian's saying, that “the Gospel has been proclaimed throughout the earth and to all peoples”43, is very far from its concrete fulfillment: the mission ad gentes is still only beginning. New peoples are appearing on the world stage, and they too have the right to receive the proclamation of salvation. Population growth in the South and the East, in non-Christian countries, is constantly increasing the number of people who are unaware of Christ's Redemption. This concern must become, so to speak, a hunger and thirst to make the Lord known, when we look openly upon the immense horizons of the non-Christian world.

b) The challenges and the difficulties
of the mission

81. The difficulties seem insurmountable and could be discouraging, if it were a merely human work.

82. In some countries missionaries are refused entry; in others, not only evangelization but also conversion, and even Christian worship, are forbidden. Elsewhere the obstacles are of a cultural nature: passing on the Gospel message seems irrelevant or incomprehensible, and conversion is seen as a rejection of one's own people and culture.

83. Nor are there lacking difficulties internal to the People of God, and these are certainly the most painful. St. Paul VI pointed out, in the first place, “the lack of fervor, which is all the more serious because it comes from within. This lack of fervor is manifested in fatigue and disenchantment, in compromise with the surroundings and in lack of interest, and above all in a lack of joy and hope”44.

84. But one of the most serious reasons for the lack of interest in the missionary task is the “indifferentist” mentality, which is unfortunately widespread, even among Christians, often rooted in incorrect theological views and marked by a religious relativism which ends by thinking that “one religion is as good as another.”

c) Evangelization of cultures45

85. The split between the Gospel and culture is without doubt the drama of our time, just as it was of other times. Therefore every effort must be made to ensure a generous evangelization of culture, or more correctly of cultures. They have to be regenerated by an encounter with the Good News. But this encounter will not take place if the Good News is not proclaimed.

86. The Gospel, and therefore evangelization, are certainly not identical with culture, and they are independent in regard to all cultures. Nevertheless, the Kingdom which the Gospel proclaims is lived by men who are profoundly linked to a culture, and the building up of the Kingdom cannot avoid borrowing the elements of the human cultures into which it is grafted. Though independent of cultures, the Gospel and evangelization are not necessarily incompatible with them; rather, they are capable of permeating them all without becoming subject to any one of them46.

87. The evangelization of culture must be carried out in a language that people understand. This will often call for a certain adaptation, which must in no case and in no way compromise the Gospel message in its full extent. Every adaptation requires much discernment, seriousness, respect, and the necessary competence that the subject or matter demands, whether in the field of liturgical expression, of catechesis, of theological formulation, of secondary ecclesial structures, or of ministries. Language should be understood here not so much on the semantic or literary level as on that which may be called anthropological and cultural.

88. The work of the evangelization of culture is undoubtedly one of the objectives of the missionary; it is an extremely important, serious and delicate work, and for this reason the missionary must always be ready to follow the directives of the Institute and the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church.

89. Evangelization loses much of its force and effectiveness if it does not take into consideration the actual people to whom it is addressed, if it does not use their language, their signs and symbols, if it does not answer the questions they ask, and if it does not have an impact on their concrete life. But on the other hand, evangelization risks losing its soul and vanishing if its content is emptied or adulterated under the pretext of translating it; if, in the desire to adapt a universal reality to a local setting, this reality is sacrificed and the unity without which there is no universality is destroyed. Now, only a Church which preserves the awareness of her universality and shows that she is in fact universal is capable of having a message which can be heard by all, beyond regional limits, throughout the whole world.

90. As she carries out her missionary activity among the peoples, the Church encounters different cultures and becomes involved in the process of inculturation. Inculturation is a slow journey which accompanies the whole of missionary life and requires the contribution of the various co-workers. Missionaries, who come from other Churches and countries, must immerse themselves in the socio-cultural world of those to whom they are sent, moving beyond the conditioning of their own original environment.

91. In this regard, certain guidelines are fundamental. Inculturation, in its correct process, must be guided by two principles: “compatibility with the Gospel of the various cultures to be assumed, and communion with the universal Church”47. It is necessary to guard “the deposit of faith,” taking care of fidelity and, above all, of discernment48, for which a deep balance is required; indeed, “there is the risk of passing uncritically from a kind of alienation from culture to an overestimation of culture, which is a product of man and is therefore marked by sin. Culture too must be ‘purified, elevated and perfected’”49.

4.

Primary objectives

a) Christian conversion

92. The proclamation of the Word of God aims at full and sincere adherence to Christ and his Gospel through faith. Conversion means accepting, by a personal decision, the saving sovereignty of Christ and becoming his disciple.

93. The Church calls all people to this conversion, following the example of John the Baptist, who prepared the way for Christ by proclaiming a baptism of conversion for the forgiveness of sins (Mk 1:4), and following in the footsteps of Christ himself, who, after John was handed over, went…into Galilee and proclaimed the Good News of God: “The time is fulfilled, and the Kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the Good News” (Mk 1:14-15).

94. Conversion calls for Baptism. The Apostles, moved by the Holy Spirit, invited all to change their lives, to be converted and to be baptized: when they heard [Peter's words] they were cut to the heart and said to Peter and the other Apostles: “Brothers, what are we to do?” Peter answered them: “Repent, and let each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit” (Acts 2:37-38). Conversion to Christ is joined to Baptism not only because of the Church's practice, but also by the will of Christ himself, who sent the Apostles to make disciples of all nations and to baptize them50; it is also joined to it by the intrinsic need to receive the fullness of new life in him: truly, truly, I say to you—Jesus says to Nicodemus— unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the Kingdom of God (Jn 3:5).

95. All this must be recalled because not a few, precisely in those places where the mission ad gentes is carried out, tend to separate conversion to Christ from Baptism, regarding Baptism as unnecessary. We are convinced, however, that if a person feels the attraction of Christ, Christ himself will lead him to the Church as to the “place” where they can truly encounter him.

b) Planting the Church

96. The mission ad gentes also has as its objective the founding of Christian communities and the development of Churches to their full maturity, as we have treated and developed above51. This is the central and specific goal of missionary activity, to the point that this activity cannot be considered accomplished until it succeeds in building up a new particular Church which functions normally in its local setting.

97. It is necessary, first of all, to strive to establish in every place Christian communities which are a “sign of the presence of God in the world”52 and which grow until they become Churches.

c) Ecumenism

98. The Second Vatican Council's Decree on Ecumenism states explicitly: “it is only through Christ's Catholic Church, which is the all-embracing means of salvation, that the fullness of the means of salvation can be obtained. We believe that our Lord entrusted all the blessings of the New Covenant to the one apostolic college presided over by Peter, in order to establish on earth the one Body of Christ into which all those should be fully incorporated who already belong in any way to the People of God”53.

99. This concern will be a motive and a stimulus for renewed ecumenical action. The links between ecumenical activity and missionary activity make it necessary to consider both as two concomitant factors.

100. Ecumenical activity and the harmonious witness to Jesus Christ by Christians belonging to different Churches and ecclesial communities have already borne abundant fruit. It is ever more urgent that they cooperate and bear united witness at this time, when Christian and para-Christian sects are sowing confusion by their activity. The expansion of these sects represents a threat to the Catholic Church and to all the ecclesial communities with which she is engaged in dialogue.

d) Interreligious dialogue

101. Without doubt, interreligious dialogue is part of the Church's evangelizing mission; indeed, it has special links with that mission and is one of its expressions. In fact, this mission is addressed to those who do not know Christ and his Gospel, and who for the most part belong to other religions. In Christ, God calls all peoples to himself, wishing to share with them the fullness of his revelation and love, and he does not fail to make himself present in many ways, not only to individuals but also to entire peoples through their spiritual riches, of which their religions are the main and essential expression, even when they contain “gaps, insufficiencies and errors”54. All this has been amply emphasized by the Second Vatican Council and the subsequent Magisterium, always maintaining that salvation comes from Christ and that dialogue does not dispense from evangelization.

102. “A vast field lies open to dialogue, which can assume many forms and expressions: from exchanges between experts in religious traditions or official representatives of those traditions, to cooperation for integral development and the safeguarding of religious values; and from a sharing of their respective spiritual experiences to the so-called ‘dialogue of life,’ through which believers of different religions bear witness to one another in daily life to their own human and spiritual values, and help each other to live according to those values in order to build a more just and fraternal society. […] Dialogue is a path toward the Kingdom and will certainly bear fruit, even if the times and seasons are known only to the Father (cf. Acts 1:7)”55.

e) Human promotion

103. Another concern will be to promote the development of persons, not in just any way, but through the education of consciences.

104. The mission ad gentes is still carried out today, for the most part, in those regions of the southern hemisphere where action on behalf of integral development and liberation from all forms of oppression is most urgently needed. The Church has always known how to awaken among the peoples she evangelizes an impulse toward progress, and now, more than in the past, missionaries are also recognized as promoters of development by governments and international experts, who marvel at the fact that remarkable results are achieved with scarce means.

105. Pope St. John Paul II affirmed that “the Church does not have technical solutions to offer for the problem of underdevelopment as such,” but that she “makes her first contribution to the solution of the urgent problem of development when she proclaims the truth about Christ, about herself and about man, applying this truth to a concrete situation”56. “The best service we can offer to our brother is evangelization, which prepares him to fulfill himself as a child of God, frees him from injustice and promotes him integrally”57. In this way the Church will teach peoples that what matters most is not “having more” but “being more,” awakening consciences through the Gospel. Authentic human development must be rooted in an ever deeper evangelization, since the development of man comes from God, from the model of Jesus, God and man, and must lead back to God. That is why there is a close connection between the proclamation of the Gospel and human promotion.

106. The contribution of the Church and of her evangelizing work to the development of peoples concerns not only the countries regarded as economically less developed, but also those which, possessing an abundance of material wealth, are exposed to the moral and spiritual poverty frequently caused by “superdevelopment.”

5.

The missionaries

a) Spiritual and moral formation

107. The missionaries of our Institute “must be prepared by a special spiritual and moral formation for so lofty a task. They must therefore be capable of taking initiative, constant in bringing works to completion, persevering in difficulties, bearing loneliness, weariness and fruitless labor with patience and fortitude”58.

108. In order to acquire the spiritual and moral temper that will enable the missionary to work fruitfully for as long as his apostolic life lasts, those who are being prepared in our houses of formation must be tested and corrected by their formators. The missionary religious vocation involves many renunciations: a candidate who, given the due time and circumstances, has not reached a good level of spiritual and moral maturity, shown in deeds and attested by those who live with him, should not be admitted to sacred orders, at least in our Institute. The Directory of Spirituality of our Institute asks that formators have a “clear intention of making a most rigorous selection; this is of capital importance for maintaining a good spirit. Knowing that in selecting it is better to err by excess than by defect”59; this truth acquires even greater force, if that is possible, when it concerns candidates who will be sent to the missions. Experience has shown that whoever does not want to live the religious life authentically not only does not help to carry our missions forward but becomes a real obstacle. “Much depends on the prudence and responsibility of the superiors not to put the Institute in this grave difficulty and not to send to the missions individuals who, instead of being a help and a source of growth, turn out in the end to be a burden and a harm”60. On the contrary, it is precisely those persons who work effectively and fruitfully at overcoming their spiritual and moral limitations during the period of formation who will later be capable of carrying religious communities forward with ever greater fruit, in spite of all the difficulties proper to the mission.

109. Those who are being prepared in our houses of formation to accomplish great works for the glory of God in the missions to which they will be sent should, so as not to deceive themselves with vain fantasies, cultivate a great love for the virtues that form the basis of spiritual growth, such as humility, charity and docility to their Superiors. “It is not enough that nothing negative be found in our candidates; it is not enough that they be sufficiently diligent in study and in outward discipline. One must study their character, gauge the fervor of their spirit, the absolute submission of their will, their generosity in sacrifice, their spirit of initiative, their fidelity to duty”61. Hardly suited for the mission will be those types of personality who “know it all,” who “get along badly with everyone,” who find fault with everything, who argue about everything, or who neither listen to nor obey anyone except when others agree with what they think. The reason why such characters will not be able to bear fruit is that God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble (1 Pet 5:5). And if God resists a missionary, what will he be able to do?

b) Cultural, doctrinal
and apostolic formation

110. It is fundamental that those who are to be sent as missionaries to the various parts of the world be true and faithful ministers of Christ, and that they “be nourished on the words of the faith and of good doctrine (1 Tim 4:6), which they will draw above all from Sacred Scripture, studying in depth the mystery of Christ, whose heralds and witnesses they are to be”62.

111. “All missionaries—priests, brothers, sisters and lay people—each according to their own state, must be prepared and trained, lest they be found unequal to the demands of their future work. From the very beginning their doctrinal formation must be so planned that it embraces both the universality of the Church and the diversity of peoples”63.

112. “Whoever is to go to a foreign people should hold in due esteem their heritage, their language and their customs. It is especially necessary for the future missionary to devote himself to missiological studies, that is, to know the teaching and norms of the Church regarding missionary activity, to know what paths the heralds of the Gospel have trodden over the course of the centuries, the present situation of the missions, and also the methods considered most effective today”64.

113. Regarding the attitude of the missionary toward the place where he will have to exercise his apostolate, the words of the conciliar decree Ad gentes are very profitable: “It is necessary that those who devote themselves to missionary activity for a set period of time receive a formation suited to their condition. But this varied formation must be completed in the region to which they will be sent, so that the missionaries may have a broad knowledge of the history, social structures and customs of the peoples; that they be well informed about the moral order, the religious precepts, and their way of thinking about God, the world and man, according to their sacred traditions. Let them learn the languages to the point of being able to use them with ease and elegance, and thereby find easier access to the minds and hearts of men. They must be properly introduced into the pastoral needs characteristic of each people. Some should also prepare themselves more thoroughly in missiological institutes or other faculties or universities, in order to discharge special duties more effectively and to be able, with their knowledge, to help other missionaries in carrying out their work, which presents so many difficulties and opportunities, especially in our time”65.

114. Following the mind of the documents of the Church, we wish to give special attention to the study of the language of the place where one is on mission. It is a historical fact that wherever the Church used the vernacular languages in her evangelizing work, the result was much greater and more lasting. Hearing the Good News in one's mother tongue will foster an authentic inculturation of the Gospel. To this end, let persons be assigned to each mission who can come to master the language or languages of the place, and let the necessary means for learning them be provided. For this purpose it will be necessary to have missionaries who can remain in those places for a sufficiently long time. Learning the language and being able to produce lasting fruit in the work of inculturation ordinarily takes several years.

115. The experience gained over the years of the Institute of the Incarnate Word's existence proves the usefulness of all missionaries knowing the English language. In an increasingly globalized world, English has already become the lingua franca. A good knowledge of this language will be a very valuable tool for the missionary. Knowing a language opens doors not only to dealing with more people, but also to a much greater base of information, both in the printed press and on the internet. Let formators and local Superiors see to it that all, to the extent of their abilities, may learn to handle themselves fluently in this language.

116. It is also a fact of experience, which ought always to be remembered in our houses of formation, that every art learned by the future missionary will ordinarily be of great pastoral benefit to him. Whoever knows and is able to pass on notions of the fine arts (music, theater, painting, sculpture and many others), languages, computing, manual or sporting skills, and all that knowledge which is generally regarded as positive by every culture, will have elements of great value for relating to the peoples he is to evangelize. For a missionary, this relationship at a merely cultural level is an authentic pre-evangelization.

117. Missionaries must humbly accept that at least the first period of their stay in a new region is in large part a continuation of their period of formation, not only with regard to languages, but also to customs, ways of behaving, etc. Let them pay attention to the advice of those who have gone before them, in particular of the members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word already present in the region.

c) Primary importance of witness

118. There is no witness without witnesses, nor is there mission without missionaries. So that they may cooperate in his mission and continue his saving work, Jesus chooses and sends certain persons as his witnesses and Apostles: You shall be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). The Good News must be proclaimed first of all through witness; all Christians are called to this witness and, in this sense, can be true evangelizers: Maintain exemplary conduct among the Gentiles, so that in the very matter in which they slander you as evildoers, they may see your good works and glorify God on the day of visitation (1 Pet 2:12).

119. The centuries-long experience of religious institutes shows that the first witness given in mission lands is the fraternal life of the missionaries. This alone is already mission. In some places and times it is the only thing that can be done. In such circumstances let the missionary not think that he is wasting his time, since there is no better witness than that of Christian charity. In fact, a fraternal life lived as it should be necessarily attracts well-disposed people, and it is very likely that God will bless that witness with even unexpected fruits.

120. For this reason it is of primary importance that those who are preparing for the mission cultivate a healthy love for the community in which they live, without in fact excluding anyone, especially the more difficult characters. This must be understood literally: if the future missionary finds difficulty with persons in the formative setting in which he finds himself, let him strive earnestly not to exclude them from his “circle of friends,” but let him work tenaciously to win those persons over as far as he possibly can, and let him go to great lengths in charity toward them as toward everyone else, and try to discover in them the will of God and the shortest path to holiness. For if you love those who love you, what reward will you have? (Mt 5:46). If possible, so far as it depends on you, live at peace with all (Rom 12:18).

121. If in mission lands the religious communities are truly such, and in them there is an effort to live fraternal charity intensely with each and every one of their members66, then much fruit can be hoped for. If, on the contrary, a missionary does not know how to live with his peers, is ignorant of the fundamental principles of dialogue, does not practice fraternal charity de facto, and makes everything revolve around himself, then in that community a good share of energy will have to be spent merely in bearing with the weak brother.

122. The consideration of this aspect of religious life should not seem to any member of our Institute something secondary or incidental. If we do not have good communities we will not be able to accomplish anything of importance; on the contrary, where communities live righteousness, peace and joy in the Holy Spirit (Rom 14:17), there no undertaking, however great, will be impossible to carry out. Let all work rigorously in this direction from the first years of formation. The best way for a religious to know whether he is a builder, or merely a consumer or, worse still, a destroyer of community, is this: let him ask his peers of virtuous life and his Superior, and let him willingly accept the answer.

123. Finally, let the missionary remember that the best witness he will be able to give will be the gift of his own life, even to the point of accepting death in order to bear witness to faith in Jesus Christ and to love of neighbor. “The ‘martyrs,’ that is, the witnesses, are many and indispensable to the progress of the Gospel. In our own time too there are many: bishops, priests, men and women religious, and lay people—often unknown heroes who give their lives to bear witness to the faith. They are par excellence the heralds and witnesses”67. “I would not exchange prison for the gift of working miracles, nor martyrdom for the apostolate, which was the dream of my life”68.

d) The need for an explicit proclamation

124. Although the witness of life is the first proclamation, it is nevertheless necessary that our witness also be presented in an explicit way, that is, that it be justified and preached; this is what the Apostle St. Peter called giving a reason for your hope (1 Pet 3:15), “made explicit by a clear and unequivocal proclamation of the Lord Jesus. The Good News proclaimed by the witness of life sooner or later has to be proclaimed by the word of life. There is no true evangelization if the name, the teaching, the life, the promises, the Kingdom and the mystery of Jesus of Nazareth, the Son of God, are not proclaimed”69.

125. The experience already gained by our Institute in mission lands teaches that there is a significant danger to which everyone, and in particular the Superiors of missionary communities, must always be alert. We are referring to the temptation to dilute the Gospel message so as to present it without its saving specificity. The danger is particularly real and harmful when our missionaries come into contact with other religious who do not have a Catholic vision of the mission ad gentes and who have worked out “their own theology,” so to speak, according to which the Church should not so much “proclaim” the Gospel of Jesus Christ as “walk alongside” the local cultures, “learn from them,” etc.

126. The temptation becomes even more subtle when a member of our Institute, in discerning the work done by other missionaries of doubtful Catholic identity, confuses in them two realities that must be carefully distinguished. Indeed, there may be in those missionaries a holy and praiseworthy dedication to work in particularly difficult lands, and this even for many years; while this dedication is worthy of imitation and of all merit, there may nevertheless be another aspect that is not so, such as the missionary's doubtful Catholic identity. A missionary may have the very best dispositions and win everyone over by his goodness and dedication, but in addition he must have a Catholic mind and heart, and our Institute wishes to preserve and increase always and everywhere its proper charism in fidelity to the Magisterium of the Church. Any missionary who doubts the teachings of the Church and goes after “pluralist” or “relativist” theological doctrines must be duly corrected by his Superior and, should he refuse to accept that correction, must be removed from the mission.

127. To avoid reaching such extremes, let the Superiors of missionary communities be very attentive to what their subjects read and to those with whom they associate, and let them foster the knowledge and study of Catholic doctrine on everything concerning the spirit of the Institute, its charism and its mission.

128. The clear Catholic identity of the message does not prevent the virtue of pastoral prudence from often dictating a less explicit witness to the Good News, as might occur in certain situations of serious intolerance or religious persecution. In such cases the temporary and circumstantial lack of a more explicit proclamation will be seen as a preparation of the ground for a clearer preaching of the Good News when God so disposes, in the way St. Charles de Foucauld bore witness to and lived the Gospel.

129. The concrete decisions to be taken in circumstances of intolerance or religious persecution are the responsibility of the competent Superiors; before taking such decisions, they must consult the patrimony of the Institute, the experience gained in other places, and the opinion of missionaries of proven virtue and Catholicity. Once an opinion on the way of acting has been obtained, they must present their proposals to the General Government so that measures may be taken which neither endanger the presence of the Institute and of the Church in those places, on the one hand, nor harm the identity and fruits of the missionary proclamation in those territories, on the other.

e) Come… and rest a while (Mk 6:31)

130. As in the time of Jesus, the apostolic tasks of the missionary usually involve physical, spiritual, intellectual and psychological work that absorbs all his energies. If the missionary applies himself tenaciously to his work, he must also take care to know how to rest. When the Apostles found themselves immersed in a thousand holy occupations, the Lord said to them: come away by yourselves to a deserted place, and rest a while (Mk 6:31).

131. The missionary, like any other laborer in the Lord's vineyard, must be watchful so as not to fall into a certain activism. The words of Benedict XVI, who referred to this theme on various occasions, are very eloquent. The Pope says that rest is necessary “to strengthen the mind and body, subjected every day to continual fatigue and wear, due to the frenetic pace of modern life”70. Commenting on St. Bernard's advice to Pope Eugene III, he recalls “the importance of inner recollection, an essential element of piety. The saint affirms that it is necessary to avoid the dangers of excessive activity, whatever one's condition and office, because—as he says to the Pope of that time, to all Popes, and to all of us—many occupations frequently lead to ‘hardness of heart,’ ‘they are nothing but suffering for the spirit, loss of understanding, dispersion of grace.’” And he continues: “This warning applies to every kind of occupation, including those inherent in the government of the Church. The message which, in this regard, St. Bernard addresses to the Pontiff, who had been his disciple at Clairvaux, is provocative: ‘See,’ he writes, ‘where these accursed occupations can drag you, if you go on losing yourself in them…, leaving nothing of yourself for yourself.’ How useful this warning about the primacy of prayer and contemplation is for us too!”71.

132. In this same vein it is fitting to recall what our Constitutions say: “The members of the Institute will also know how to interrupt their apostolic works with periods of retreat and spiritual refreshment, knowing that no one can procure the sanctification of others unless he first possesses the perfection he wishes to transmit”72.

133. It is important that the Superiors of apostolic communities take into account the wear and tear proper to the mission and offer their subjects various ways of being able to rest, particularly by leading a strong community life. Let them not hesitate to use all the means at their disposal so that, while always safeguarding the spirit and discipline of religious life, the missionaries may “recover their strength” through well-deserved rest. The particulars of rest will vary according to place, but there are elements that may be common to all missionary communities, such as sports, weekly or monthly community outings, eutrapelia, gatherings, etc. It will also be necessary to give due importance to the time of annual vacation stipulated in each case.

134. One must avoid the temptation of thinking that time given to good rest is time lost; let suggestions be held in suspicion that would lead to suppressing healthy moments of rest and recreation in community under the pretext of greater asceticism or a greater need for apostolic work.

135. Given the characteristics of work in mission lands, Superiors will also consider special and prolonged times of rest, particularly when they see that a missionary has flagged in spirit or shows signs of a certain weariness. A few months in another community or engaged in other activities, under the guidance and supervision of a qualified Superior, can restore to the missionary the spirit and strength that may perhaps have been spent in apostolic battles. It is not a time in which “nothing is done,” but one in which the missionary can recover his physical energies, resume his intellectual work which has perhaps been somewhat neglected, more easily enjoy the company of good friends, bear witness to his missionary life, etc.

f) Consecrated life, a privileged means for the mission

136. Let the religious of our Institute know that consecrated life is “a privileged means for effective evangelization”73. Religious life is, in fact, a call to holiness, and it is to this holiness that we must bear witness before a world thirsting for God.

137. The silent witness of poverty and detachment, of purity and transparency, of self-abandonment in obedience, can be at once a challenge to the world and to the Church herself, an eloquent sermon, capable of touching even non-Christians of good will who are sensitive to certain values.

That we may be servants of the truth74

138. The Gospel which the missionary proclaims is also the word of truth. A truth which makes us free (cf. Jn 8:32) and which alone gives peace of heart; this is what people are seeking when we proclaim the Good News to them. The truth about God, the truth about man and his mysterious destiny, the truth about the world. A difficult truth that we seek in the Word of God and of which we are neither the masters nor the arbiters, but the depositaries, the heirs, the servants.

139. The Church and the people expect missionaries to possess a fervent devotion to the truth, since the truth which they study in depth and communicate is none other than revealed Truth and, therefore, more than any other, it shares in the first truth, which is God himself. The preacher of the Gospel will be one who, even at the cost of renunciation and sacrifice, always seeks the truth that he must transmit to others. He never sells or conceals the truth out of a desire to please men, to cause astonishment, or out of originality or a wish to make an impression. He never refuses the truth. He does not obscure revealed truth through laziness in seeking it, for the sake of his own comfort, or out of fear. He does not neglect to study it. He serves it generously without enslaving it.

Animated by love75

140. The missionary does not perform the task of a functionary: the proclamation of the Gospel presupposes and demands in the missionary an ever-growing fraternal love for those whom he evangelizes, like St. Paul, the missionary to the Gentiles: so, being affectionately desirous of you, we were ready to share with you not only the Gospel of God but also our own lives, because you had become very dear to us (1 Thess 2:8; cf. Phil 1:8).

141. What kind of love is this? It is much more than that of a teacher; it is the love of a father; and even more, that of a mother (cf. 1 Thess 2:7.11; 1 Cor 4:15; Gal 4:19). Such is the love that the Lord expects from every preacher of the Gospel, from every builder of the Church.

142. A sign of love will be the desire to offer the truth and to lead to unity. A sign of love will likewise be to devote oneself without reserve and without looking back to the proclamation of Jesus Christ. Other signs of this love will be:

a) Respect for the religious and spiritual situation of the person being evangelized. Respect for his pace, which must not be forced too much; respect for his conscience and his convictions, which must not be treated harshly.

b) Care not to wound others, especially if they are weak in their faith (cf. 1 Cor 8:9-13; Rom 14:15), with statements that may be clear to the faithful but that can be a source of bewilderment or scandal to the newly initiated, inflicting a wound on their souls.

c) The effort made to transmit to Christians solid certainties based on the Word of God, and not doubts or uncertainties born of poorly assimilated erudition. The faithful need these certainties in their Christian life; they have a right to them inasmuch as they are children of God who, placing themselves in his arms, abandon themselves totally to the demands of love.

With the fervor of the Saints76

143. We must proclaim the Gospel with the fervor and enthusiasm of the saints, even in moments of difficulty and persecution in a de-Christianized and atheistic world. The saints are a most eloquent sign of the Church's vitality; they have always transformed the world. They have been the true reformers of the world and of the Church. They are the finest members of the Mystical Body of Christ. They are the greatest and most complete fruit of the Incarnation and the Redemption. The saints have been the eminent witnesses and guardians of the divine Tradition of the Church; that is, by their lives they recall and pass on the very breath of the Church. They knew how to overcome all the obstacles that stood in the way of evangelization, and therefore they are models to be followed and to whom we must turn in this work.

144. We must preserve, cultivate and ask God for spiritual fervor, the joy of evangelizing, even when we have to sow in tears. “May it mean for us—as it did for John the Baptist, for Peter and Paul, for the other Apostles, and for a multitude of splendid evangelizers all through the Church's history—an interior enthusiasm that nobody and nothing can quench. May it be the great joy of our consecrated lives. And may the world of our time, which is searching, sometimes with anguish, sometimes with hope, be enabled to receive the Good News not from evangelizers who are dejected, discouraged, impatient or anxious, but from ministers of the Gospel whose lives glow with fervor, who have first received the joy of Christ, and who are willing to risk their lives so that the Kingdom may be proclaimed and the Church established in the midst of the world”77.

g) Missionaries ad vitam and missionary cooperation

A special vocation

145. The existence of missionaries in the Church has been emphasized by the Second Vatican Council: “Although the task of spreading the faith, according to his state, falls upon every disciple of Christ, Christ the Lord always calls from among his disciples those whom he wills, so that they may be with him and so that he may send them to preach to the nations”78.

146. It is, then, a “special vocation” which has as its model that of the Apostles: it is manifested in a total commitment to the service of evangelization; it is a self-giving that involves the missionary's whole person and whole life, demanding of him a limitless gift of energy and time.

147. “The special vocation of missionaries ad vitam retains all its validity: it is the model of the Church's missionary commitment, which always stands in need of radical and total self-giving, of new and bold endeavors. Therefore the men and women missionaries who have devoted their whole lives to bearing witness to the risen Lord among the nations must not allow themselves to be daunted by doubts, misunderstanding, rejection or persecution. They should revive the grace of their specific charism and courageously continue on their way, preferring—in a spirit of faith, obedience and communion with their Pastors—the humblest and most difficult places”79.

148. Given the missionary character of our Institute, we must use all the necessary resources, our experience and creativity, in fidelity to the original charism, to prepare ourselves adequately and so that there may be no lack of missionaries, thus ensuring the renewal of the spiritual, moral and physical energies of our members.

Each according to his condition and ability

149. Our priests are impelled by the sacrament of Holy Orders: “The spiritual gift which priests have received in ordination prepares them, not for any narrow and limited mission, but for the most universal and all-embracing mission of salvation ‘to the end of the earth,’ for every priestly ministry shares in the universal scope of the mission entrusted by Christ to his Apostles”80.

150. Our members of contemplative life will respond to the invitation of the Second Vatican Council, repeated by St. John Paul II, to establish communities in the young Churches, so as to give “splendid witness among non-Christians to the majesty and love of God, as well as to unity in Christ”81.

151. For our members of active life immense spaces open up for charity, the proclamation of the Gospel, Christian education, culture, and solidarity with the poor, the discriminated against, the marginalized and the oppressed.

152. Our missionary religious brothers will translate their consecration of poverty, chastity and obedience for the sake of the Kingdom into manifold fruits of paternity according to the Spirit. Precisely the mission ad gentes offers them a vast field in which to give themselves with a total and undivided love.

153. The members of the Third Order, in their own measure, are not excluded from the missionary urgency. The mission belongs to the whole People of God. The participation of the laity in the spread of the faith is clearly evident from the earliest days of Christianity, through the efforts of individual faithful and families, and also of the entire community. They therefore have the general obligation, and enjoy the right, both individually and in association, to work so that the divine message of salvation may be known and received by all people throughout the world. Moreover, given their own secular character, they have the specific vocation “to seek the Kingdom of God by engaging in temporal affairs and ordering them according to the plan of God”82. As circumstances permit, let consideration be given to the possibility of inviting members of the Third Order to take part, for a set period of time, in the missions ad gentes that have been entrusted to the Institute.

154. In this way all the members of our Institutes, by virtue of Baptism, share responsibility for missionary activity. “The Council invites all to a profound interior renewal, so that, having a lively awareness of their own responsibility for the spreading of the Gospel, they may accept their share in missionary work among the nations”83.

Missionary cooperation and animation

155. Among the forms of participation, first place belongs to spiritual cooperation: prayer, sacrifice and the witness of Christian life. The sacrifice of the missionary must be shared and supported by that of all the faithful. For this reason, we urge those who exercise their pastoral ministry among the sick to instruct them on the value of suffering, encouraging them to offer it to God for missionaries.

156. Cooperation is also shown in promoting missionary vocations within one's family and surroundings. It is striking that several nations, while increasing their monetary donations, show an ever lower rate of missionary vocations, which reflect the true dimension of self-giving to one's brothers. Vocations to the priesthood and to consecrated life are a sure sign of the vitality of a Church.

157. The material and financial needs of the missions are many: not only for founding the Church with minimal structures (chapels, schools for catechists and seminarians, housing), but also for supporting works of charity, education and human promotion—an immense field of action, especially in poor countries. The missionary Church gives what she receives; she distributes to the poor what her children who are better off in material resources generously place at her disposal.

158. In our parishes, therefore, missionary animation must be included as a primary element of ordinary pastoral care, in associations as well as in groups, especially youth groups. To achieve this end, information through the missionary press and the various audiovisual media is valuable above all. Animation activities must always be directed toward their specific goals: to inform and form the People of God for the universal mission of the Church; to promote vocations ad gentes; to foster cooperation in the work of evangelization. Indeed, one cannot give a reductive picture of missionary activity, as if it were principally help to the poor, a contribution to the liberation of the oppressed, the promotion of development, or the defense of human rights. The missionary Church is also involved on these fronts, but her primary task lies elsewhere: the poor are hungry for God, and not just for bread and freedom; missionary activity must first of all bear witness to and proclaim salvation in Christ, establishing local Churches which then become instruments of liberation in every sense.

159. We will carry out all our missionary undertakings in communion with the Church, aware that it belongs to the missionary Dicastery to direct and coordinate throughout the world the work of the evangelization of peoples and missionary cooperation.

h) A proper spirituality

160. Missionary activity demands a specific spirituality, which applies in particular to those whom God has called to be missionaries.

161. “This spirituality is expressed first of all by a life of complete docility to the Spirit; it commits us to being molded from within by him, so that we may become ever more like Christ. Docility to the Spirit also commits us to welcoming the gifts of fortitude and discernment, which are essential elements of missionary spirituality”84.

162. “An essential characteristic of missionary spirituality is intimate communion with Christ: we cannot understand or carry out the mission except in reference to Christ, sent by the Father to evangelize (cf. Lk 4:18.21). Paul describes his attitudes: Have among yourselves the same mind that was in Christ: who, though he was in the form of God, did not cling greedily to being equal with God, but emptied Himself, taking the condition of a servant, becoming like men and appearing in his bearing as a man; and he humbled Himself, being obedient unto death, even death on a cross (Phil 2:5-8)”85.

163. “The missionary is required ‘to renounce himself and everything that up to this point he considered as his own, and to make himself everything to everyone’: in a poverty which sets him free for the Gospel; in detachment from the people and things of his own surroundings, so as to become a brother to those to whom he is sent and bring them Christ the Savior. This is the goal of missionary spirituality: I became weak with the weak… I have become all things to all, so as to save some at all costs. And I do all this for the sake of the Gospel (1 Cor 9:22-23)”86.

164. “Missionary spirituality is also marked by apostolic charity, the charity of Christ who came to gather into one the children of God who were scattered (Jn 11:52)”87.

165. “The missionary is moved by ‘zeal for souls,’ a zeal inspired by Christ's own charity, which takes the form of attentiveness, tenderness, compassion, openness, availability, and interest in people's problems. Jesus' love is very deep: he who knew what was in man (Jn 2:25) loved everyone by offering them redemption, and he suffered when it was rejected”88.

166. “The missionary is a person of charity: in order to be able to proclaim to every person that he is loved by God and is himself capable of loving, he must bear witness to charity toward all, spending his life for his neighbor. […] Like Christ, he must love the Church: Christ loved the Church and gave Himself up for her (Eph 5:25). […] For every missionary and every community, fidelity to Christ cannot be separated from fidelity to his Church”89.

167. The call to mission derives, of its nature, from the call to holiness. The universal vocation to holiness is closely linked to the universal vocation to mission. Every member of the faithful is called to holiness and to mission. The missionary spirituality of the Church is a journey toward holiness.

168. “The renewed impulse to the mission ad gentes demands holy missionaries. It is not enough to update pastoral techniques, organize and coordinate ecclesial resources, or delve more deeply into the biblical and theological foundations of faith: what is needed is the encouragement of a new ‘ardor for holiness’ among missionaries and throughout the Christian community, especially among those who work most closely with missionaries”90. “The missionary must be a ‘contemplative in action’ who finds answers to problems in the light of the Word of God through personal and community prayer”91.

169. “The missionary is a person of the Beatitudes. Before sending out the Twelve to evangelize, Jesus instructs them, pointing out to them the paths of mission: poverty, meekness, acceptance of suffering and persecution, the desire for justice and peace, charity—in other words, he points out to them precisely the Beatitudes, practiced in the apostolic life (cf. Mt 5:1-12). By living the Beatitudes, the missionary experiences and shows concretely that the Kingdom of God has already come and that he has accepted it. The characteristic of every authentic missionary life is the inner joy that comes from faith. In a world tormented and oppressed by so many problems, a world tempted to pessimism, the one who proclaims the ‘Good News’ must be a person who has found true hope in Christ”92.

6.

Mary, model of the missionary

170. The Most Blessed Virgin Mary occupies a privileged place in the work of man's Redemption accomplished by her Son: “With him and in dependence on him, she placed herself, by the grace of almighty God, at the service of the mystery of Redemption”93. In this way she is, for the Church, Mother and model in the mission which the Church received from the Savior.

171. Indeed, Mary, according to the divine good pleasure, even after her glorification in heaven, has continued to show, in her various apparitions to the men and women of every age and place, what the attitude of the Lord's disciples ought to be in the work of Redemption. She has drawn near to the humblest and has simply adapted herself to the language and the capacity of reception of her sons and daughters, even in the outward appearance of her face. All this with one sole aim: to form, in the school of prayer and penance and of the following of Jesus in the various states of life, true disciples who actively participate in the redemptive task with her same attitude: “that maternal love by which all who join in the Church's apostolic mission for the regeneration of men should be animated”94.

172. The Church, missionary by her very essence, finds in Mary an eminent model to follow in her task of inculturating the Gospel, especially when she is sent by Christ ad gentes. Throughout the centuries her finest sons and daughters have known how to respond to the Lord's calls by entrusting their lives and their various apostolic tasks entirely to the protection of the Mother of God.

173. Countless are the concrete examples of missionary saints who entrusted to Mary their efforts, their difficulties and their daily joys in the task of reaching every person and every culture in order to offer the Gospel of Redemption. On many occasions they placed under the patronage of the Blessed Virgin, invoked under one of her many titles, the mission in territories and among cultures where the proclamation was being made for the first time.

174. Mary's example is particularly illuminating when she proclaims at one and the same time “the greatness of the Lord” and the “lowliness of his handmaid,” showing—centuries ahead of false controversies—that the proclamation of Jesus Christ is not arrogance, but a work of justice and charity.

175. May the first missionary after her Son Jesus Christ give us that soul and that heart as great as the world, which is the heart that the missionary must have.

Conclusion

At the beginning of the third millennium of the Redemption, “God is preparing a great springtime for Christianity, and we can already see its first signs. In fact, both in the non-Christian world and in the world of ancient Christian tradition, people are gradually drawing closer to Gospel ideals and values, a development which the Church seeks to encourage. Today in fact there is a new consensus among peoples about these values: the rejection of violence and war; respect for the human person and for human rights; the desire for freedom, justice and brotherhood; the surmounting of different forms of racism and nationalism; the affirmation of the dignity and role of women. Christian hope sustains us in committing ourselves fully to the new evangelization and to the worldwide mission, and leads us to pray as Jesus taught us: Thy Kingdom come, Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven (Mt 6:10). […] We cannot be content when we consider the millions of our brothers and sisters, who like us have been redeemed by the blood of Christ but who live in ignorance of the love of God. For each believer, as for the entire Church, the missionary task must remain foremost, for it concerns the eternal destiny of humanity and corresponds to God's mysterious and merciful plan”95.

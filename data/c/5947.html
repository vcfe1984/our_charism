“The Institute of the Incarnate Word will undertake the apostolates most conducive to the inculturation of the Gospel,” we read in no. 169 of the Constitutions. Therefore, “in response to the requests of bishops, the direction of parishes may be accepted, preferably in mission areas or areas of greater need”1.

Thus today the Institute serves some 110 parishes throughout the world, some of them shrines2, others cathedrals3, some in the midst of great cities such as New York or Hong Kong, others in more rural areas such as those in Scotland or Bolivia, some located in residential areas such as the parish in Australia, others in the middle of the jungle, such as the parish the Institute serves in Wagina (Solomon Islands). This means that the immense majority of the more than 400 priests of the Institute are dedicated –in one way or another– to parish ministry, whether as pastors, parochial vicars, members of the community attached to a parish, or occasionally collaborating with the preaching of triduums, novenas, patronal feasts, confessions, etc., that take place in some parish.

Wishing, then, to prolong the redemptive work of Christ himself4, according to our proper charism of inculturating the Gospel in the diversity of all cultures5, the Institute gives the parish apostolate “great importance”6 and “preference”7 –since it is one of the principal “places” where the Institute can exercise its specific charism8– and we consider it a privileged setting for the encounter with the message of Christ9.

That is why we would like to take this opportunity to highlight the characteristics that, according to our proper charism, must shine forth both in the priest dedicated to parish ministry and in the IVE parish itself, if we wish to contribute effectively “to enriching the universal Church”10.

Needless to say, the reading of the Directory of Parishes is highly recommended in this matter; though very brief, it sheds much light on the practice of parish ministry.

Before beginning to address these two points, and by way of introduction, we would like to define what we understand by a parish.

“The word ‘parish’ comes from the Greek para (= near) and oikos (= house), meaning a group of houses or also a group of neighboring inhabitants. Etymologically it means neighborhood, in which Jesus Christ is the principal Neighbor. Others derive the word ‘parish’ from the Greek parojeo (= to transport) or from the Greek parechō (= to supply). Both fit very well with the office of the pastor, who dwells in the midst of his parishioners to supply them with sound doctrine, the sacraments, etc. In fact, three things are understood when speaking of a parish11: 1st The body of the faithful who are under the jurisdiction of the pastor. 2nd The church where he exercises his functions. 3rd The portion of the territory of the diocese”12.

St. John Paul II once affirmed that “it could be said that the parish is a family of families, since the life of the parish is intimately bound up both with the vigor and with the weakness, or with the needs, of the families that make it up”13. And all those families that make up a given Catholic parish are gathered together precisely by their faith in Jesus Christ. In the book of the Acts of the Apostles we read the following about the life of the first Christians: They devoted themselves assiduously to the teaching of the apostles, to communion, to the breaking of bread and to the prayers14. Therefore, instruction in the faith of the Apostles, the building up of a living community, the celebration of the Eucharist and the other sacraments, and the life of prayer constitute the essential elements of the life of every parish15.

In this sense, every parish is founded on a theological reality, and that theological reality is Jesus in the Eucharist. “Without Eucharistic worship as its beating heart, the parish becomes sterile”16.

We therefore consider it fitting to make this clarification explicit: “the parish is not principally the building, nor the institutions, nor the canonical aspect, nor the territory, nor charitable works, nor paperwork. Principally it is the theological reality of being a Eucharistic community. It will also be, but secondarily, the church building, the institutions, the canonical aspect, the territory, help for the poor, and the minimal and indispensable procedures proper to an organized society”17.

1. The IVE pastor

Naturally, when a bishop entrusts a parish to the Institute, he does not entrust it to a religious considered individually but to the Institute as such. Therefore, it is a matter of the presence of the Institute in a diocese and not of religious considered individually18. So we say that when a parish is entrusted to the Institute, “in a certain sense it becomes a religious parish [of the IVE], inasmuch as in such a parish the Institute expresses its proper charism and proper apostolate, even while subject to the bishop”19. Thus, an IVE pastor must shape the parish according to the charism and spirituality of the Institute20. For this it is imperative that the pastor be steeped in the charism of the Institute and show great fidelity to it, including the sound traditions that form part of the Institute’s patrimony21.

Our Proper Law abounds in details about community life and the government of the community of religious in a parish for its harmonious functioning, but we will not deal with that now. We refer the reader to the Directory of Parishes, 8-40. We likewise leave aside consideration of those human aptitudes that so greatly facilitate pastoral work, such as organizational ability, friendliness and good humor, facility for languages, gifts as an educator and catechist, foresight and personal initiative.

Here, rather, we want to speak of the characteristic virtues that should stand out in the conduct of a priest especially dedicated to parish ministry.

▪ Charity

Like every priest, whoever holds the office of pastor is called to reflect the fatherhood of God in daily life. Not for nothing is the name most frequently given us by the sensus fidelium precisely that of father, and parish ministry offers the right setting to exercise spiritual fatherhood with great fruit through the concrete exercise of charity. That is why it is not surprising that the first virtue that must stand out in an IVE pastor is charity, since “charity is indispensable for evangelizing the culture”22.

For, in imitation of the Incarnate Word who came not to be served but to serve23, a pastor is expected to be the first to serve others, sharing with the souls entrusted to him their problems and difficulties.

It is interesting to note that our Proper Law specifically states how this charity is to be manifested according to the kind of sheep he is given to shepherd. Thus, for example, it says that he must lavish “charity on the sick, especially the dying, solicitously strengthening them with the administration of the sacraments and commending their soul to God”24; that an IVE pastor “will always treat with particular diligence the poor, the afflicted, those who are alone, emigrants or those who suffer special difficulties”25; that “he must go to the utmost in charity and kindness”26 with those weak in faith, imitating Christ: He will not quarrel nor cry out… the bruised reed he will not break and the smoldering wick he will not quench, until he brings justice to victory27. He must be affable with all who come to his parish28, with those who enter “the parish office, with each family that receives his visit”29, etc.

Given that the great majority of our parishes are in difficult places (many in places where no one wants to go), where poverty, whether material or spiritual, is the normal setting in which our parishioners’ lives unfold, it seems good to us to recall that “we should be deeply ashamed if ever, straying from the path of concrete charity toward the poor, we stopped attending to them in order to devote our attention only to those sick people, students, etc., who can repay us materially”30. We must beware of the temptation to think that “all the poor who knock at your door are freeloaders”31.

This virtue, which should stand out in priests dedicated to parish ministry, makes evident the need for “the whole formation of candidates for the priesthood to be directed toward preparing them in a specific way to communicate the charity of Christ, the Good Shepherd”32.

▪ Patience

One of the virtues most necessary for parish ministry is certainly patience. That is why our Proper Law warns: “if we do not have patience with the parishioners, then nothing will hold the sheep back and they will go off after false shepherds, because the shepherds God had given them fed themselves”33. And it adds: “in contrast to a ministry of high-handedness, we prefer that of Jesus Christ”34.

This is the patience asked of an IVE pastor in dealing, for example, with those who doubt in the faith, so as to “answer with patience and clarity the objections they raise”35, or with those weak in faith, since “more than once this kind of faithful end up drifting away from the Church of Christ not only because of their weakened faith but above all because of the impatience of the shepherd”36.

St. Manuel González, speaking of the circumstances in which the office of pastor is exercised today, said: “It is true that they could not be worse”37. And in one stroke he illustrates some of those situations that not infrequently test the priest’s patience: “The priest who, as the fruit of a life of sacrifices and bitterness, reaps a church almost always empty; five percent, perhaps, of the faithful fulfilling the Easter and Sunday precept; most of the sick dying impenitent or, at most, with the conditional holy oil; the very men he has favored with jobs, favors or alms turning their faces away so as not to greet him; even those who frequent the church bored, as like as not; and, to cap and crown it all, his works, the best ones, those done with the best intention, misinterpreted or slandered. The priest, I repeat, who sees all this truly needs all the heroism of a martyr to rise each day with a smiling face, a hopeful heart and a spirit tranquil enough to go on plowing his furrow without losing heart and without falling”38.

We must not insist on deceiving ourselves; we must admit that the priest, as a man, is made of the same clay as the children of Adam. And long and sad experience shows that it is a rather fragile and brittle clay. We know, because sacred theology teaches it, that without a most special privilege of grace no one can be exempt from each and every venial sin, and Sacred Scripture says that the just man falls seven times a day. Nevertheless, we can work to modify our character and diminish the number of our faults, and even rid them of their habitual character. But without ever forgetting that, to know what a man is capable of, one must always reckon with the fact that he has a character that some will like and others will not, and faults that will sometimes make him fall short and at other times overshoot39.

That is why St. John Paul II, addressing a group of priests in the city of Prato, emphasized that, if “patience in apostolic work has always been necessary, [it is] all the more so in our times. Patience –the Holy Father clarified– does not mean acquiescence to error, passive tolerance, timid and inert coexistence, giving in to equivocation and ambiguity. Patience means acceptance of the designs of Providence, which respects the times and ways of maturation of each individual and of peoples. To have patience means to eliminate irritations, exasperations, annoyances and also frustrations, demoralization, weariness, in order to commit oneself to the fulfillment of one’s own mission with tireless dedication, in humility and in silence, always with consistency and promptness”40.

▪ Zeal

Our Proper Law says it clearly: “the minister of God… will have a tireless zeal for souls, a spirit of sacrifice”41.

In a well-known sermon St. Alphonsus Mary de Liguori declares that it is “the obligation of all priests to have zeal for souls.” It is said: “There are many priests and there are few; many in name, but few in their works,” and St. Alphonsus adds: “The world is full of priests, but those who strive to be true priests are few, that is, to fulfill the office and dignity of the priest, which is to save souls”42.

Let it be well understood that the great charism of religious and priestly life is generous love for Christ and for the members of his Body. That love is expressed in service and consummated in sacrifice. Therefore, the readiness for self-giving will be proportional to love, and when love is perfect, the sacrifice is complete43. Hence what is proper to us is to be ready to stake our lives so that others may have life and hope44, and to try with “healthy creativity”45 –and without losing heart– to create opportunities to reach all the souls entrusted to us in our priestly ministry. No excuses that ‘one is alone,’ that ‘one has no means’ or that ‘one lacks support’… a priest of the Incarnate Word must “be moved by the impulses of zeal”46. What is proper to us is “to be impatient to preach the Word in every form… We must seek out the sheep, use the method of dialogue, of witness and of solidarity, correct sinners, teach doctrine –faith comes through hearing47–, visit the sick, bring souls to the sacrament of Reconciliation”48. In a word, we must work industriously for the cause of Christ.

It is fitting here to recall that the priestly program, especially in our Institute, which is missionary by nature, “implies availability”49. Far from us be those “priests who, on the slightest excuse, so as not to expose themselves to a bit of work or out of fear of some illness, neglect helping souls. St. Charles Borromeo said that the pastor who wants to adopt every kind of comfort and use whatever he can to favor his bodily health will never be able to fulfill his obligations well. And he added that the pastor should never go to bed except after three attacks of fever”50.

At the same time we must remember the wise maxim of St. Manuel González, which guards us against occasional discouragement, and be convinced “that we can and must work with all our might to convert souls, because that is our part. But we must not be troubled if they are not converted, because that is the part of God and of the souls themselves”51.

▪ Faith

The pastor must bear witness to the faith as a priest, that is, be a channel of grace to communicate divine life through the worthy celebration of the divine mysteries, the administration of the sacraments and, in particular, of the sacrament of reconciliation.

We must be men of faith. Without faith religious and priestly life makes no sense. We must be men of integral faith, so that, on hearing us, all may unmistakably recognize the Word of God. Christ alone is the light of men that shines in the darkness52. God placed this light in the hands of the Church, which with the assistance of the Holy Spirit faithfully preserves and transmits it. Therefore, a clear expression of that faith is sincere and trusting adherence to all the doctrine of the authentic magisterium of the Church, of which our preaching and our catechesis must be an echo. Let everyone always remember: Faith is strengthened by giving it!53.

Finally, our Proper Law, reminding us that pastoral ministry is a Cross, adds the following to these characteristics that must stand out in the priest dedicated to parish ministry: “mercy, prudence, presence of mind, tranquility of soul, the ability to overcome the repugnance that certain illnesses can cause, spiritual fortitude in the face of the dangers that may arise in the fulfillment of his ministry and, finally, availability to all”54.

2. The IVE parish

As we said earlier, the parishes entrusted to the Institute become, so to speak, religious parishes55, since, although the exercise of the apostolate as such is subject to the authority of the bishop, we must exercise our own mode of apostolate, which is in keeping with the character and nature of our religious Institute56. We must bear in mind that the bishop has called us to his diocese precisely to do that: our apostolates, in our own way and style. For this we received the canonical mission on the day of the canonical erection of our Institute. That is, we were sent by the Church to carry out our apostolates in the name of that same Church.

Our Institute has a charism to maintain and promote in the Church, which implies that the religious must remain faithful to their own identity and their own patrimony, at the service of the whole Church, without identifying with, losing themselves in or letting themselves be absorbed by a particular Church; and, on the other hand, they must work within it and in fidelity and obedience to the legitimate pastors57. How sad it is when one visits an IVE parish and hardly notices any difference from the neighboring diocesan parish!

All this means that parishes constitute for the members of the Institute “a special setting for the exercise of some of our specific apostolates,” and, therefore, these must be given preference, since they in fact shape a style of our own. Regarding these apostolates, our Proper Law specifies and emphasizes that ours must be an “enthusiastic ministry”58, not a drowsy one; “incisive”59, not bureaucratic; “one of proposal”60, not of cancelling at the slightest obstacle.

Allow us, then, to refer now to these specific apostolates.

▪ The Eucharistic celebration: The liturgical celebration, particularly the Eucharistic celebration, must be the center of the parish community of the faithful61. That is why it is necessary that priests dedicated to ministry in parishes take as a personal obligation the task of teaching the faithful to receive the Eucharist devoutly, “through serious liturgical formation, so that they may understand the meaning of the symbols they celebrate and take an active part in the celebrations”62.

▪ To this end, our Proper Law invites pastors to “organize teams dedicated to the preparation of the various celebrations: commentators, lectors, those in charge of the collection, organists and those responsible for liturgical music, choirs, those in charge of the sacristy and the church, groups of altar servers”63, etc. And although Sunday will be the central day of the week for the whole parish64, it is also foreseen that there be great and solemn celebrations on occasions such as “Holy Week, novenas and patronal feasts, first communions and confirmations, popular missions”65, Christmas, the Easter Vigil and Pentecost66. It will then be the pastor’s task to promote these celebrations energetically and well in advance if he wants the greatest possible number of people to attend67, besides preparing them well. St. Alberto Hurtado already said it in one of his poems: “improvising on principle” is one of the sins of the man of action68. That is why Sunday Mass must be well prepared, because for many the preservation and nourishment of their faith is tied to participation in that Eucharistic celebration. Moreover, Eucharistic adoration cannot be lacking in an IVE parish.

An important detail that is sometimes forgotten is this exhortation found in our Constitutions: “In a special way, according to the place, let the churches be kept open, and if there are dangers, let some kind of surveillance be prudently arranged; but, except in truly grave and insoluble circumstances, let the People of God be able to approach Jesus present in the Tabernacle with ease”69. How sad it is to see churches open only at Mass time, and one hardly even notices that they are open because the door has merely been unlocked, but the doors remain closed when they should be wide open (so that people know it is open); and, worse still, inside everything is gloomy and dark, because on the excuse that it uses a lot of electricity only a few lights are turned on during the celebration of Mass!

We mention, moreover, that the Instruction Redempitionis Sacramentum specifies that “it is the right of the community of the faithful that, above all in the Sunday celebration, there should be suitable and fitting sacred music, according to custom, and that the altar, the vestments and the sacred linens should always, according to the norms, shine forth in their dignity, nobility and cleanliness”70. This will give the church and its worship an attractiveness that will very soon turn into an increase in the devotion of the priest himself and of his faithful.

St. Manuel González further warns that “lack of punctuality in Holy Mass or in services is often sufficient cause to leave a church deserted… A fixed schedule for parish functions not only attracts the faithful, but is even a good example of order and seriousness given to Christian families”71.

▪ Catechesis, both of children and of adults. It must have priority among parish apostolates –of every parish, not only of those served by us. But it falls to us to carry out this apostolate very conscientiously also according to our Proper Law, which says clearly72 that we want to devote ourselves “in a special way… to the preaching of the Word of God… in all its forms. In the study and teaching of Sacred Scripture… Catechesis…”73, sharing in the prophetic function of Christ74. Therefore, “as a Congregation we must always keep in mind the words of the Holy Father to religious: ‘I exhort you with all my heart, you who by virtue of religious consecration must be all the more available to serve the Church, to prepare yourselves as well as possible for the catechetical task… May communities dedicate the utmost of their abilities and possibilities to the specific work of catechesis!’”75.

▪ The work of the Spiritual Exercises: “The preaching of the Ignatian Spiritual Exercises in our parishes is a characteristic proper to the Institute. The way in which these Exercises strengthen parish life through the strengthening of the spiritual life of the faithful has been proven. All priests assigned to parish ministry must regard the holding of the Exercises as their own responsibility and collaborate in whatever falls to them. It is fitting that in every parish there be a committee to support the organization and coordination of the Exercises”76.

The varied reality of our parishes perhaps makes the preaching of the Spiritual Exercises more difficult in some cases, but that does not mean that our priests are exempt from preaching them, or that our parishioners cannot make them, since with the various adaptations that are stipulated77 they can be put within reach of all, so that “all the faithful may enjoy the benefit that the life of prayer brings”78.

That is why it is to be desired that in all our parishes the pastors not only actively promote the Spiritual Exercises, but strive to send people from their parish to the Exercises organized at the provincial level, and that they themselves preach Exercises in their parishes or invite other priests to do so on those days of the year when it is easiest for the faithful to attend, making them accessible to the greatest possible number of people. There should be no IVE parish in which the catechists, married couples, young people and members of the Third Order do not make the Exercises annually. After the administration of the sacraments, this should be a priority apostolate for the pastor. For, as our Constitutions establish, “the Institute shall undertake the evangelization of culture through the sanctification of individual persons, which will be done preferably by the preaching of Spiritual Exercises according to the method and spirit of St. Ignatius of Loyola, and, in addition, by spiritual direction”79.

▪ Confessions: “The utmost availability of priests for confession must be a hallmark of our parishes, also seeing to it that there are always one or more confessors during Holy Mass”80. Likewise, the Directory of Parishes insists in another paragraph that the “apostolate toward sinners will be exercised in a particular way in the administration of the sacrament of reconciliation, being always available at any moment to hear confessions, striving to possess the requisite knowledge and keeping up to date on moral questions and, above all, after the example of Christ, showing a heart of true mercy”81. It may happen that some of our parishes occasionally have only one priest, which makes the desired availability difficult; nevertheless, our Proper Law makes the following provision to be borne in mind: “Other priests from outside the parish should also be invited to hear confessions, and where possible and fitting, let a permanent confession center be set up”82.

The holy and experienced bishop of Málaga affirmed: “A priest seated in his confessional from before sunrise, resolved not to grow tired or bored with the solitude, will not have to wait long before he sees Samaritan women and Samaritan men arrive to ask him for the water that springs up to eternal life”83. Because “a priest seated in his confessional from very early, even if he has no penitents to confess the whole morning, or until very late, is always a sweet and overpowering violence upon the Heart of Jesus, that He may pour out extraordinary graces”84. Something to bear in mind.

▪ Preaching: “The pastor and the assisting priests will see to it that ‘the Word of God is proclaimed in its entirety to those living in the parish’85, instructing the lay faithful ‘in the truths of the faith, above all through the homily’86”87. Therefore, although canon law establishes “the homily which is to be given on Sundays and holy days of obligation”88, our priests will also strive to give a brief homily every day89, which must be conscientiously prepared and in some cases even rehearsed.

The parish is the center of religious instruction; therefore, the pastor has the grave duty to complete with catechism the religious instruction received in the family, to spread the Word of God through popular missions, through ordinary preaching, through talks given to the various groups of the parish, etc., seeking that its members give authentic witness to Jesus Christ as living and active members of his Mystical Body, teaching them to order their family and working life according to God, and to be integrated with the universal Church through participatory, missionary, solidary and ecumenical attitudes90.

St. Manuel González gives some advice regarding daily preaching that may be useful to us: “by parish preaching is meant simple preaching, without shouting or dramatic theatrical effects, and above all lasting ten minutes. Time and habit take away from the pastor’s preaching the appeal of novelty, and it bores or drives away novelty-seeking faithful, that is true. But it is also true that what it loses in appeal for these, it gains in authority, prestige and appreciation among good parishioners, above all if it is brief and heartfelt preaching. Brevity avoids weariness; heartfeltness produces interest and strengthens bonds. The priest who preaches daily need not worry about the danger of ‘exhausting the subject matter,’ since at most what he will repeat will be ideas, and it is good that these be repeated so that they are not forgotten and are put into practice”91.

At the same time, it is expected that in our parishes “‘initiatives that promote the evangelical spirit, and everything that concerns social justice’ will be fostered”92: with the poor, seeking to alleviate their situation, promoting a ministry of charity93; and with the rich, fostering in them detachment, a sense of justice, and mercy toward the weakest94. On our part, “justice will be exercised in visiting homes, in caring for the sick, in affability toward those who come to the parish, in patience with those who do not understand the things of God. These will be our weapons”95.

In turn, it is recommended that all our parishes, especially those where the faith is more deeply rooted, include “missionary animation as a primary element of ordinary ministry, both in associations and in groups, especially youth groups”96; since “the parish must beget faithful capable of making the seed of the Gospel germinate in the environment in which they live”97. That is why the pastor and the other priests of the community must strive “by every possible means, also with the collaboration of the faithful, so that the Gospel message may reach likewise those who have stopped practicing or do not profess the true faith”98. In this regard the Directory of Parishes reminds us several times of the relevance, excellence and effectiveness of popular missions.

Many other apostolates proper to us could be mentioned here, which for lack of space we will not address. Care of the sick must be a priority, as it is one of the pillars of all parish ministry; but since we are speaking of our parishes, this presentation would be incomplete if we did not mention these last two, albeit briefly:

▪ Oratory: “Let every superior see to it that a festive Oratory is organized in his parish, school, house and other works of his jurisdiction, considering that this work is one of the most important of all those entrusted to him”99. Therefore, the festive Oratory should not be lacking in any IVE parish, however small and poor it may be. Let it be our longing “that many, very many children and young people frequent the Oratory and love it; and even that a work such as this may multiply and prosper where it already exists or arise where it does not yet exist, next to every parish”100.

The Oratory is moreover an incipient source of vocations because, being a school of service, where one learns to work selflessly for the community, for the little ones and the poor, it becomes a privileged path for the birth and growth of authentic vocations to the priesthood and to consecrated and missionary life, as well as of solid lay vocations, married or not, based on the gift of self in the service of others101. “Let it be remembered that vocations will be the sign of a parish’s maturity102, and that vocational work must become a pastoral priority103, knowing how to foster them with generosity and to let go of them when they decide to follow Jesus Christ more closely”104.

▪ Third Order: because “the parish is a privileged place for the insertion and apostolate of the Third Order”105, in all IVE parishes “efforts will be made to foster the establishment and growth of the Secular Third Order of the Religious Family of the Incarnate Word, seeking –as far as possible– its incorporation into or participation in the life of the parish and of the local religious community”106.

Moreover, it is more than desirable that in all our parishes the work the Institute carries out in the world be known; that people know of our devotion to Our Lady of Luján, of our fourth Marian vow, of our love and veneration for St. John Paul II; that they be persuaded by our conduct of the non-negotiables; and that little by little, according to the makeup of the parish, our “sound traditions” become customary among them. How much good it does when, whenever possible, the faithful of our parishes take part in the feasts organized by the Religious Family, or when the parish itself hosts one of these feasts! What a great boost to the faith of young people and children when they take part in the camps, the youth days, the courses for university students organized by the Religious Family! The same may be said of when they attend ordinations and clothing ceremonies. How many blessings it draws down upon our parishioners when they take part in popular missions, in volunteer work in the works of the Institute; when they are benefactors of our missionaries or of our members in houses of formation!

In short, by the grace of God, in our Institute we have opportunities to spare to promote and instill the charism among the faithful of our parishes and to contribute greatly to enriching the local Church. Not a little depends on the love the pastor has for the Institute and on his good will and magnanimity in doing good to others107.

***

We want to end with the ardent watchword that St. Manuel González gave to his priests:

“Most beloved priests, you who sit in the first stalls of the Cathedral as well as you who shepherd the remote village, receive and keep in your hearts this single word that I give you as a watchword for this hour, so full of confusions that inflame and of pessimisms that cow and depress; in this hour, I know not whether of funereal sunsets or of mysterious dawns: Fulfill your ministry. In the name of Jesus Christ who chose and appointed you, of Mother Church who needs you, of society which in the end will miss you, of the paralyzed souls who cry out for the man who will make them walk, of the little ones who ask for bread, of your own interests, spiritual and even earthly… Do your duty! The whole duty, that of justice and that of charity! And afterward… Wait in peace!”108.

“Remain at your posts, even if the solitude and silence of abandonment surround you; let your mouth not cease to open to speak of the Gospel, even if no one hears you, and let your hands not cease to stretch out to offer with one the Eucharist that nourishes souls, and with the other the piece of bread of your poverty that sustains the body, even if they meet no mouths or hands to receive it from you. Let your feet not cease to move in order to go, as the Master commanded, always to go, even if thorns are strewn along your path and not even a pair of friendly eyes meets your gaze… Remain at your posts, come what may; for at the very least you give glory to the heavenly Father who sends you; you lessen and delay, without any doubt, the triumph of evil; and you give an example, the example the world so greatly needs at this moment, that battles are won not by deserting duty but by fulfilling it”109.

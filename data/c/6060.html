Homily for the 20th Anniversary of the Major Seminary “St. Joseph of Anchieta” – Brazil

[Exordium] To celebrate the great apostle of Brazil, St. Joseph of Anchieta, patron of this beloved Major Seminary, is already reason for a great feast. To celebrate today, as well, the twentieth anniversary of its foundation, and to say that this is currently the largest major seminary of the Institute1, makes this feast take on limitless dimensions of happiness and, therefore, of heartfelt gratitude to the Incarnate Word and to his Most Holy Mother, from whom all gifts come to us. Touches of the merciful Providence of our Lord that must not go unnoticed.

Moved by the sublime example of this great missionary, in this sermon, which I wish to dedicate very especially to our major seminarians who will be future missionaries, I would like us to reflect on one aspect of the life of “that humble man named Joseph Anchieta,” as a poet called him2, and who was one of the greatest missionaries ever to set foot in America.

Life as a student

Joseph of Anchieta was born, as you know, on the feast of St. Joseph in the year 1534, the same year in which Ignatius of Loyola was gathering his first companions in Paris. At the age of 14 he was sent to study Philosophy at the University of Coimbra, Portugal. Having finished those three years of Philosophy, he entered the Ignatian order on May 1, 1551 (a day on which St. Joseph is also celebrated today). And as soon as he had finished his novitiate, at the age of 19, he was sent to the Mission of Brazil with the third expedition of Jesuits, which set out from Lisbon precisely on a May 8, 1553 (the day of Our Lady of Luján). He was at that time the youngest missionary in the New World.

The great miracle of the Apostle of Brazil was his life. It was humanly impossible for him to survive no less than 44 years with his double tuberculosis3, which he contracted while in the novitiate; but he, without “shying away from the missionary adventure”4, despite his illness and the absolute lack of resources, went to a mission that was just beginning and there he displayed a most fruitful apostolic activity. To the point that today he is venerated not only as the apostle of Brazil, but as the founder of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro; author of the First Tupi-Guarani Grammar; creator of Brazilian literature and theater; first historian of the minerals, of the flora and fauna, and of the ecology and anthropology of Brazil.

But let us begin at the beginning: on arriving in Brazil in 1553, this young religious, without experience, a stranger to the customs of the place, and sick, was sent to the south of Brazil, to São Vicente, where the Jesuits had a school and where some vocations had arisen. The only one who could teach Latin was Anchieta, so there he went. And, sick as he was, he gave three different classes daily from morning until night, having to write out by hand the texts for his students and preparing his classes during the nights. That was his life as a student, his life as a seminarian.

In a letter, which perhaps you know, addressed to Fr. Diego Laínez, Superior General of the Society of Jesus, dated June 1, 1560, Joseph of Anchieta wrote word for word: “For this reason, without letting ourselves be intimidated by the great heat, the storms, the rains, the torrential and impetuous currents of the rivers, we strive without rest to visit all the villages and towns, both of the Indians and of the Portuguese, and even at night we go to the sick, crossing dark forests at the cost of great fatigue, both because of the roughness of the paths and because of the bad weather.” And describing even more openly the conditions of those who, with him and like him, devoted themselves to the sublime work of evangelization, he reveals still more deeply the greatness of his love and of his spirit of sacrifice when he says: “But nothing is difficult for those who cherish in their heart, and have as their only end, the glory of God and the salvation of souls, for whom they do not hesitate to give their life.”

Hence St. John Paul II, in the homily of a Mass celebrated here in São Paulo in the year 1980, affirmed that Joseph of Anchieta did not come here as a conqueror in search of treasures and glory, but “came as a missionary, to proclaim Jesus Christ, to spread the Gospel. He came with the sole aim of leading men to Christ, transmitting to them the life of children of God, destined for eternal life. He came without demanding anything for himself; on the contrary, ready to give his life for them”5.

Perhaps we could point out here that Joseph of Anchieta was very young, that he had a high level of education for the time, that he certainly had European customs and that, for that reason, practically everything, from the food to the smells, the local language and the extreme poverty, was a cause of self-denial for him. Not to mention the six or seven months without confession or Mass, or having to meditate not in a comfortable little corner –as he says– but in the midst of the wickedness of the Indians6. All this already speaks of an “admirable”7 seminarian whom God wanted to make very holy. However, there is something else I would like to emphasize, and it is the great forgetfulness of self, and the love for souls and for his Congregation, that he showed from the very beginning. For there is a letter he writes to the sick in Coimbra, with whom he had even shared the infirmary, in which he tells them that he was basically cured by teaching classes. He tells them that even though he taught practically all day and therefore had almost no time to rest like any sick person, even though the food there was very poor –in fact, he recounts that he ate “boiled mustard leaves and other vegetables of the land and other delicacies that you over there cannot imagine”8–, and even though he had fasted the whole of Lent, he was practically cured in the mission. And in that letter he reveals an important detail; the saint says: “as soon as I acted as if I were not sick, I soon began to be healthy.” There you have the forgetfulness of self that God blessed so superabundantly.

That is why he goes on to tell his companions back in Coimbra: “if the Father Master wished to send here all of you who are poorly and half sick, half well, the land is very good, the air very healthy, the medicines are labors, and all the better the more they conform us to Christ.” And he goes on to say, pay attention: “I also tell you, my dearest ones, that it is not enough to leave Coimbra with just any sort of fervor, which withers right away before crossing the line [of the Equator], or cools afterward with desires to return to Portugal. It is necessary, brothers, to bring your saddlebags full, so that they last until the end of the journey, because without a doubt the labors that the Society has here are great, and virtue is needed in each one, so that the honor of the Society may be entrusted to him”9.

That is to say, it is not enough to jump about singing “Verbo Encarnado,” or to say in words that one loves the Institute; it is not enough to have read the Constitutions at some point; it is not enough just to say “we are essentially missionary and Marian”10, nor even to be a good student in the seminary, to make of us a thorough missionary of the Incarnate Word. All of that, even though good, if it is not with conviction, if it does not have deep roots in the soul, belongs to those kinds of fervor the saint speaks of, which wither right away and grow cold and make one want to turn back. Because, as St. Ignatius taught very well –and we meditate on this every year when we make the exercises– “love ought to be put more in deeds than in words”11. Do you understand me?

St. Joseph of Anchieta had understood this well; that is why he tells his companions that one must have one’s saddlebags full of fervor, of the fervor that withstands the great waves of trial that will undoubtedly come upon the missionary; saddlebags full of “apostolic charity”12 –as our Proper Law asks of us– that make the missionary forget himself in order to work “even in the most difficult places and in the most adverse conditions”13, and this not in just any way, but with great zeal for the good of souls and the expansion of the Church of Christ; full of the fervor that makes the missionary –even when he no longer has the spiritual consolations he once had “and which one day God withdraws completely”14, and stripped also of “the supports and assurances regarding the state of his soul”15– nevertheless trust “with unshakable firmness that even the most adverse events, and those most opposed to his natural view, are ordered by God for his good”16.

Conditions for the mission

For this reason the Directory of Missions Ad Gentes establishes as a most important condition for the mission that capacity to deny oneself, to renounce that selfish attitude that makes us seek our own interests, our own comfort, in order to work with zeal in the place and in the office that the Institute entrusts to us for the greater glory of God. The directory says: “The missionary is asked ‘to renounce himself and everything he had until then and to become all things to all’: in the poverty that leaves him free for the Gospel; in detachment from the persons and goods of his own milieu, so as to become thus a brother to those to whom he is sent and to bring them to Christ the Savior”17. Notice what it says: to renounce himself and everything he had until then… detachment from the persons and goods of his own milieu… this is part of the program of all who wish to be missionaries in the Institute, and they must begin to exercise themselves in it from now on.

That is why St. Joseph of Anchieta adds in his letter: “I say no more to you, except that you prepare yourselves with great interior fortitude and great desires to suffer, so that, even though the labors be many, they may seem few to you”18.

To which I add here other virtues that our Proper Law mentions for us: “Those who in our houses of formation are preparing to carry out great works for the glory of God in the missions to which they will be sent [notice that it is speaking directly to you], so as not to deceive themselves with vain fantasies, should cultivate a great love for the virtues of humility, charity and docility to superiors.” Then our Proper Law continues: “‘It is not enough that nothing negative be found in our candidates; it is not enough that they be sufficiently diligent in study and exterior discipline. One must study their character, measure the fervor of their spirit, the absolute submission of the will, generosity in sacrifice, the spirit of initiative, fidelity to duty’19. Those types of personality that ‘know everything,’ ‘get along badly with everyone,’ find fault with everything, argue about everything, or neither listen to nor obey anyone except when others agree with what they think, will hardly be suited for the mission. The reason why such characters will not be able to bear fruit is that God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble (1 Pet 5:5). And if God resists a missionary, what will he be able to do?”20. All this makes “evident that a great part of the formative effectiveness [for our seminarians] depends on the mature and strong personality of the formators from the human and evangelical point of view”21. Thus far our Proper Law.

Joseph of Anchieta was in the Captaincy of São Vicente, today the state of São Paulo, for 12 years, from where he left for the north, to Bahia, to be ordained a priest the following year, in 1566. His companions were ordained at 23 or 24 years of age. He, at 32. Did this delay trouble him? Not for a moment. Another trial that he knew how to bear with faith. He would be a priest when God so disposed. His part was to wait.

Later on, when he was Provincial, he was conversing in Rio de Janeiro with other Fathers and Brothers on the subject of the conformity that every Jesuit must have with his office in order to preserve interior peace. Joseph confessed with the greatest simplicity: “When I was a brother, it never entered my imagination that I could be a priest. And when I least expected it, I found myself with orders. When I was a priest, it never crossed my mind that I could be professed or a superior… here you see me as Provincial”22. He was provincial superior for ten years.

[Peroratio] Dear seminarians: Our mission is marvelous, sublime I would say, and at the same time challenging. Consider yourselves, then, the favorites of the Incarnate Word, whom He himself has called to be his missionaries. Having embraced this vocation, know that you have at the same time taken on the mission; therefore you must feel responsible for your personal preparation. The superiors and the formators are here to help, certainly, but continual growth in the spiritual, intellectual, human and pastoral dimensions depends on you, on the awareness of your duty23 as religious of the Incarnate Word. The Holy Patron of this beloved seminary gave us a magnificent example of this very thing throughout his thirteen years of preparation for the priesthood.

Christ needs the contribution of all in order to bring to other hearts the word that not all can understand24, the word that the Word became flesh. Are you ready?… then put that love into practice and strive to acquire that interior fortitude and that longing to suffer for Christ which made Joseph of Anchieta an “authentic missionary, a true priest”25. That is my message.

And now, to conclude, I would like to renew my gratitude, my trust and my affection to all of you, to all the priests who in one way or another are involved in your formation, and I encourage you to keep going, always forward!, giving the best of yourselves for the glory of God and the expansion of the Institute.

To the Most Holy Virgin of Aparecida I entrust the intentions of all of you, and I ask Her to take under her maternal protection the present and future vocations of this seminary and to make them “tireless evangelizers”26. May the Mother of the Incarnate Word and of the Church always accompany your steps, and may the Holy Spirit continue to raise up numerous Brazilian vocations.

A very happy anniversary!

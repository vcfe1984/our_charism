1. The mystery of the Church is situated within the mystery of the Wisdom and Goodness of God, who draws the whole human family, and even the whole of creation, into unity with himself1.
  2. The religious reality of the world presents us with the Catholic Church and, alongside her, a constellation of churches and confessions that have broken away from her over the centuries; and, beyond these, the immense field of those who do not accept Christ and of those who do not believe in God. Toward all of these the Church directs her maternal solicitude and her responsibility before God.
  3. Two-thirds of humanity are not Christian; the remaining third is made up of 52% Catholics, 18% Orthodox and 30% Protestants. The saving mission directed to non-Christians (the “gentiles”) is properly called missio ad gentes; the saving concern for her own children is designated as “pastoral work”; finally, the mission toward non-Catholic Christians is defined by the term “ecumenism.”
  4. Our Institute is fully confronted with the problem of the de-Christianization of the world and of the fragmentation of Christian reality itself. Our firm decision to go wherever we may be called, that is, into all the world (Mk 16:15), so as not to shy away from missionary adventure, brings us into contact with the reality of an astonishing religious symbiosis and of a growing confusion in the faith. Our work in territories of long Protestant tradition or of Eastern Orthodox traditions, the growing reality of mixed marriages in many countries, and our very own preferential apostolate in the world of culture, of the university and of schools in general, where men and women of diverse and even antagonistic confessions meet, demand of us extremely clear guidelines and convictions, not only regarding the carrying out of the mission ad gentes but also with respect to non-Catholic Christians. To serve the Church is to serve unity: “To serve the Church is to serve Christ in his plan to gather into one all the children of God who are scattered (Jn 11:52), and to renew all things and finally sum them up in him, in order to subject everything to his Father, so that in the Spirit we may all be eternally the praise of his glory. This service is great! It is worthy of all our energies. In truth it surpasses our own strength. It obliges us to pray continually”2.
  5. 5. St. John Paul II teaches in the Encyclical Letter Ut unum sint: “United in following the martyrs, believers in Christ cannot remain divided. If they wish truly and effectively to oppose the world's tendency to reduce to powerlessness the mystery of Redemption, they must profess together the same truth about the Cross. The Cross! The anti-Christian current seeks to nullify its value, to empty it of its meaning, denying that man finds in it the roots of his new life, and claiming that the Cross can open neither perspectives nor hopes: man, it is said, is only an earthly being who must live as if God did not exist. No one can fail to see the challenge that all this represents for believers. They must accept it. Indeed, how could they refuse to do everything possible, with God's help, to break down the walls of division and distrust, to overcome the obstacles and prejudices that hinder the proclamation of the Gospel of salvation through the Cross of Jesus, the one Redeemer of man, of every man?”3.
  6. That is why the Holy Father John Paul II also said: “The work of Christian unity is, I believe, one of the greatest and most beautiful tasks of the Church in our time”4. And likewise, during the Fifth Extraordinary Consistory: “Ecumenical dialogue, with all the activity that springs from it on behalf of Christian unity, is one of the fundamental tasks of the Church with a view to the year 2000. Despite the opinions of those who speak of a stalemate in this field, the ecumenical effort retains its dynamism intact”5.

Hence the importance of the present Directory.

  1. And hence also our Institute's desire to take part in this most beautiful task. Providence has willed that St. John Paul II, whom we regard as the “father” of our Congregation, should have developed this theme in an outstanding way during his Magisterium. We regard his Encyclical Letter Ut unum sint as the magna carta of Catholic ecumenism, and the Declaration Dominus Iesus (approved by St. John Paul II) as an indispensable document for a correct understanding of this subject.

1. The Catholic notion of ecumenism

A) The Catholic Church and the Church of Christ

8. We said that the mystery of the Church is situated within the divine plan of salvation for all men. To carry out this plan, God sent into the world his only Son, who, lifted up on the Cross, poured out the Holy Spirit, through whom he called and gathered into the unity of faith, hope and charity the People of the New Covenant, which is the Church. In order to establish this holy Church everywhere until the end of the ages, Christ entrusted the office of teaching, governing and sanctifying to the College of the Twelve, over which he placed Peter as head. Through the faithful preaching of the Gospel, through the administration of the sacraments, and through governance in love, exercised by the Apostles and their successors under the action of the Holy Spirit, Jesus Christ wills that this people should grow and that its communion should become ever more perfect6.

9. The Church founded by Jesus Christ, the Council says, subsists in the Catholic Church7. That is, she is, without any break in continuity, the community of believers founded by Jesus Christ upon Peter and confirmed at Pentecost. Thus the Catholic Church believes and professes that she is the full Church of Jesus Christ, lacking no substantial element bestowed by her Founder.

10. “With the expression subsistit in, the Second Vatican Council sought to harmonize two doctrinal statements: on the one hand, that the Church of Christ, despite the divisions which exist among Christians, continues to exist fully only in the Catholic Church, and on the other hand, that ‘outside of her structure, many elements can be found of sanctification and truth,’ that is, in those Churches and ecclesial communities which are not yet in full communion with the Catholic Church. But with respect to these, it needs to be stated that ‘they derive their efficacy from the very fullness of grace and truth entrusted to the Catholic Church’”8.

11. Therefore: “Catholics hold the firm conviction that the one Church of Christ subsists in the Catholic Church, ‘which is governed by the successor of Peter and by the Bishops in communion with him.’ They confess that the entirety of revealed truth, of sacraments and of ministry that Christ gave for the building up of his Church and the carrying out of its mission is found within the Catholic communion of the Church. Certainly Catholics know that personally they have not made full use of and do not make full use of the means of grace with which the Church is endowed. For all that, Catholics never lose confidence in the Church. Their faith assures them that she remains ‘the worthy bride of the Lord, ceaselessly renewing herself through the action of the Holy Spirit until, through the Cross, she arrives at the light which knows no setting.’ Therefore, when Catholics use the words ‘Churches,’ ‘other Churches,’ ‘other Churches and ecclesial Communities,’ etc., to refer to those who are not in full communion with the Catholic Church, this firm conviction and confession of faith must always be kept in mind”9.

12. “In the proper sense, sister Churches are exclusively particular Churches (or groupings of particular Churches: for example, the Patriarchates and Metropolitan provinces). It must always be clear, even when the expression sister Churches is used in this proper sense, that the one, holy, catholic and apostolic universal Church is not sister but mother of all the particular Churches”10.

“One may also speak of sister Churches, in a proper sense, in reference to particular Catholic and non-Catholic Churches; thus the particular Church of Rome can also be called the sister of all other particular Churches. However, as recalled above, one cannot properly say that the Catholic Church is the sister of a particular Church or group of Churches. This is not merely a question of terminology, but above all of respecting a basic truth of the Catholic faith: that of the uniqueness of the Church of Jesus Christ. In fact, there is but a single Church, and therefore the plural term Churches can refer only to particular Churches”11.

  1. Extra Ecclesiam nulla salus. Moreover, it is a firm conviction of the Catholic faith that “it is only through Christ's Catholic Church, which is the all-embracing means of salvation, that the fullness of the means of salvation can be obtained. We believe that our Lord entrusted all the blessings of the New Covenant to the one apostolic college presided over by Peter, in order to establish on earth the one body of Christ into which all those should be fully incorporated who already belong in any way to the People of God”12. This is the doctrine of the traditional principle extra Ecclesiam nulla salus; outside the Church there is no salvation: only in her are all the means of salvation found in their fullness, and it is through her and in relation to her that the Holy Spirit stirs up saving grace in the hearts of those who are not visibly united to her in a perfect way.
  2. The Catechism of the Catholic Church explains the sense in which this principle must be understood when it says: “How are we to understand this affirmation, often repeated by the Church Fathers? Reformulated positively, it means that all salvation comes from Christ the Head through the Church which is his Body: ‘Basing itself on Scripture and Tradition, the Council teaches that the Church, a pilgrim now on earth, is necessary for salvation: the one Christ is the mediator and the way of salvation; he is present to us in his body which is the Church. He himself explicitly asserted the necessity of faith and Baptism, and thereby affirmed at the same time the necessity of the Church which men enter through Baptism as through a door. Hence they could not be saved who, knowing that the Catholic Church was founded as necessary by God through Christ, would refuse either to enter it or to remain in it’13. This affirmation is not aimed at those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ and his Church: ‘Those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel of Christ or his Church, but who nevertheless seek God with a sincere heart, and, moved by grace, try in their actions to do his will as they know it through the dictates of their conscience—those too may achieve eternal salvation’14. ‘Although in ways known to himself God can lead those who, through no fault of their own, are ignorant of the Gospel, to that faith without which it is impossible to please him (Heb 11:6), the Church still has the obligation and also the sacred right to evangelize all men’”15.

b) The unity of the Church
in the will of Christ

15. The Church is, in Christ, the sacrament and instrument of intimate union with God and of the unity of the whole human race16. She is the People of God which unites in itself, through the work of the Holy Spirit and the mystery of Christ's Death and Resurrection, men and women of all nations and cultures, endowed with the various gifts of nature and grace, for the perfecting of the saints for the work of ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ, until we all come together into the unity of the faith and of the full knowledge of the Son of God, to the maturity of the perfect man, to an organic growth proportioned to the fullness of Christ (Eph 4:12-13). The unity of the People of God is, therefore, an essential constituent of the Church. She realizes this koinonia/communion through the threefold bond of faith, sacramental life and hierarchical ministry. That is why one of her essential characteristic marks is unity17.

16. For the same reason, “the lack of unity among Christians is certainly a wound for the Church; not in the sense that she is deprived of her unity, but ‘in that it hinders the complete fulfillment of her universality in history’”18.

17. St. John Paul II said: “Ut omnes unum sint. Unity, the resplendent mark of the true Church, is the summit of Christ's priestly prayer at the Last Supper; it is his last testament of love, the charge he left us before his Passion: antequam pateretur… We cannot escape the examination of conscience to which this word subjects us. It is the touchstone of the credibility of Christian discipleship in the world: ut credat mundus quia tu me misisti (Jn 17:21). If we are not one, as the Father is one in Christ and Christ is one with the Father, the world will not believe: the concrete proof of the mystery of Redemption escapes it, the mystery by which the Lord has made of scattered humanity a single family, a single organism, a single body, a single heart”19.

18. And also: “The unity of the Church belongs unquestionably to her essence. It is not an end in itself. The Lord gives it so that the world may believe (Jn 17:21). Let us spare no means to bear witness together to what has been given to us in Christ. He is the one mediator between God and men (1 Tim 2:5). There is salvation in no one else (Acts 4:12)”20.

19. Not only unity but universal unity: “The communion in which Christians believe and for which they hope is, in its deepest reality, their oneness with the Father through Christ in the Holy Spirit. Since Pentecost, it has been given and received in the Church, the communion of saints. It is accomplished fully in the glory of heaven, but is already realized in the Church on earth as it journeys toward that fullness. Those who live united in faith, hope and love, in mutual service, in common teaching and sacraments, under the guidance of their pastors, are part of that communion which constitutes the Church of God. This communion is realized concretely in the particular Churches, each of which is gathered together around its Bishop. In each of these ‘the one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church of Christ is truly present and active’21. This communion is, therefore, universal by its very nature”22.

20. Unity is the express will of Christ: That they may be one (Jn 17:21), and it is also the express will of Christ that this unity be a sign of credibility for the world: That they also may be one in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me (Jn 17:21). Hence “the will of Christ, the witness we must bear to Christ—this is the motive that impels each and every one of us not to grow weary or discouraged in this undertaking”23.

21. It is thus understood that every division “openly contradicts the will of Christ, provides a stumbling block to the world, and inflicts damage on the most holy cause of proclaiming the Gospel to every creature”24, because when those who call upon Christ present themselves divided among themselves, it is “as if Christ himself were divided”25: Is Christ divided? (1 Cor 1:13).

22. St. John Paul II repeated insistently that the drama of the division of Christians is a cause of scandal:

– “It is incredible that the drama of division among Christians should still exist, which is for all a cause of perplexity and perhaps also of scandal”26.

– “Have I not already said that the divisions among Christians are intolerable?”27.

– “Without this full organic unity Christians are unable to bear adequate witness to Christ, and their division remains a scandal to the world, most especially in the young churches of mission lands”28.

– “Unity is a characteristic and a requirement of the Catholic Church. Dissent, divergence and division are contrary to God's plan”29.

c) The end of ecumenism: the full
visible unity of Christians

23. Ecumenism starts from an inescapable fact: “no Christian, man or woman, can be satisfied with these imperfect forms of communion. They do not correspond to the will of Christ, and they weaken his Church in the exercise of her mission”30.

24. The ultimate end of the ecumenical movement is to achieve the full visible communion of all Christians: “Its specific mission is the restoration of unity among Christians”31.

25. Ecumenism is the search for unity. However, it must be kept in mind that when the Catholic Church aspires to unity she does not do so on the assumption that such unity does not already exist. It exists, and it exists in her, as unity of faith, of worship (sacramental) and of government. What is more, it is the unity that Christ gave her when he founded her, and which has never ceased to exist throughout all the historical vicissitudes through which she has passed. This is how the Council's expression “subsists in her” must be understood32. This unity, which is an essential and distinctive mark, rests visibly, by the express will of Christ, upon the Apostle Peter and his successors.

26. This unity is unity in faith: “The decree [Unitatis redintegratio] defines this unity as consisting ‘in the profession of one faith’”33. St. John Paul II already said at the beginning of his pontificate that the end of ecumenism is “to overcome the obstacles that separate us from the unanimous profession of the same faith”34. And some time later: “The integrity of the apostolic faith, as it has been handed on to the saints once and for all in the apostolic Tradition (cf. Jude 3), must be fully preserved if we want our unity to be the unity for which Christ prayed… As in the time of St. Paul, so now too all our efforts to restore unity among Christians will be in vain if they are not carried out in complete fidelity to the faith in Christ handed down by the Apostles”35.

27. It is unity in worship and in the sacraments: “… in the common celebration of divine worship”36. St. John Paul II expressed it thus: “This suffering must spur us on to overcome the obstacles that still separate us from the profession of the same faith and from the reunification of our separated communities in one and the same sacramental ministry”37.

28. It is unity in concord: “… in the fraternal harmony of the family of God”38.

29. It is unity in an external bond, which can be none other than Peter: “… this unity, which of its very nature requires full visible communion of all Christians, is the ultimate goal of the ecumenical movement”39. In some way St. John Paul II says this when he affirms: “In sending St. Augustine to preach the Gospel to the Anglo-Saxon people, St. Gregory was fulfilling the pastoral and missionary responsibility proper to the ministry of the Bishop of Rome. In his writings we discover a rich and profound appreciation of the universal primacy entrusted to the Bishop who occupies the See of Peter. It was he who called the Bishop of Rome caput fidei and who described the one who exercises this ministry as the servus servorum Dei40… Today the divisions among Christians demand that the primacy of the Bishop of Rome be also a primacy in action and initiative on behalf of the unity for which Christ prayed so fervently”41.

30. This founding will of Christ regarding Peter has its revealed basis in the text of Mt 16:18-19: I tell you that you are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the Kingdom of Heaven, and whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in Heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in Heaven. Ecumenical unity can never dispense with this fundamental text and its correct interpretation.

31. Peter is the source of undividedness: “In order that the Episcopate itself might be one and undivided, he placed blessed Peter over the other Apostles, and instituted in him a permanent and visible principle and foundation of unity of faith and communion”42.

32. “The Catholic Church, both in her praxis and in her official documents, holds that the communion of the particular Churches with the Church of Rome, and of their Bishops with the Bishop of Rome, is—in God's plan—an essential requisite of full and visible communion. Indeed full communion, of which the Eucharist is the highest sacramental manifestation, needs to be visibly expressed in a ministry in which all the Bishops recognize that they are united in Christ and all the faithful find confirmation for their faith”43.

“Is it not precisely a ministry of this kind that many of those involved in ecumenism today feel a need for? To preside in truth and love so that the boat—that beautiful symbol which the World Council of Churches has chosen as its emblem—will not be buffeted by the storms and will one day reach its haven”44.

  1. Therefore, “unity” means “unity under Peter in the Catholic Church,” since Christ, as the Council says, “… chose Peter and… entrusted all his sheep to him to be confirmed in faith and shepherded in perfect unity”45.

d) To whom ecumenism is directed

34. “The fullness of the unity of the Church of Christ has been maintained within the Catholic Church, while other Churches and ecclesial Communities, though not in full communion with the Catholic Church, in reality retain a certain communion with it”46.

35. Ecumenical work is concerned with those non-Catholic Christians who maintain a singular relationship of belonging with respect to the Catholic Church.

36. Strictly speaking, all men who are still wayfarers can in some way be called members of the Church. We can thus distinguish different degrees of belonging:

37. a) Full members. These are those who are fully incorporated into the Catholic Church: “Fully in communion with the Catholic Church here on earth are those baptized who are joined with Christ in its visible structure, that is, by the bonds of the profession of faith, of the sacraments, and of ecclesiastical governance”47. This is judged on the basis of two elements that determine belonging to the Church. One is the apostolic faith, dogmatic and sacramental; this also includes the hierarchical bond with the successors of the Apostles, since it is their task to watch over and guarantee this faith: he appointed twelve to be with him and to send them out to preach (Mk 3:14); and also: Go into the whole world and preach the Gospel… whoever believes… will be saved (Mk 16:15-16); and among these, Peter and his successor: Simon, I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail; and you, when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers (Lk 22:32). The second element consists in the supernatural life that the Holy Spirit produces by dwelling in the hearts of his faithful. These two elements properly constitute the man of the New Law, since this Law consists in “the grace of the Holy Spirit which is given to us through faith in Christ”48. Among these members we must distinguish those who possess both elements and those who lack the second:

38. – The just: “They are fully incorporated in the society of the Church who, possessing the Spirit of Christ, accept her entire system and all the means of salvation given to her, and are united with her as part of her visible bodily structure and through her with Christ, who rules her through the Supreme Pontiff and the bishops, by the bonds of the profession of faith, the sacraments, ecclesiastical government and communion”49. Pius XII wrote: “only those are really to be included as members of the Church who have been baptized and profess the true faith, and who have not been so unfortunate as to separate themselves from the unity of the Body, or been excluded by legitimate authority for grave faults committed”50.

39. – Catholic sinners who have not defected from the faith: “He is not saved, however, though he is part of the body of the Church, who does not persevere in charity. He remains indeed in the bosom of the Church, but, as it were, only in a ‘bodily’ manner and not ‘in his heart’”51.

40. b) Members not in full communion. These are all those who lack the fullness of the apostolic faith, either because they do not hold it in its entirety in its dogmatic and sacramental aspect, or because they lack unity of governance, that is, hierarchical communion with Peter, who is the guarantor of the faith: “The Church knows herself to be joined by many bonds to all those who are honored with the name of Christians, since they are baptized, even though they do not profess the faith in its entirety, or do not preserve unity of communion under the successor of Peter. Many of them, indeed, hold Sacred Scripture as the rule of faith and of life, and show religious zeal; they believe in the love of God the almighty Father and in Christ, Son of God and Savior; they are marked by baptism, through which they are joined to Christ; and they even recognize and receive other sacraments within their own Churches or ecclesial Communities. Many of them also have the episcopate, celebrate the Holy Eucharist and foster devotion to the Virgin Mother of God. To this is further added a communion of prayers and of other spiritual benefits; indeed, a certain true union in the Holy Spirit, since He also acts in them with His sanctifying power through gifts and graces, and to some of them He has even given the strength of martyrdom. In this way the Spirit stirs up in all the disciples of Christ the desire and the effort that all may be peacefully united in one flock under one Shepherd, as Christ ordained. To obtain this, Mother Church never ceases to pray, to hope and to work, and she urges all her children to sanctify and renew themselves so that the image of Christ may shine more clearly on the face of the Church”52.

41. Clearly, the degree of greater or lesser imperfection will depend on the fullness of the faith professed and on the understanding of the Primacy of Peter in each particular Church. The situation of Christians who are merely schismatic is not the same as that of those with whom the Church differs on serious and profound dogmatic questions. That is why Pope John Paul II speaks of the Eastern Christians as those with whom we have “a communion that is almost full, though still imperfect”53.

42. Once the doctrine of the absolute identity of the Church founded by Jesus Christ with the Catholic Church has been made clear, one may accept the expression that points to the existence, in the other Christian Churches and ecclesial Communities, of vestigia Ecclesiae, vestiges of the Church; understood in the right sense, this means that the Catholic Church recognizes in other separated Churches and ecclesial communities elements of truth and of life which she herself possesses in fullness54.

43. c) Members in potency. “Those who have not yet received the Gospel are ordered in various ways to the People of God. In the first place, that people to whom the covenants and the promises were entrusted, and from whom Christ was born according to the flesh, a people most beloved by reason of election on account of their fathers, for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable. But the plan of salvation also embraces those who acknowledge the Creator, and among these the Muslims hold first place; professing explicitly the faith of Abraham, they worship with us the one, merciful God, who will judge mankind on the last day. Nor is this same God far from others who, amid shadows and images, seek the unknown God, since He gives to all life and breath and all things, and as Savior wills all men to be saved. Indeed, those who through no fault of their own do not know the Gospel of Christ and His Church, yet sincerely seek God and, moved by grace, strive to carry out in their works His will as known through the dictate of conscience, can attain eternal salvation”55. These, “by a certain unconscious desire and longing, are ordered to the mystical Body of the Redeemer”56.

44. Ecumenical work is directed toward those whom we have called members not in full communion, while potential members are the object of the missio ad gentes.

45. Ecumenism, therefore, starts from and is grounded on a certain communion, though imperfect, of faith in “the Triune God and Jesus Christ as Lord and Savior”57.

e) Ecumenism as grace

46. Bearing in mind that the unity of Christians is the will of Christ, and that the pursuit of this unity is the end of ecumenism, it is evident that true and authentic ecumenism is not a purely human project, nor can it be adequately explained by the noblest aspirations of men of good will. On the contrary, ecumenism presupposes a divine grace, a supernatural motion, which moves the children of God to travel the hard road toward the rebuilding of unity. “The ecumenical movement seeks to be a response to the gift of God’s grace, which calls all Christians to faith in the mystery of the Church, according to the plan of God who wishes to lead humanity to salvation and to unity in Christ through the Holy Spirit”58.

47. “But remember: the unity that Christ wills for His Church is His own gift”59. “Unity, in the end, is a gift of God, a gift that we must ask for and prepare ourselves to receive, so that it may be granted to us. Unity, like every gift, like every grace, depends on God who shows mercy (Rom 9:16). Since the reconciliation of all Christians ‘surpasses human strength and ability’60, unceasing and fervent prayer expresses our hope, which does not disappoint, and our trust in the Lord who will make all things new (cf. Rom 5:5; Rev 21:5)”61.

48. “Unity can only be bestowed on us as a gift from the Lord, as the fruit of His Passion and Resurrection in the appointed ‘fullness of time’”62.

49. Ecumenical work demands uncommon charity, patience, diligence proof against all discouragement, absolute fidelity to the truth, delicacy and tact, docility to the inspirations of the Holy Spirit and constant asceticism. And this can only be the fruit of love for the cross and of the gifts of the Holy Spirit: “The ecumenical movement is a grace of God, granted by the Father in answer to the prayer of Jesus63 and to the supplications of the Church inspired by the Holy Spirit”64.

f) Cause of ecumenical unity:
the Holy Spirit

50. The Church, in her ecumenical hope, is aware that without the assistance of the Holy Spirit, the source of her unity, she can do nothing.

51. “The Holy Spirit, who dwells in believers and fills and governs the whole Church, brings about this wonderful communion and joins them all so intimately in Christ that He is the Principle of the Church’s unity”65. Ecumenical unity “is the very unity for which Christ prayed and which the Holy Spirit brings about”66. For this reason, the unity that is the fruit of ecumenism is asked for by the Church in the form of supplication:

52. – Pius XI: “And may our blessing, become a prayer, reach the throne of God and repeat to Him the petition which, in these very days, the divine Spirit placed on the lips and in the heart of His Church: that You deign to call all who have strayed back to the unity of the Church…, we beseech You, hear us”67.

53. – Pius XII: “We desire and wish that all who carry in their hearts the earnest call to embrace Christian unity—and no one who belongs to Christ should think lightly of so great a matter—offer supplications and prayers to God, from whom, as from their Author, order, unity and beauty arise, so that the praiseworthy desires of all good people may soon be brought to fulfillment”68.

54. – Second Vatican Council: “This holy desire to reconcile all Christians in the unity of the one and only Church of Jesus Christ exceeds human strength and ability. Therefore it places all its hope in the prayer of Christ for the Church, in the love of the Father for us, in the power of the Holy Spirit. And hope will not be disappointed, because the love of God has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us (Rom 5:5)”69.

55. – St. John XXIII: “That they may be one! This is the outline drawn by the divine Redeemer which we must carry out, venerable brothers, and it is a weighty task entrusted to the conscience of each one… This heartbeat of the Heart of Christ must invite us to a renewed resolve of self-giving, so that among Catholics love for and witness to the first mark of the Church (one) may remain most firm, and so that across the vast horizon of the Christian denominations, and beyond, that unity may be achieved toward which the longing of upright and generous hearts rises”70.

56. – St. Paul VI: “May the most kind Redeemer, who, when about to go to His death, prayed to the Father that all who would believe in Him might be one, as He and the Father are one (cf. Jn 17:20-21), deign to hear, and very soon, this most ardent desire of ours and of the universal Church: that we may all celebrate the Eucharistic mystery with one voice and one faith and, made sharers in the body of Christ, become one single Body, bound together by the same ligaments with which He willed it to be formed! (cf. 1 Cor 10:17)”71.

57. If peace in the world and the restoration of all things in Christ can only be the work of God (St. Pius X calls it opus Dei72), with equal or greater reason will the work of the unity of the Church be so, which Pius XI calls opus in primis Dei: “This achievement of union will not come about by human judgment, but by the goodness of God alone, who shows no partiality (Acts 10:34)… But since this gathering of all peoples into ecumenical unity, being chiefly the work of God, must be attained with divine help and aid, let us persevere in devout prayer, following the example and teaching of St. Josaphat, who labored for unity above all through the power of prayer”73.

58. None of the Pontiffs cited fails to see the miraculous dimension of this unity. Indeed, it is precisely its miraculous nature that gives it the character of a sign of credibility leading to the recognition of Jesus Christ: That all may be one, as You, Father, are in me and I in You, that they also may be one in us, so that the world may believe that You sent me (Jn 17:21). Thus, for example:

59. – Leo XIII, in his prayer for the conversion of Jews and Turks (1899): “O sweet Heart of Mary! Tell Jesus what we neither know how nor are able to tell Him, and He will listen to You…, and if, to overcome the resistance of those for whom we pray to You, a miracle is needed, O Immaculate Virgin, we ask it of You by the immense love You have for Jesus. Ah, yes, deign to appear to the Jews and to the Turks, as You once appeared to Ratisbonne, and at a sign of Your right hand they, like him, will be converted! Oh, may the day come, may it come soon, when the most holy Trinity reigns through You in all hearts, and all know, love and adore in spirit and truth the divine fruit of Your womb, Jesus!”74.

60. – St. Paul VI prayed at the abbey of Grottaferrata for “complete Catholic unity, so that there may flourish before our eyes, in our historical landscape so full of suffering, the evidence of the miracle that we are all one flock of one shepherd”75.

61. – St. Paul VI: “As you know, this problem of the ‘union of Christians in the unity of the Church’ is of great importance and timeliness, and we must face it, even though it meets with, and indeed brings to light, many difficulties. Divine help is more necessary than ever, like a miracle of the Lord. But perhaps the hour is near. That is why we must pray much”76.

62. That is why St. John Paul II pointed out that, following the example of the Virgin Mary, “docility to the Holy Spirit… is the deepest center of the ecumenical attitude”77.

g) Formal cause of ecumenical unity

63. The formal cause of every society consists in a certain order, that is, in the particular relationship established among its members with a view to a common end; thus, the formal principle is that which is able to establish the bonds of union and mutual ordering among its members.

64. We can thus say that the formal cause of ecumenical unity is in some way attributable to grace78, because among Christians all relationships, insofar as they are Christians, presuppose and proceed from created grace. It is grace that establishes the fundamental incorporation into God, since grace itself is a participation in the divine nature79. The Christian’s grace is derived from the fullness of grace of Christ; as St. Thomas says: “What is principal in the new law is constituted by the grace of the Holy Spirit, which is manifested in faith working through charity. Now men obtain this grace through the Son of God made man, whose humanity was first filled with grace (replevit gratia), so that it might then flow down to us. That is why Jn 1:14 says: The Word became flesh; and further on adds: full of grace and truth, and further below (v.16): From His fullness we have all received, grace upon grace. Hence it is added (v.17) that grace and truth have come through Jesus Christ”80.

65. The Christian’s grace is derived from the grace of the Son, and makes us sons by adoption; consequently it makes men brothers to one another. From grace flow the other bonds of union:

– From it springs faith, which unites the faithful in the profession of the same revealed truth.

– From it springs hope, which unites wayfarers in one and the same supernatural hope.

– From it springs charity, which unites the faithful with God and with one another in the most excellent way (“Proprium amoris est unire”81); that is why St. Paul calls it the bond of perfection (Col 3:14).

  1. It is also grace that truly unites Christians with one another; and since it is the Holy Spirit who accomplishes the work of grace, He establishes mysterious bonds which, for the moment, escape the visible dimensions of the Church: these are the ones who belong invisibly to the visible Church.
  2. h) Ecumenical hope
  3. It is evident that the Church directs strenuous efforts toward procuring unity; one might then ask about the hope she cherishes of attaining what she seeks: on what is it founded, what certainty does it have, what is its precise object?

The foundation

  1. The foundation of the Church’s ecumenical hope rests basically on a prophecy and on a prayer of Christ Himself.
  2. a) The prophecy. St. John recalls it (10:16): I have other sheep that are not of this fold, and I must bring them, and they will hear my voice, and there will be one flock and one shepherd. The prophetic character of the verse is evident from its very wording, set in the future—they will hear, there will be— and the affirmation of an unconditioned future from the mouth of God is prophecy in the strict sense. This is how the Holy Fathers, Pontiffs and Councils have understood it, using this text in a prophetic sense82. So much so that St. John Chrysostom states: “it does not indicate necessity, but something that will certainly come to pass”83, and St. Jerome: “which, although we see it partly fulfilled in the Church every day, will nevertheless be more fully fulfilled at the consummation of the world and at the second coming of the Savior”84. In support of the prophetic character of this passage, the Pauline prophecy, which may be considered parallel, is also used: I do not want you to be unaware, brothers, of this mystery—so that you may not be wise in your own eyes—that a hardening has come upon part of Israel, until the fullness of the nations has come in; and so all Israel will be saved (Rom 11:25-26).
  3. b) The prayer. The prayer is the one Jesus makes at the Last Supper, before His Passion: That all may be one, as You, Father, are in Me and I in You, that they also may be one in us, so that the world may believe that You sent me (Jn 17:21). This unity asked for by Jesus has been identified by numerous Fathers, Doctors and Pontiffs with ecumenical unity. Since it is an absolute prayer of Christ, its efficacy is certain and infallible.

The certainty

  1. The certainty derives from the character of these two texts: from the prophetic character of the one, and from the character of efficacious prayer of the second.

The object of the Church’s hope

  1. The object of this hope, that is, what concretely is hoped for, is contained in the exegesis and use that Tradition and the Magisterium have made of these biblical texts; in short:
  2. – The fold into which “the other sheep” that Christ has are to be incorporated has always been identified with the Catholic Church and with communion with the Apostolic See of Rome.
  3. – The other sheep are all those who are outside the Catholic Church, founded by Christ on Peter and the Apostles, in any time and place. The text of St. Paul speaks of fullness; this must be understood as a moral, not a mathematical, fullness, that is, what people ordinarily mean by “the whole world,” without this excluding the existence of non-Catholic or non-Christian minority groups85.
  4. – The Shepherd in some texts is Christ86, in others it is Peter and the Roman Pontiff, Successor of Peter and Vicar of Christ87. In general terms, in the customary use that Tradition has made of the Johannine text, Christ cannot be dissociated from Peter and his successors.
  5. Therefore, the object of the Church’s ecumenical hope is the future (but historical, prior to the Second Coming of the Savior) union of the fullness of mankind and of the Churches in the one flock shepherded by Peter, Vicar of Christ.

2. The practice of ecumenism

  1. For a fruitful practice of ecumenism, the Church has gradually developed a series of norms that must guide all ecumenical activity.

a) Need for
institutional renewal

78. True ecumenism must begin with the exemplary presentation of the Catholic Church herself. The Church would lose credibility if she proposed a restoration of the unity of Christians while at the same time displaying internal failings of unity and holiness. “Christ calls the pilgrim Church on her way to this perennial reform, of which the Church herself, as a human and earthly institution, always has need… This reform, then, has an extraordinary ecumenical importance”88.

79. “In the teaching of the Council there is a clear link between renewal, conversion and reform. It states: ‘The Church, on pilgrimage in this world, is called by Christ to that continual reform which she, as an earthly and human institution, constantly needs; so that if certain things, owing to circumstances of time and place, have been observed less carefully, they should be set right at the opportune moment and in the proper way.’ No Christian Community can evade this call”89.

80. In this way we can point out the great enemies of ecumenism, from the standpoint of the Church’s exemplarity:

– Lack of unity. Failings of unity among the Catholic faithful are enemies of ecumenism: failings against submission to the authentic hierarchy, failings against the common patrimony of the faith (the work of dissenting theology), failings against unity resulting from the fostering of false dialectics (Church of the poor and Church of the rich, theology from above or theology from below, white theology or black theology, etc.). Failings against the Catholic liturgy, abandoning it to improvisation, to chaos, to contradiction. In this sense, progressivism, Marxist liberation theology, dissenting moral theology, etc., are enemies of authentic ecumenism. We will work in favor of ecumenism to the extent that we sow unity of faith, of governance, of worship.

  1. – Lack of holiness. Holiness is a mark of the true Church founded by Christ, in which the union that ecumenism seeks must come about; therefore, one sins against ecumenism when one presents an image of the Church stripped of holiness. In this sense, every sin, but particularly the sin of scandal (above all the sin of pastors), destroys ecumenical work. One works in favor of ecumenism when the works of mercy are lived in their fullness. Charity toward the poor, the sick, the most needy; the example of those who live the evangelical counsels radically; the example of the Martyrs: this is what moves toward ecumenical unity.
  2. – Lack of catholicity. That is, lack of missionary spirit and drive. The loss of missionary fervor, the lack of conviction about Jesus Christ’s mandate to evangelize, is a sin against ecumenism, because it is precisely this that manifests the Church founded by Him.
  3. – Lack of apostolicity. That is, the lack of love for Peter and his successors, contempt for his teachings and his directives, reluctance or indifference in supporting his initiatives, his particular intentions, his missionary plans. When this happens, one does not beat with the heart of the Church.
  4. For this reason, the practice of ecumenism requires a renewal and revitalization that is:

a) Institutional: giving examples of poverty, undertaking works of charity, of beneficence, of assistance, of service, founding missionary works, etc.

b) Liturgical: presenting the captivating richness of Catholic worship, respecting the diversity of rites (Latin, Ukrainian, Coptic, Malabar, etc.); enhancing our liturgy with solemnity and sacredness; showing the centrality of worship in the life and spirituality of the Church.

c) Doctrinal: presenting the faith with depth and clarity; giving renewed value to biblical, theological, patristic and philosophical studies; not hiding the truth but showing its splendor. It is important to stress that one of the arguments often put forward by converts to Catholicism has been, and still is, seeing the Catholic Church as a body of doctrine that is solid, secure and guaranteed.

b) The demand for personal renewal

85. “Although the Catholic Church possesses all the truth revealed by God and all the means of grace, her members nevertheless do not live by them consistently with all due fervor. As a result, the face of the Church shines less brightly before the eyes of our separated brethren and of the whole world, and the growth of the kingdom of God is thereby delayed. For this reason, all Catholics must strive toward Christian perfection and, each according to his state, work so that the Church, bearing in her body the humility and mortification of Jesus Christ, may be purified and renewed more each day, until Christ presents her to Himself in glory, without spot or wrinkle”90.

86. St. John Paul II said: “Unity can only be the fruit of a conversion to Christ, who is the Head of the Body which is the Church. Such conversion must be profound and embrace all the members in the many aspects of their life, so that unity may truly come about”91.

87. “There is no true ecumenism without interior conversion. Indeed, the desire for unity arises and matures in the renewal of the soul, in self-denial and in a generous outpouring of charity. That is why we must implore from the Holy Spirit the grace of sincere self-denial, of humility and gentleness in our service, and of brotherly generosity of soul toward others… Let all the faithful remember that the better they strive to lead a purer life, in conformity with the Gospel, the better they will bring about and promote the union of Christians. For the closer their communion with the Father, the Word and the Spirit, the more intimately and easily will they be able to deepen their mutual brotherhood”92.

88. “Each one, then, must be converted more radically to the Gospel and, never losing sight of God’s plan, must change the way he looks at things. Through ecumenism, the contemplation of the ‘wonders of God’ (mirabilia Dei) has been enriched with new spaces in which the Triune God gives rise to thanksgiving: the perception that the Spirit is at work in other Christian Communities, the discovery of examples of holiness, the experience of the limitless riches of the communion of Saints, contact with unexpected aspects of Christian commitment. On the other hand, the need for penance has also spread: awareness of certain exclusions that wound fraternal charity, of certain refusals that need to be forgiven, of a certain pride, of that unevangelical obstinacy in condemning ‘others,’ of a contempt born of a harmful presumption. Thus the whole life of Christians is marked by ecumenical concern, and they are called to take it up”93.

89. St. John Paul II said: “There can be no unity among brothers unless there is profound union—of life, of thought, of soul, of purpose, of imitation—with Christ Jesus; indeed, unless there is an intimate pursuit of interior life in union with the Trinity itself…”94.

c) Primacy of “spiritual ecumenism,” or the primacy of prayer

90. We have said that the principal Author of ecumenism is the Holy Spirit, that ecumenical unity is a grace, and that the return to unity surpasses human strength. Hence the primacy given to prayer in the work for unity. Ecumenical fruits do not spring principally from meetings and dialogues, but fundamentally from prayer to God.

91. “All of us acknowledge the great value of prayer for achieving what is humanly difficult or perhaps impossible. Jesus Himself told us: what is impossible for men is possible for God (Lk 18:27). We know how important it is to turn to God humbly, day after day, asking Him for the gift of continual conversion of life, which is so closely bound up with the question of Christian unity”95.

92. “This conversion of heart and this holiness of life, together with private and public prayers for the unity of Christians, are to be regarded as the soul of the whole ecumenical movement, and may rightly be called spiritual ecumenism”96.

“‘Ecumenical’ prayer serves the Christian mission and its credibility. That is why it must be especially present in the life of the Church and in every activity whose aim is to foster the unity of Christians. It is as if we were always to gather again in the Upper Room of Holy Thursday, even though our common presence in that place still awaits its perfect fulfillment, until, once the obstacles to perfect ecclesial communion have been overcome, all Christians gather in the one celebration of the Eucharist”97.

d) Need for a specifically ecumenical formation

93. An important point on which the Magisterium insists is the ever-growing need for the general ecumenical formation of the faithful, and of priests in particular. St. John Paul II said: “With ever greater urgency, theological formation, and above all that of future priests, requires an ecumenical dimension, truly well-founded and always assured. The Second Vatican Council clearly pointed out this need98. The present demands of the Church’s mission call for an ecumenical collaboration that cannot be put into practice without suitable spiritual, doctrinal and cultural preparation”99.

94. “Ecumenical formation” means preparation for taking up this task of promoting ecumenical unity. It begins with becoming aware of the place ecumenism holds within the Church and her mission, of the responsibility the Church has before Jesus Christ to seek this unity, and of the urgency of working for unity in our time. It requires knowledge of the history of the Church, of the divisions, of the doctrinal differences; it requires knowledge of the particular features of the other Churches and ecclesial Communities separated from the Catholic Church. It also demands a solid formation in the faith, a sound ecclesiology, in order to be able to give an account before the other confessions of “the reasons for our hope”100.

95. This formation must be provided to the faithful in general, through listening to and studying the Word of God, preaching (in particular on the mystery of the unity of the Church), catechesis, the liturgy (especially through prayer for the unity of Christians), and the spiritual life (reminding the faithful of the importance of personal conversion in order to aspire to unity)101.

96. As for ordained ministers, it is fitting to present to them the ecumenical dimension of the various subjects they study throughout their formation102, offering them, where possible, a special course on ecumenism103.

97. It is also desirable to aim for some (lay faithful or priests) to specialize in the ecumenical disciplines104.

e) Respect for legitimate diversity
in unity

98. Diversity and unity are not contradictory, because diversity can be brought back to unity through harmony. The accidental diversity of liturgical, spiritual and even disciplinary elements within the Church, harmonized with one another, helps to manifest outwardly the immense richness of the Catholic Church: “While preserving unity in what is necessary, let all in the Church, each according to the office entrusted to him, maintain due freedom, both in the various forms of spiritual life and of discipline and in the variety of liturgical rites, and even in the theological development of revealed truth; but in all things let them practice charity. In this way they will manifest ever more fully, day by day, the true catholicity and apostolicity of the Church”105.

f) Demand for a just appreciation

99. “On the other hand, it is necessary that Catholics joyfully acknowledge and esteem the truly Christian values, coming from the common patrimony, that are found among our separated brethren. It is right and salutary to recognize the riches of Christ and the virtuous works in the lives of all who bear witness to Christ, at times even by shedding their blood; for God is always wonderful and worthy of admiration in His works. Nor should it be forgotten that whatever the grace of the Holy Spirit works in the hearts of the separated brethren can also contribute to our own edification. What is truly Christian can never be opposed to the authentic values of the faith; on the contrary, everything can help toward a more perfect attainment of the mystery of Christ and of the Church”106.

g) A climate of authentic dialogue
and the search for unity in truth

100. “If prayer is the ‘soul’ of ecumenical renewal and of the yearning for unity, it is on prayer that all that the Council defines as ‘dialogue’ is based and from prayer that it draws its strength. This definition is certainly not far from personalist thought The attitude of ‘dialogue’ is situated at the level of the nature of the person and of his dignity. From the philosophical point of view, this position is connected with the Christian truth about man expressed by the Council. Indeed, man ‘is the only creature on earth that God has loved for its own sake’; therefore he ‘cannot fully find himself except through a sincere gift of himself.’ Dialogue is a necessary step along the path toward man’s self-realization, both of the individual and of every human community. Although the cognitive moment (dia–logos) seems to come to the fore in the concept of ‘dialogue,’ every dialogue contains a global, existential dimension. It involves the human subject entirely; dialogue between communities engages in a particular way the subjectivity of each of them”107.

101. Dialogue is fundamental for resolving the discrepancies produced as an effect of division and for dispelling those that were the cause of disunion. St. Peter warns us that we must always be ready to give an account of our hope: always ready to make a defense of the hope you cherish, answering everyone who asks you for a reason for it (1 Pet 3:15). Jesus Christ Himself gave us an example of His teaching method by dialoguing with those who hungered and thirsted for the truth: this is shown by His dialogue with the Samaritan woman108, with His disciples109, and with His enemies in order to lead them to the truth110.

102. St. Paul VI pointed out the following as characteristics of dialogue:

a) Clarity above all: dialogue presupposes and demands intelligibility; it is an exchange of thought; it therefore demands our apostolic diligence in making our language understandable.

103. b) Meekness: to which Jesus Christ Himself exhorts us: Learn from Me, for I am meek and humble of heart (Mt 11:29). “Dialogue is not proud, it is not hurtful, it is not offensive. Its authority is intrinsic, coming from the truth it sets forth, from the charity it spreads, from the example it offers; it is not a command or an imposition. It is peaceful, it avoids violent methods it is patient, it is generous”111.

104. c) Trust: both in the value of one’s own word and in the willingness of those in the conversation to receive it.

105. d) Pedagogical prudence: which takes careful account of the psychological and moral conditions of the listener.

106. e) Integrity: The presentation of the truth must be authentic and complete. “Eagerness to draw near to our brothers must not result in a watering down or lessening of the truth. Our dialogue cannot be a weakness with respect to the duty owed to our faith. The apostolate cannot make terms with a kind of ambiguous compromise regarding the principles of thought and action that must mark our Christian profession. Irenicism and syncretism are, at bottom, forms of skepticism about the power and content of the word of God that we wish to preach. Only one who is truly faithful to the doctrine of Christ can be an effective apostle. And only one who lives the Christian vocation to the full can be immune to the contagion of the errors with which he comes into contact”112.

107. Ecumenical dialogue, and especially theological dialogue, must not be superficial, because it certainly deals with delicate and profound questions: “If for almost a millennium the Churches of East and West have not celebrated the Eucharist together, this means that they have judged the disputed problems to be serious”113.

108. The unity that ecumenism seeks not only cannot dispense with the truth, but is unity in the truth itself. As St. John Paul II has said, vigorously and beautifully: “You must not resign yourselves to the disciples of Christ failing to give the world the witness of unity. What is required is unshakable fidelity to the truth, attentive openness to others, sober patience along the way and a love full of delicacy. Compromises are of no use; the only unity of value is that which the Lord Himself founded: unity in truth and in love. One hears it said again and again nowadays that the ecumenical movement among the Churches has stalled, that after the springtime blossoming of the Council a season of coldness has come. In spite of some regrettable difficulties, I cannot agree with this judgment. The unity that comes from God has been given to us on the cross. We are not permitted to try to get around the cross in order to attempt a quick harmonizing of differences, setting aside the question of truth. Nor, however, are we permitted to give up on one another, to do without one another, because the patient and self-sacrificing love of the Crucified demands that we draw near to one another. Let us not allow ourselves to be turned away from the wearisome road, whether to choose apparent shortcuts that are nothing but detours”114.

109. And with the same clarity, in Ut unum sint: “In this context it is not a matter of modifying the deposit of faith, changing the meaning of dogmas, removing essential words from them, adapting the truth to the tastes of an age, or striking certain articles from the Creed on the false pretext that they are no longer understandable today. The unity willed by God can only be achieved through common adherence to the full content of revealed faith. In matters of faith, a compromise solution is in contradiction with God who is Truth. In the Body of Christ, who is the way, the truth and the life (Jn 14:6), who would consider legitimate a reconciliation obtained at the cost of the truth?”115.

h) Practical collaboration

110. St. John Paul II teaches the ecumenical importance of collaboration among Christians. Indeed, this collaboration is the fruit of relations among Christians, and at the same time it is a school of ecumenism and a true evangelizing witness.

“Relations among Christians do not aim merely at mutual acquaintance, common prayer and dialogue. They foresee and demand, starting now, every possible form of practical collaboration in the various spheres: pastoral, cultural, social, and even in bearing witness to the message of the Gospel.

‘The cooperation of all Christians vividly expresses that bond by which they are already united with one another, and sets in a fuller light the face of Christ the servant.’ Such cooperation, founded on the common faith, is not only rich in fraternal communion but is an epiphany of Christ Himself.

Moreover, ecumenical cooperation is a true school of ecumenism, a dynamic path toward unity. Unity of action leads to full unity of faith: ‘Through such cooperation, all who believe in Christ will easily learn how they can know one another better, esteem one another more, and smooth the path to the unity of Christians.’

In the eyes of the world, cooperation among Christians takes on the dimensions of a common Christian witness and becomes an instrument of evangelization for the benefit of both sides”116.

3. False conceptions
of ecumenism

  1. Ecumenism is opposed by errors of excess and of defect.

a) Errors of defect

112. Every view of ecumenism that sees in this phenomenon a novelty arising in the Church from the Second Vatican Council onward is false. Ecumenism, as we have already established, belongs to the very mission of the Church, as Jesus Christ established it. To consider it a “novelty” is the fruit of a defective ecclesiology, of ignorance of the Tradition and history of the Church, and of a poor understanding of the will of our Lord made explicit in the Discourse at the Last Supper, as it appears in the Gospel of St. John117.

113. Many, without going that far, are reluctant toward the efforts made and show themselves indifferent to this particular mission of the Church. Of them St. John Paul II said at the beginning of his pontificate: “There are people who, faced with the difficulties, or also judging negatively the results of the initial ecumenical work, would have preferred to turn back. Some even express the opinion that these efforts are harmful to the cause of the Gospel, that they lead to a further rupture of the Church, provoke confusion of ideas in questions of faith and morals, and end in a specific indifferentism… To all those who for whatever reason would wish to dissuade the Church from seeking the universal unity of Christians, it must be said once more: Is it permissible for us not to do it?”118.

b) Errors by excess: ecumenism
as religious syncretism

114. True ecumenism is also destroyed by all those practices or conceptions that see in this movement a tendency toward the formation of a super-Church, the fruit of pooling together all the Churches present, including the Catholic Church as just one more among them. This is an anti-Christian, anti-Catholic and anti-evangelical ecumenism. Such a conception of ecumenism is a threat to the identity of the Church.

115. In practice this is proposed on two levels:

a) Those who aspire to a purely pragmatic unity, in which each one would keep his own belief in a personal capacity. These are the ones who think that ecumenism consists in getting together to march side by side, while what each one thinks is not so important119.

b) Those who aspire to a unity that is even dogmatic, establishing for this super-Church a super-dogma that would set aside the elements that create “difficulty” for union; particularly the Catholic doctrine on the Primacy of Peter, papal infallibility, sacramental doctrine (especially the doctrine on the Eucharist and on the ministerial priesthood), etc.

116. It would be fitting to recall once more the words of the Holy Father John Paul II: “True ecumenical activity means openness, drawing closer, readiness for dialogue, a common search for the truth in the full evangelical and Christian sense; but in no way does it mean, nor can it mean, renouncing or in any way harming the treasures of divine truth, constantly confessed and taught by the Church… This absolutely does not mean losing the certainty of one’s own faith, or weakening the principles of morality, the lack of which will very soon make itself felt in the life of entire societies, bringing about, among other things, deplorable consequences”120.

117. This can in no way help the cause of Christian unity. Indeed, we must say that the best means to distort the ecumenical ideal is to contaminate it with ambiguous generosities, with calculated silences, with false tolerances (especially in the sacramental field)121; all of this proceeds more from the weakness or the cunning of men than from the clear, strong, most loyal fidelity of the adopted children of God.

118. True ecumenism is destroyed when Catholic theologians work while neglecting the principles of Catholic theology; when one operates with the principle of sola Scriptura, neglecting Tradition, the Magisterium and perennial philosophy. Also when specific aspects of the faith or of the structure of the Catholic Church are presented obscurely or in a veiled manner, particularly the sacramental doctrine on the ministerial priesthood, the Eucharist, the forgiveness of sins through individual Confession, and the indissolubility of marriage. This cannot contribute to unity; whereas, on the contrary, clear definitions of one’s own faith serve everyone, including one’s interlocutor; it must never be forgotten that dialogue can deepen and purify the Catholic faith, but it cannot change it. What St. John Paul II said can be applied here: “The reason for a certain number of difficulties in believing and of individual or collective religious crises lies in the fact that the absolute is relativized and the relative is absolutized”122.

“The ministry of divine mercy”1

God reconciled us to Himself through Christ and entrusted to us the ministry of reconciliation2. The words of the Apostle are especially challenging to us, to whom, as priests, belongs the sublime exercise of the ministry of divine mercy.

“Never as in this time has man had such need of mercy,” St. John Paul the Great said already in the ’80s. It is in this same sense that Pope Francis reminded us in his Apostolic Letter Misericordia et misera: “The Sacrament of Reconciliation needs to find again its central place in Christian life; for this, priests are needed who place their lives at the service of the ministry of reconciliation3, so that no one who has sincerely repented may be prevented from approaching the love of the Father, who awaits his return, and so that all may be offered the possibility of experiencing the liberating power of forgiveness”4.

That is why to speak of the priesthood implies speaking of the care of souls, and to speak of priests is to speak of men who by the sacrament of Orders have been called to be “living and effective signs of the mercy of God”5, knowing themselves to be “the first to be forgiven”6.

1. The assiduous pastoral practice of confession7

As members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word we are called in a particular way to evangelize the culture. “But how does one evangelize a culture? How does one help the work of the Holy Spirit among men? One begins by evangelizing people, since culture is produced by people and is forged by the quality of their relationships with one another and with God. Thus, the first step is to evangelize, as Jesus did, calling each one by name to conversion”8. A conversion that implies the vital need to be united to Christ through an intense Eucharistic life and to be continually renewed through the reception of the sacrament of Reconciliation.

Understood in this way, the mission of the Institute is the same as that of the Church, that is, “to lead men to conversion to God, to ‘full and sincere adherence to Christ and His Gospel through faith’9, which must tend toward the worthy reception of the sacraments”10. From which it follows that the reception of the sacrament of Confession constitutes a special help—or, better said, an indispensable one—when it comes to evangelizing the culture. Indeed, we are convinced that “mercy is indispensable for the spiritual progress of each soul, as well as for human, civil and social progress”11, and therefore we consider that our mission is effective if, as John Paul II said, it “moves to conversion, that is, to a return to the truth and to the friendship of God, those who had lost faith and grace through sin; if it calls routine Christians to a more perfect life; if it sets souls on fire; if it convinces people to live the beatitudes; if it awakens priestly and religious vocations”12. It is striking that we should have to remind ourselves and reaffirm to ourselves and to others these truths in our time, when it often seems that a veil is drawn over the founding and fundamental mission of the Church in the world. For its part, the apostolate of the confessional is explicitly highlighted by our Proper Law in a forceful way when it says: “our priests and the whole Institute of the Incarnate Word should be known for the assiduous pastoral practice of confession […] Let people know that everywhere there are priests of our Religious Family, they will always be able to find a ‘spiritual father’”13.

The Exhortation Reconciliatio et Paenitentia, written by the Spiritual Father of our Religious Family, maintains that “the Son of God, coming as the Lamb who takes away and takes upon Himself the sin of the world14, appears as the one who has the power both to judge15 and to forgive sins16, and who has come not to condemn, but to forgive and save17”18. And this power that Jesus has to forgive sins He conferred on the Apostles and their successors, responsible for continuing His work of evangelization and redemption. From this the greatness of the figure of the minister of Penance becomes evident, who must act in Persona Christi as “brother of man19, merciful high priest, faithful and compassionate20, shepherd determined to seek the lost sheep21, physician who heals and comforts22, sole master who teaches the truth and points out the ways of God23, judge of the living and the dead24, who judges according to the truth and not according to appearances25”26.

Now, the assiduous pastoral practice of Confession requires on our part great pastoral charity and great mercy in order to bring relief to the souls entrusted to our care.

Confession is often spoken of as the “tribunal of penance,” and that is not wrong, because Penance has something of a tribunal about it, and the priest is the judge. But Penance is much more than that. “Unlike purely human tribunals, it is more an instrument of mercy than an instrument of justice. That is why it is also called the sacrament of Reconciliation. In absolving, the priest frees the penitent from the sin that held him prisoner. In the confessional, besides being judges, we must be fathers, teachers, physicians—in a word, true shepherds of the little sheep placed at our feet. We must consider the circumstances, background and psychology of the penitent; have the heart of a father, of the good Samaritan who cauterizes the wounds inflicted by the robbers. If God has waited, showing infinite patience, by what right do we, who are also sinners just like the one before us, flare up at the penitent, or treat him with bitterness of spirit?”27. And where does a priest learn mercy? He learns it from the deep conviction that God did not treat us as we deserved28, said Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen.

The exercise of the sublime office of hearing confessions takes on for us, priests of the Institute of the Incarnate Word, certain particular features that our Proper Law especially underlines, echoing the abundant treasure of the Magisterium of the Church, and which it is always fitting to keep in mind if we do not want to betray our identity and mission in the Mystical Body of the Church. Let us pay attention:

  • “The maximum availability of priests for confession must be something characteristic of our parishes, also making sure that there are always one or more confessors during Holy Mass. Other priests from outside the parish should also be invited to hear confessions, and where possible and fitting, a permanent confession center should be set up”29. Our Proper Law repeats something very similar elsewhere, declaring that the “apostolate toward sinners will be exercised in a particular way in the administration of the sacrament of reconciliation, being always available at any moment to hear confessions”30.

This maximum availability asks of us that we be always available for so high a ministry, and not only during “confession hours.” But it is also true that this availability must be made visible to people. One of the ways in which this availability on the part of IVE priests to hear confessions can be made visible to souls is what St. Manuel González called ‘early-morning worship’: “A church open from very early, at least an hour before the work of the town begins, with a priest who is the first to come in and the last to leave, and who waits seated in the confessional, and this in a constant and fixed way, is a church that cannot be long in seeing itself well attended.[…] A priest seated in his confessional from before sunrise, determined not to tire or grow bored with the solitude, will not be long in seeing Samaritan women and men arrive to ask him for the water that springs up to eternal life”31. “How are we to foster among our parishioners, especially busy ones, daily meditation at a fixed time, frequent confession and spiritual direction, if we do not give them, at a fixed and early hour, an open church and Tabernacle and a confessor at their disposal?”32. A holy suggestion to keep in mind both in the parishes of the most remote villages and in those set in the great cities.

This disposition is also shown when it comes to attending to the sick promptly (without delay), which so often calls for the sacrament of divine mercy. “Illness makes it possible for many sick people to approach the Sacraments for the first time; that is why our solicitude will be very important, working with their family, and especially when the family opposes the Sacraments of Penance and Anointing for fear of the fright this may cause the patient”33. It is an act of true love to comfort the sick with the sacraments, especially Confession, the Anointing of the sick, and the viaticum of the Eucharist—the last, before they see God beyond the sacramental signs. That is why the Polish Pope insistently recommended to priests: “Always be diligent and fervent in the administration of these sacraments of mercy, sparing neither energy nor time, deeply aware that ‘the Church lives an authentic life when she professes and proclaims mercy—the most stupendous attribute of the Creator and of the Redeemer—and when she brings people close to the sources of the Savior’s mercy, of which she is the trustee and dispenser’34”35.

Moreover, on the occasion of popular missions, one must not be remiss in dedicating one day of the mission to be “a penitential day, on which confessions are heard all day long. Strictly speaking, it is ideal for one missionary father always to remain at the mission center and for the people to know that he is at their disposal from the first day of the mission”36.

Persuaded of the importance of the life of grace and of the immense value of the sacrament of confession, we can affirm that most of our pastoral activities should include this availability for confession. We have learned this countless times from the beginning: for example, alongside our processions there were always priests with the purple stole, praying the rosary, whose mere presence was a silent but eloquent call to reconciliation with God for the people taking part in or watching the procession; the same happens in our camps, in gatherings with young people, with families, in the oratories, where there must never be lacking the priest who waits patiently, ready to hear confessions.

Given the special circumstances we are going through because of the pandemic, how edifying and praiseworthy has been the example of several of our members who, even at the risk of becoming infected themselves, with all courage and priestly spirit went to the sick to hear their confession and bring them the last sacraments. That is the true mettle of a priest of the Incarnate Word! One who “does not flinch at any difficulty, nor shrink back before any obstacle!”37.

From what has been said so far we can conclude that every priest of the Institute must devote a generous part of his time to the ministry of the sacrament of Penance—which the grace of God will make fruitful—and to instructing souls about the value and importance of this sacrament for the Christian life; which brings us to the second characteristic of the exercise of this ministry highlighted in the documents of our Institute.

  • “It seems very fitting that priests preach about the marvelous benefits of frequent confession”38. In this regard the Directory of Preaching of the Word specifies—following the teachings of St. Alphonsus Maria de Liguori39—that when choosing the subject matter of preaching, the priests of the Institute must “take care to choose those that in a special way move people to abhor sin and to love God”40; “… the love Jesus Christ has for us, the love we owe Him, and the trust we must always have in His mercy when we want to amend our lives”41; “… the means of remaining in the grace of God, such as fleeing dangerous occasions and bad company, frequenting the sacraments…”42; “… bad confessions made by concealing sins out of shame”43, etc.

That is why the Constitutions point out that people (including our own members, and especially those in formation) must be helped to “discover the beauty and joy of the sacrament of penance, in a world that has lost the sense of sin and of divine mercy”44.

  • “Striving to possess the due knowledge and keeping up to date in moral questions”45. For the members of the Institute “knowledge of the due moral science is indispensable”46, and this on a twofold account: both for exercising the ministry of reconciliation47 and also “for fulfilling the purpose of our Institute of evangelizing cultures, which presupposes ordering them so that they help man to attain his end, and therefore a discernment of their morality, since morality is the transcendental relation of what is human toward the ultimate end”48. That is why all “priests must have knowledge of spiritual theology and must exercise themselves in the discernment of spirits”49. For priests are “educators in the faith, formers of consciences, guides of souls, so as to enable each Christian to develop his personal vocation”50; that is why it is fitting, and very much so, to prepare oneself well—remotely and proximately—for the administration of the sacrament of Confession.

In Reconciliatio et Paenitentia we read that for the effective fulfillment of the ministry of Confession the priest “must also have a serious and careful preparation, not fragmentary but integral and harmonious, in the various branches of theology, in pedagogy and in psychology, in the methodology of dialogue and, above all, in a living and communicative knowledge of the Word of God. But it is even more necessary that he live an intense and genuine spiritual life. In order to guide others along the path of Christian perfection, the minister of Penance must first travel this path himself and, more by deeds than by long speeches, give proof of real experience of lived prayer, of the practice of the theological and moral evangelical virtues, of faithful obedience to the will of God, of love for the Church and of docility to her Magisterium”51.

Of the great confessor St. Leopold Mandić it is said that “he had taken very seriously his preparation for carrying out so delicate a ministry, not only by studying thoroughly dogmatic theology, law, moral theology, ascetical and mystical theology during the period of his studies prior to the priesthood, but afterward he continued to apply himself very seriously to this kind of study throughout his whole life.

His preferred authors were St. Augustine, St. Thomas and St. Alphonsus Maria de Liguori; along with them he always had at hand other modern authors […]. He also attached great importance to the encyclicals of the Popes, since, as he used to say, in them one finds the easiest and surest way to resolve the most difficult questions, even from the point of view of moral casuistry”52.

It is particularly recommended to us that, in our effort to acquire knowledge and exercise ourselves in the discernment of spirits, prayer, the study of Sacred Scripture, of the writings of the Fathers, of the great Theologians and of the Mystics, one’s own experience of the spiritual life, as well as familiarity with and deep knowledge of the Rules for the discernment of spirits of St. Ignatius of Loyola53, should all occupy a preponderant place.

“Do not cease to study and pray, so as to be equal to the ministry of reconciling man with God, a faculty so unheard-of that it made people exclaim in astonishment: Who can forgive sins but God alone?”54.

  • Finally, and above all, following the example of Christ, we must exercise this precious ministry of Confession showing a heart of true mercy55.

“The priest has to live a truly heroic mercy. […] A heroic mercy that must be shown in a particular way in the exercise of that great sacrament of mercy which is confession, which involves hours and hours and is very tiring. At some point it can become a boring thing for the priest who for so many years has been hearing more or less the same things, give or take, with some small addition. One must have that disposition which only a merciful heart gives”56.

St. Alphonsus, Patron of confessors, merited this title because from the first years of his priesthood he heard a great many confessions, above all during spiritual exercises and popular missions, acquiring in this field an incomparable experience.

In his Praxis confessarii he indicates the conditions for the exercise of this ars artium—as he liked to define it, following the example of St. Gregory the Great—to be fruitful: “The confessor cannot consider himself satisfied with a holiness limited to the mere state of grace, but must feel himself filled with charity, meekness and prudence”57.

“Thanks to these virtues,” commented St. John Paul II, “the confessor will be able to become a minister of divine charity, exercising the far from easy task of father, physician, teacher and judge.

As a father, he will welcome penitents with sincere love, showing greater understanding to those who have sinned more, and then he will send them off with words imbued with mercy, so as to encourage them to return to the path of Christian life58.

As a physician, he must prudently diagnose the roots of the evil and suggest to the penitent the appropriate therapy, thanks to which he may live in accordance with the dignity and responsibility of a person created in the image of God59.

As a teacher, he will seek to know the law of God thoroughly, deepening its various aspects through the study of moral theology, so as not to give the penitent personal options, but what the magisterium of the Church authentically teaches60.

As a judge, finally, he is to practice equity. It is necessary that the priest always judge according to the truth, and not according to appearances, taking care to make the penitent understand that in the fatherly heart of God there is room for him too”61.

In imitation of the Incarnate Word we must have, in all our pastoral endeavors, that patience and that kindness of which the Lord Himself has left us an example, having come not to judge, but to save62. “Like Christ, be uncompromising with evil, but merciful with persons. In whatever difficulties they may have, the faithful must find in your words and in your shepherds’ hearts the echo of the voice of the Redeemer, meek and humble of heart63”64, said St. John Paul II.

Fulton Sheen tells of a woman who, after thirty years, decided to go to confession. The confessor, a priest who in thirty years had not made his meditation before Mass, barked the following question at her: ‘How is it possible that you have been away from the Church for thirty years?’ To which the woman replied: ‘Because thirty years ago I met a priest just like you.’ “The depth of our compassion is the measure of our apostolic success”65, the venerable American archbishop concluded.

“Many times the priest is very busy, but he has to learn to occupy himself with the principal things of the ministry, not with accidental or secondary things. Souls need to speak with the priest and need to tell him their concerns, because the priest is also a physician of souls”66.

Finally, but no less importantly, let us say that if in the recent past, and even more in the present, there is something like a crisis of the sacrament of Confession, we must recognize that among the many factors that may have contributed to this situation, a lack of enthusiasm or availability for the exercise of so demanding and delicate a ministry not infrequently has a negative influence. Therefore, if the pastoral work of the Institute is to be in every sphere “with grit,” enthusiastic and proactive, it must also be so when it comes to carrying out with firmness and conviction the apostolate of the reconciliation of man with God. In this sense, “the sacrament must be celebrated in the best possible way and in the liturgically prescribed forms, so that it may preserve its full character as a celebration of Divine Mercy”67. In this sense it is important that the minister fulfill his obligation well. His capacity to welcome, to listen, to dialogue, and his constant availability, are essential elements for the ministry of reconciliation to manifest all its value. The faithful proclamation, never reticent, of the radical demands of the word of God must always be accompanied by great understanding and delicacy, in imitation of Jesus’ manner with sinners”68.

2. Personal experience of the sacrament of Reconciliation

It has always been the teaching of the Church that through priests is shown the mercy of God, who forgives sins in the sacrament of Penance. And although the administration of the sacrament of forgiveness is “without doubt the most difficult and delicate, the most tiring and demanding, it is also one of the most beautiful and consoling ministries of the Priest”69. “Be, therefore,” John Paul II said to priests and religious, “the first to receive this sacrament frequently, with authentic faith and devotion”70. For in order to be faithful and to fulfill adequately our ministry of sanctifying, our personal experience of the sacrament of reconciliation, through frequent confession, is indispensable. Since the joyful experience of being forgiven by Christ nourishes the desire to offer His forgiveness to others71.

That is why, “for our tiny Religious Family the holy sacrament of Reconciliation or Penance occupies a most important place in the spiritual life, so much so that we consider it advisable to receive it weekly”72. That has been and continues to be normal practice according to our way of living religious life in the Institute. And our Proper Law exhorts us in a fatherly way to “have devotion to frequent confession”73, listing the “many fruits that follow from it: ‘…genuine self-knowledge is increased, Christian humility grows, bad habits are uprooted, spiritual lukewarmness and indolence are resisted, the conscience is purified, the will is strengthened, a salutary direction of consciences is obtained, and grace is increased by virtue of the sacrament’”74.

That is to say, in the same way that availability is asked for in administering the sacrament of Confession, assiduity in receiving it is also asked of our members, so that Christ may constantly purify our hearts, making His ministers less unworthy of the mysteries they celebrate75.

It is worth noting that the Liturgy, very aptly, in the Roman Canon calls priests “sinners”: “To us, also, your sinful servants…”76. Priests carry a treasure in earthen vessels77. That is why it is important “to protect it and care for it, through daily prayer, through the celebration of Holy Mass and through frequent reception of the sacrament of Penance”78, the Spiritual Father of our Religious Family assured us. Our members must never underestimate the importance of the sacrament of Penance, together with that of the Eucharist, for strengthening the soul for the time of trial.

“Who does not see that the priest, constituted by God as minister of Christ’s reconciliation, is called to be the first to experience in himself the gift of reconciliation, making it operative in his own life? We are persuaded that it is not possible to propose the message of reconciliation to others if we are not capable of living its saving power in ourselves.

In a Church called to renew herself, priests must go before their brothers by their example and their life. From this personal appreciation of the sacrament of penance as the royal road of purification and growth in faith there will also come to you a further appreciation of the immeasurable gift the Lord has given you in choosing you as His priests, to forgive sins in His name”79.

The priest who is familiar with the Lord in prayer is the one who has the clearest awareness of being a sinner, even if he had never committed a mortal sin in his life. That is why, in his first Letter to Priests for Holy Thursday 1979, John Paul II insisted to them: “To be converted means to return to the very grace of our vocation, to meditate on the immense goodness and infinite love of Christ, who has addressed each one of us and, calling us by name, has said: Follow me. To be converted means to give an account at every moment of our service, of our zeal, of our fidelity, before the Lord of our hearts, so that we may be ministers of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God80. To be converted means also to give an account of our negligences and sins, of our cowardice, of our lack of faith and hope, of thinking only ‘in a human way’ and not ‘in a divine way’.”

The priestly vocation is a special vocation to holiness, so that, acting in the person of Christ, priests may sanctify others. Thus, then, as ministers of the sacraments, priests bring the gift of salvation to the people of God and nourish them with the divine life that they themselves have received from Christ. Now, the challenge of presenting Christ demands constant conversion. “The priest,” says Fulton Sheen, “who has been crucified and has borne his passion with patience will always be a merciful priest. If outside one confessional on a Saturday there is a long line of penitents and at the other confessional there are one or two penitents, it is time for that priest [at the latter confessional] to ask himself some questions. Holiness attracts penitents. Because the attraction of such priests is that of Christ”81.

If for all the members of the Institute it will always be of enormous spiritual benefit to consider oneself a sinner, how much more so for the ministers of the sacred mysteries, who need continual conversion. St. Joseph Cafasso said: “To be an exemplary priest, the judgment of the world is not enough. A priest can be reputed a saint and not be one before God. A third of the virtues proper to a cleric are enough for him to be considered a saint, but the Lord does not recognize him as such unless he strives with all his strength to flee not only mortal sin, but also venial fault and the appearance of guilt”82. It is this clear awareness that we are sinners that must lead His ministers to be men of mercy.

With great realism the venerable American archbishop wrote: “Every time his knees bend in confession, the priest admits that he has had something to do with the Crucifixion of our Lord, and when his feet move, like worms, coming out from beneath the curtain of the confessional, he rises from the dead. God did not choose angels to be His priest-victims, because they would not have understood what human weakness is. He chose instead fragile vessels who know, in their very weakness, the hope of being strong again that burns in the heart of a believer. In hearing the confessions of others, a priest sees more empty tombs than those the sun of the First Easter lit up in Jerusalem. When he himself goes to confession, his spirit feels the sweet indifference that comes from faith. If I die, I shall be with Christ; if I live, Christ will be with me”83.

“There are priests,” said St. Joseph Cafasso, “who go to confession, when they can, in hiding, in secret: they seem afraid that people will know they go to confession. Why so much mystery on our part when we go to confession? Will the faithful perhaps cease to esteem us if they see us frequenting such a sacrament?”84. Quite the contrary, the saint considered it a good example for people to see a priest go to confession.

The vast majority of the members of the Institute have the grace of living with another priest of the Institute or at least near others. Whatever the case, our Proper Law emphasizes that the “confession of the members to one another is an excellent manifestation of the exquisite charity that must reign in the community”85, and points out that “every priest of the Institute of the Incarnate Word should have as an absolute apostolic priority attention and availability toward his brothers in religious life. That is why fostering visits—especially to those who are farthest away and most isolated—and being generous with one’s own time toward one’s brothers in the priesthood and religious life is one of the clearest manifestations of the exquisite charity that our Family wants to live”86.

These visits to the priests who are farthest away, even if they involve long hours of travel only to be there for an hour or two and then set out again on the journey back, are something we have grown up seeing our elders do, and they should not be lost; rather, it is a precious example that we priests of the Institute should pass on to future generations.

In a word: let us love this sacrament, let us cooperate through our availability in giving it the central place in Christian life that the Holy Father asks of us, and let us receive it often ourselves.

* * * * *

Let us conclude with the words of Reconciliatio et paenitentia, which, although written 36 years ago now, remains as relevant as it was then:

“Be all of one mind…, not returning evil for evil…, be zealous for what is good87. […] For it is better to suffer for doing good, if such is the will of God, than for doing evil88.

This watchword is imbued with the words Peter had heard from Jesus Himself, and with ideas that were part of His joyful good news: the new commandment of mutual love; the desire for and commitment to unity; the beatitudes of mercy and of patience in persecution for the sake of justice; returning good for evil; the forgiveness of offenses; love of enemies. In these words and ideas is the original and transcendent synthesis of Christian ethics or, better and more profoundly, of the spirituality of the New Covenant in Jesus Christ”89.

We entrust to the Father, rich in mercy; to the Son of God, made man as our redeemer and reconciler; and to the Holy Spirit, source of unity and peace, the pastoral ministry of reconciliation that the Institute carries out throughout the world, and which is so intrinsically united to our specific end.

May Most Holy Mary, in whom the reconciliation of man with God was accomplished by Christ, increase our appreciation of the need for and importance of the Sacrament of Penance90 and inspire in us an enthusiastic pastoral ministry in this regard. Likewise, let us ask Our Lady for the grace to know how to recognize that the sacrament of Confession is also a help, a guidance and a medicine for priestly life91.

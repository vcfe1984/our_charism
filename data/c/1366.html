Votive Mass of St. Thomas Aquinas

[Exordium] Dear all: today we have the immense grace –which brings with it so much happiness!– of celebrating Holy Mass in this place, in the Abbey of Fossanova, thus marking the beginning of the academic year of our houses of formation in Italy.

Fossanova is a historic place, a sacred place and, we must also admit, a very significant place for all of us.

– It is a sacred place! Through this place passed the direct disciples of St. Benedict, who in the year 529 (let us remember that St. Benedict died on March 21 of the year 547) founded the first monastery here upon the remains of an ancient Roman villa (some of whose ruins can still be seen in the square, as one leaves the Church).

– St. Bernard also passed through here, and left a group of monks to found in this place the first convent of the Cistercian order in Italy; they were French Cistercians who came from the abbey of Hautecombe in France. It was Pope Innocent II who in the year 1135 granted them this place, strategically located on the road that led from Rome to Naples, from where countless monks would set out to ‘colonize’ –we might say– all of central Italy1 (let us remember that St. Bernard died in the year 1153). It was these same monks who 28 years later began the construction of this most beautiful Church, which was consecrated after 55 years of work, in the year 1208.

– That is why this church, where the Incarnate Word has gathered us today, has a very rich history, in which it is also worth mentioning that it has been distinguished by the presence of –at least– two successors of Peter: once, by Pope Innocent III (Lotario dei Conti di Segni, born in Gavignano, in the territory of our dear Segni… the pope of the Fourth Crusade and of the Crusade against the Albigensian heretics), who came to this place to consecrate this temple… the temple where we are today! The other presence was that of Pope Gregory IV2, for he was a monk here in the 9th century, before being Pope.

– And to add even more to the great significance this place has for us, we must say that these walls, magnificent in their architecture, one day sheltered St. Thomas Aquinas, who, feeling ill when he had already set out to attend the Council of Lyon, stayed in the guesthouse of the Abbey, where on March 7, 1274, he was called by our Lord to enjoy definitive union with him as a reward for all his labors.

– It was here, in this very abbey, that with heartfelt emotion the Holy Doctor wished to make his profession, on his knees before the Eucharist that would be his Viaticum to eternal life, saying: “Now I receive You, my Jesus, who paid with your blood the price of the redemption of my soul. All the teachings I have written manifest my faith in Jesus Christ and my love for the Holy Catholic Church, of whom I profess myself an obedient son.” It was here, on his deathbed, that he produced his posthumous work, commenting on the Song of Songs for the monks3.

– And it was also here that Divine Providence determined that his remains should rest at the foot of this high altar, and that there should be carried out in 1321 what was called the Second Process of canonization of St. Thomas, devoted exclusively to the miracles worked by him after his death4.

Today, 743 years after the death of him who was “the Prince and Master of all the scholastic doctors”5, by divine grace and providence, our Institute comes to this place… we have come here! On a day that from now on will also be historic for our religious family, on this occasion, to begin a new academic year.

1. The importance of good formation

It is always worthwhile at the beginning of the year to reflect on the importance of our formation; for it is a reality… a fact… that many aspects of what our students in formation will do in the future, as religious and missionaries, in the various posts where God calls them to serve him –whether in the cloister or in mission lands–, will be marked in some way by the seriousness, the integrity, the solidity and the depth of the formation that today, with the celebration of this Holy Mass, begins again.

That is why I would like briefly on this occasion to highlight the great importance for our times, because of the heavenly fruits it produces, of the study of the doctrine of St. Thomas, which in the words of Pope Urban V is “truthful and Catholic”6 and which “has above the others –Innocent VI added– propriety of words, order in its matters, truth in its judgments, in such a way that those who follow it will never be seen to stray from the path of truth, and whoever attacks it will always be suspect of error”7. A doctrine that becomes key when it comes to evangelizing the modern world, and which –as we have just said in the lectio brevis– shows itself more necessary and timely than ever for the times in which we have to live.

2. The world we are called to evangelize

The world in which we live… The Venerable Servant of God, Archbishop Fulton Sheen, in the prologue of his book “Religión sin Dios,” describes with clairvoyance the religious panorama we face today… the religious panorama of the contemporary world. He says there, for example, that in today's world “the word ‘God’ is still used by certain thinkers, but it is emptied of all its content and left watered-down and diffuse, so that it can be made to signify any idea, however volatile, or any kind of insubstantial fantasy.” He characterizes modern thought as a thought in which “God has been dethroned, the heavens have been abandoned, and man has been put in the place of God[…].”

Thus the Venerable Archbishop formulated the same thing that our Cornelio Fabro would later denounce systematically and brilliantly in his studies, in his courses and in his books: namely, the primacy of immanence and its fatal consequence, atheism, a world without God.

Bishop Fulton Sheen continues his “description” of our world, saying that: “The problems that formerly centered on God are now solved with reference to man, and those that formerly referred to man are now merged with the whole universe. […] God is humanized, and man naturalized. Physical science […] comes to tell us what God is and what man is.” And he goes on to say: “Man now not only imagines himself to be God, but actually believes he is beginning to be so. And thus man, raised to this state which they call ‘supernatural,’ needs no redeemer; just as in the natural state he does not need God. As a result of this philosophy of self-sufficiency there arises the bizarre phenomenon proper to our times, of a religion without God and a Christianity without Jesus Christ.” The general principle implicit in all this is, in the end, the denial of the transcendent and the affirmation of the immanent8.

From this follow the nefarious errors with which we have to live. And so –I continue quoting the venerable Archbishop– preeminence is given “to man instead of God… to love of neighbor instead of love of God… [one speaks of] a social message instead of a message of salvation… of openness to the world instead of the Cross… of the truth of the times instead of absolute truth”9; “faith is diluted into the rational, the sacred becomes profane”10, and so there is an attempt to accommodate the Church to the modern world, to group Catholicism with Protestantism; theology and philosophy are finally confused with sociology and psychology11, and there is no longer any talk of the slavery of sin, of the reality of hell, etc., all of which is consistent with immanence, since thus, under these presuppositions, as Fr. Cornelio Fabro said, “without hell, existence becomes a country outing”12.

3. Our preparation to evangelize the world in which we live

Our Proper Law says that it is to this world that God sends us as lambs among wolves in order to transform – “the wolves themselves into lambs”13; to seek and know how to identify the “seeds of the Word” through which the Holy Spirit continues to act in the heart of man14. To be the salt and light of the world15.

This magnificent undertaking requires of us –who are essentially missionaries– a competent philosophical preparation, which above all gives us “a solid and coherent knowledge of man, of the world and of God”16, for it represents “an indispensable help for deepening the understanding of the faith and communicating the truth of the Gospel to those who do not yet know it”17. It is in this sense that our Constitutions highlight the urgency of its study18, since it is sound philosophy, the metaphysics of being, that gives us a “certainty of the truth,” and “without it there is no basis for a total personal self-giving to Jesus and to the Church, since ‘if one is not sure of the truth, how can we stake our own life and have the strength to seriously challenge the lives of others?’”19

Let us strive, then, to acquire what our Proper Law –making use of an expression of Fr. Cornelio Fabro– calls “essential Thomism.” In such a way that, well imbued with the doctrine of the Angelic Doctor, we may be capable of thinking from him; and, “from him, enter into dialogue and debate with contemporary problems and thinkers” –as our specific end asks of us and our Proper Law commands us–. Only thus will we be able to discern what corresponds to the Gospel, in order to accept it, and what does not, in order to reject it. This obviously entails an active judgment on human thought and on Thomism itself in relation to modern thought20.

That is why today we are asked that, taking into account the philosophical research of modern times, especially that which most influences the life of nations and the progress of the sciences, we come to know well the character of the age in which we have to live, so as to be prepared for dialogue with our contemporaries21. And in this way to seek the solution of human problems in the light of Revelation; and to know how to apply the eternal truths to the changing condition of human affairs, and of course, to communicate them in an appropriate manner to the souls entrusted to us22.

That is why today, as we begin this new academic year, in this place that witnessed the passing to eternal life of him who made “a full submission of mind and heart to divine revelation”23 (as our St. John Paul II says), I would like to quote here, by way of conclusion, the wise counsel set out in our Directory of Intellectual Formation: “Without the discipline and habit of study, the future priest will not be able to be the wise man according to the Gospel who, in season and out of season, exhorts with the word of God, convinces with the truth and frees from error. The priest is called to be a teacher of the Christian faith and must therefore be capable of giving a reason for the faith he preaches and teaches. Dedication to study must be undertaken with this pastoral perspective”24, and the same can be applied to women religious.

That is why I told you at the beginning that many aspects of all that we do in the future depend –with the help of God's grace– on the seriousness, solidity and depth of our formation.

The philosophy of St. Thomas is the philosophy of being, that is, of the ‘actus essendi’, whose transcendental value is the most direct path for rising to the knowledge of subsistent Being and pure Act, which is God. And only by rediscovering in its fullness being and the first principles of being and of thought can man validly ascend to Subsistent Being itself, his beginning and end, and the supreme guarantor of his inalienable freedom.25 Let us remember that each one of us is a disciple of Him who one day presented himself to the disciples saying I am26. So we too must be priests, religious and missionaries of ‘being.’

This implies: “being pioneers, ready to take the first steps for Christ, to commit oneself totally to God, trusting in his Word, in his power and in his mercy. It also implies being men of transcendence, not confined or trapped in the temporal sphere of the principle of immanence, but rather knowing how to rise up through created things so as to reach also beyond the limited horizons of this world”27. It implies launching out –with a genuinely catholic spirit– to help with the needs of the whole Church, with a spirit ready to kindle the light of the Gospel everywhere.28

[Peroration] We can, then, on this day, implore of the Mother of the Incarnate Word, through the intercession of that giant who was St. Thomas Aquinas, the same graces that Archbishop Fulton Sheen asked for on the eve of his priestly ordination, and which were two: “I begged that these two graces be granted me –he writes in his autobiography–: the first, to know very well what the thinking of the modern world is; and the second, to know how to refute the errors of modern thought in the light of the philosophy of St. Thomas Aquinas”29

May the Mother of God, who is Teacher by the holiness of her example, educate us with maternal wisdom to conform our lives to that of the Incarnate Word. Amen.

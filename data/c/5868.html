Without enemies1

You say you have no enemies?

What a pity, my friend! That is a poor thing to boast of.

Whoever has joined in the struggle of duty,

which the valiant endure,

is bound to have made enemies!

If you have none at all,

the work you have done is slight.

You have struck no traitor from behind,

you have snatched the cup from no perjured lips,

you have never changed evil into good,

you have been a coward in the struggle!

It is said that Margaret Thatcher kept this poem on her desk. This detail of the former British prime minister’s life recently came to light when, in a contemporary film series, she recites this poem to Queen Elizabeth II after the latter expressed her concern that the government was making enemies for itself with the reforms it was pushing forward.

Beyond the media popularity the poem enjoys today because of internet television, and beyond the prime minister who recited it, the verses express a truth, namely the fact that the mediocre, preferring to ride the waves of popularity and to spare themselves every kind of inconvenience, choose to negotiate with the spirit of the world and its henchmen. The valiant, on the other hand, that is, those who have dirtied their hands in the struggle of just duty, are the ones who reap enemies: “If you have none at all, the work you have done is slight.”

That is why being accepted just like that, without any opposition, is often a sign of mediocrity (or of cowardice, in the poet’s language), whereas being persecuted is a compliment. It is in this sense that the Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen said that our “persecutors pay us the beautiful tribute of hostility; they pay us the fine compliment of opposition”2. It is the mystery of persecution. A mystery which, when that persecution is unjust or is for Christ, involves one of the Gospel beatitudes.

1. Blessed are those persecuted for the sake of justice

That is why one day the adorable lips of our Lord pronounced these profound words: Blessed are those who are persecuted for the sake of justice, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven3.

“For Matthew, for his readers and hearers, the expression those persecuted for the sake of justice had a prophetic meaning. For them it was an advance allusion the Lord had made to the situation of the Church in which they were living. It had become a persecuted Church, persecuted for the sake of justice. In the language of the Old Testament, ‘justice’ expresses fidelity to the Torah, fidelity to the word of God, as the prophets had always demanded. It is a matter of persevering on the straight path indicated by God, whose core is formed by the Ten Commandments. In the New Testament, the concept equivalent to that of justice in the Old Testament is that of faith: the believer is the ‘just man,’ the one who follows the ways of God4. For faith is walking with Christ, in whom the whole Law is fulfilled; it unites us to the justice of Christ himself”5.

These persecuted ones of whom Christ speaks, explains Pope Benedict XVI, “are those who live by the justice of God, by faith. Since man’s aspiration always tends to emancipate itself from the will of God and to follow only itself, faith will always appear as something that stands opposed to the ‘world’ –to the dominant powers of each moment– and that is why there will be persecution for the sake of justice in every period of history. […] In her lack of power and in her suffering, the Church is aware that she finds herself where the Kingdom of God is arriving”6. That is why we who follow Christ will never be popular nor, so to speak, comfortable, since nonconformity with evil is a form of resistance that the world cannot bear, because the spirit of the world demands collaborationism7; it asks you to work hand in hand with it, in a sort of “single way of thinking.” And this happens even to religious and churchmen when they become worldly, for these, rather than suffering persecution, are normally the ones who carry it out. It happened to Jesus!

Let us suppose for a moment that our Religious Family no longer had any external difficulty, any persecution. That would certainly be very pleasant, but it could at the same time be a sign that our salt has lost its savor and our flame has gone out. For this reason, as our Proper Law very well says, “the greatest grace that God can grant to our tiny Religious Family [is] that of persecution”8.

We must realize that “the world, which so dislikes zeal for the kingdom of God, hated Christ first. It was his zeal that led Him to the Cross. The world loves the indifferent, the mediocre, the ordinary, the good-natured, but it hates two kinds of people: those who are truly good and those who are truly bad. […] Some go to the cross because they are very good in contrast with the majority or with the system; others go to the cross because they are very bad.

And since the world hates in the same way in which it hated Christ, it hates the one who is zealous for the cause of Christ because he reproaches the world for its mediocrity; but it also hates the wicked, as it hated the thieves crucified on either side of Christ, because they were a nuisance to its self-complacency.

Do you see? The good go to death because they are good; the wicked go to death because they are wicked; the mediocre survive. The mediocre are those who “settle in,” who try to occupy posts for which they are not fit, who covet and abuse power and unscrupulously wield it against those who want to do things well. In the words of our Lord: The world cannot hate you; but Me it hates, because I testify against it that its works are evil9. And if this was true of our Lord, it must also be true of us. The servant is not greater than his Master”10.

That is why those who slander us or attack us unjustly do nothing but confess that they take our purpose seriously and that –if evil wants to triumph– they must eliminate the cause we defend. The same thing happened to Jesus with his worldly contemporaries; here lies the theological foundation of persecution: If the world hates you, you know that it hated Me before you. If you were of the world, the world would love its own; but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you. Remember the word that I said to you: ‘A servant is not greater than his master’… But all this they will do to you on account of my name, because they do not know the One who sent Me. If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin; but now they have no excuse for their sin11.

That is why persecution is the law of the Church –as Fr. Castellani rightly pointed out–; it is the burden we must carry, and today we must look it in the face.

Throughout history persecution of the good has always been present; not that all Christians have been persecuted, certainly. Not, for example, Christians in name only, who not only do not suffer but at times even serve the ungodly as useful idiots. For as St. Paul said: all who want to live piously in Christ Jesus will be persecuted12. He did not say “all the baptized”; he said: all who want to live piously13.

Just by way of illustration, let us mention the martyrdom of the Apostles, who were martyred in different parts of the world, followed by the ten bloody persecutions in Rome, in which thousands of Christians of every condition were put to death, almost always with refined and terrible tortures. Also the countless persecutions that still today assail the good. To the point that at times it seems that “Jesus here on earth grants us as Christians only one right: the right to persecution and to the curses of men”14.

But internal persecution also exists. To go no further, as we were saying, it is the one Christ himself suffered at the hands of Judas. That is why He warned us: And a man’s enemies will be those of his own household15, a stealthy and treacherous persecution of which St. Paul himself complained when he said that he went through dangers among false brethren16. And very probably that is the worst kind of persecution, because it is full of hypocrisy and not infrequently of cruelty. St. Peter Julian Eymard expressed it thus: “There is above all one kind of pain that makes one suffer, and that is the persecutions and slanders of [supposedly] devout persons. Nothing wounds so deeply, because their [supposed] virtue gives grounds for believing that they must be right and that it is God himself who is angry. He permits that at times the [apparently] best do not look kindly on one and persecute him, despite his innocence, in order to purify him better”17. And, therefore, it is nonetheless a grace.

If in our eagerness to remain faithful to the will of God expressed in our Constitutions18 unfounded attacks come upon us, blessed be God! Because “this Cross prepares for us an eternal weight of incalculable glory”19. Sometimes we complain because our persecutors or “false brethren” harass us with false accusations, in the same way that they accused Christ, calling Him a glutton and a drunkard20. Sometimes we protest because some people question everything we do, not because they want an answer, but precisely because they do not want one; but careful! not without first telling us that they want to hear our opinion, that they are interested in hearing our view. In the face of all these machinations, deep down we must know how to say: blessed be God!

If we did not pray, if we had a lax formation, if we had a light spirituality instead of a serious (though not “rigid”) spirituality, if we had no vocations, if we did not defend the charism of the Institute tooth and nail, if we were subservient, they would never harass us. They would leave us completely in peace.

In any case, one may validly ask: why so much opposition? To which the Venerable Fulton Sheen answers: “They ridicule you and harass you because you have something they do not have, and because your faith is a reproach to their wickedness. In the depths of their souls they would like to have your peace; they would like to have your certainty instead of their doubts; your joy instead of their fears, your trust in God instead of their confusion. They envy you and what you have, but they want all of that at their price, not at Christ’s price. In a word, they want your faith, but without the Cross”21. To the point that without meaning to they seem to fulfill the psalm that says: they repay me evil for good because I strive for what is good22.

“In former times a person’s character was known by the people who loved him; nowadays character is better known by those who hate him”23, Fulton Sheen affirmed. Thus, having enemies in some way declares to the world that the one persecuted for the sake of justice possesses a princely spirit, for such men “are those who know at every instant the things for which one must die”24, “they do not ask for freedom but for hierarchy”25 and “they feel honor as they feel life”26. And since all these values clash with the spirit of the world, the world seeks to eliminate them; that is why they seek to divide and scatter us.

Now, we would not be realistic if we said that the attacks cause us no grief. But we must be convinced in faith that God draws good out of evil and therefore that the most adverse events contribute to our good. They will serve, with God’s favor, for personal growth and the growth of the Institute, and as badges of the good fight.

Let us hear the advice that St. Peter Julian Eymard gave to some religious sisters: “God permits the saints to be slandered, despised and persecuted. And since the Lord was too, it is an honor for you to be so”27.

Thus, for example, St. John of the Cross was envied by Diego Evangelista, who had once felt humiliated by a correction from the saint. It was to a great extent this resentful friar who set in motion the last great persecution suffered by the Mystic of Fontiveros. St. Mary Mackillop was excommunicated by the bishop after being accused of insubordination; and so many, many, many other examples we could cite. Fulton Sheen himself, who had millions of followers on television, who had converted thousands of souls, under whose leadership the office of Propaganda Fide raised great sums for the missions, was falsely accused of embezzling funds. Another example: Blessed Marie Anne Blondin, the foundress and superior general of the Sisters of St. Anne, was greatly persecuted by the chaplain assigned to the community, who, exercising dictatorial control over it, had elections called for a new superior, not without first warning Marie Anne not to accept any position of authority even if she were elected by the sisters. She spent the rest of her days doing domestic work; she did not even appear on the lists of the sisters. And St. Joseph Calasanz. And St. Josemaría Escrivá de Balaguer. And St. Pio of Pietrelcina… The list is endless. That was the road the saints followed, strewn with stones and intricate accusations, in constant conflict with their enemies, who breathed cruelty upon them; but all of this served them to purify their faith and unite themselves more to God. With that attitude of noble gallantry we must not only accept but face the tribulations that God is pleased to send us, or at least permits.

Disloyal gossip, slander, false judgments: all are born, in one way or another, of envy. In this sense, the venerable American archbishop points out: “every word born of envy is based on a false judgment about our own moral superiority. Sitting in judgment makes us feel as though we are above those being judged and that we are more just and more innocent than they”28. That is why accusing others is a way of saying “I am not like them,” or like saying “they have robbed me of what is mine.” In this way “envy becomes a total denial of justice and of love. And so a cynicism develops in individuals that destroys all moral values, because to bring others to ruin (stripping them of all good reputation, for example) is ultimately to bring oneself to ruin”29. There are churchmen who envy someone for having many vocations, and so they speak ill, they criticize… And one may well ask whether this is not precisely what they lack.

In other words: those who speak ill of others with slander often, in the end, speak ill of themselves. When envy takes root in a group of people it produces such a deception and such a lie that it makes the group extend a welcoming hand to anyone who dissents, for example from a religious congregation –regardless of his record, as long as he joins the attack– until he becomes powerful enough and, therefore, bothersome; then they expel him because he no longer serves their cause. We have seen it many times!

Fulton Sheen therefore advises that “since envy is so rampant in today’s world, it is extremely good advice not to believe 99% of the twisted assertions we hear about others. Think how much the good thief had to discount from what the others were saying in order to arrive at the truth. He had to distrust the judgment of four envious judges, set aside the mockery of the elders and scribes of the temple, turn a deaf ear to the blasphemous slanders of the curious onlookers, and dismiss the envious mockery of the bad thief, who was willing to lose his soul in exchange for keeping his nimble fingers to commit more thefts.

Realize that it is very likely that there is a bit of jealousy, a bit of envy behind every sharp comment and every stinging whisper we hear our neighbor say. And for those who are unjustly attacked, let it be a consolation to know that it is physically impossible for someone to get ahead of us if he is always behind us kicking us”30.

2. Christ had enemies

Christ had enemies. The Sadducees and the Pharisees, Judas and the Sanhedrin, Rome and the priests of the Temple, Pilate and Herod –all these, who were in a certain enmity among themselves– united in great hostility to confront Christ. Let us speak of the Pharisees.

Fr. Castellani says that the whole biography of Jesus as man can be summed up in this formula: “He was the Messiah and He fought against the Pharisees”31. Without the Pharisees the story of Christ would not be the same, just as the history of the Church would not be the same.

Pharisaism is religious pride: it is the subtlest and most dangerous corruption of the greatest truth, the truth that religious values come first. But the moment we claim them for ourselves, we lose them; the moment we make our own what is God’s, it ceases to belong to anyone, if it does not become the property of the devil.

The Pharisee is the man of practice and of will. It is true that the Pharisee is not like other men; only he is not better, as he believes, but worse. Christ called them hypocrites32, for they are those who say, but do not do33. We could say with St. Paul that the Pharisees, enemies of Jesus, are those who, boasting of being wise, became fools34.

Hypocrite, in itself, means being an actor; but whoever continually behaves as an actor runs the risk of becoming a fraud, for concern with appearances goes hand in hand with unconcern for what one really is. That attitude ends up leading to blindness and absurdity. They deceive others, beginning by deceiving themselves. And in their blindness they can even go so far as to speak out against and deplore Pharisaism itself, deceiving still more those who lend them an ear. According to Fr. Castellani, Pharisaism “is a radically irreligious, and even antireligious, attitude that appears religious”35. Because hypocrites live on appearances, they know how to put on a holy-card face, they make themselves look just36, they make themselves up as good37, they repeat to you ad nauseam that they want to help you, they present themselves with a ‘savior’ attitude and, as if they were masters of all contingencies, they assure you a future full of sunshine if you agree with them and join them… only, mind you, until they come out ‘apparently’ victorious, because when you are no longer useful to them they discard you. To them apply the words of the psalm that says they are workers of iniquity, who speak peace to their neighbor and plot evil in their heart38.

To achieve his aim the Pharisee uses the hypocrite’s technique39: he flatters you, makes you believe he loves you, deceives you with his ‘pious ways’ of proceeding, but deep down acts with bad intention, since he hides a secret ambition. Several centuries ago St. John of the Cross already noted this way of proceeding when he said: “The Calced are touched by the vice of ambition, and so everything they do they color and tint with goodness, so that they are incorrigible…”, about which Fr. Castellani comments: “Ambition in religious, which at times becomes for them a stronger passion than lust in lay people, is one of the finest parts of Pharisaism: To love the first places… to love the vain honor that men give”40. It becomes for them “a way of life”41.

That is why Pharisaism is a hundred times worse than any other vice. For Pharisaism is a spiritual vice, that is, it is diabolical, since corruptions of the spirit are worse than corruptions of the flesh.

Thus Christ said to St. Catherine of Siena: “That is why I assure you that if all the other sins they have committed were placed on one side and this one alone on the other, this one would weigh more than the others”42. For Pharisaism is like a compendium of all the spiritual vices: avarice, ambition, vainglory, pride, obstinacy, hardness of heart, cruelty, which has come to empty out diabolically from within the three theological virtues, thus constituting the sin against the Holy Spirit. […] But the flower of Pharisaism is cruelty: stealthy, cautious, slow, prudent and subterranean cruelty, putting to death while believing one is offering service to God. Everything is done in silence, in the dark, by means of cover-ups and complicated schemes, behind a curtain of feigned goodness and evangelical spirit. But “hypocrisy destroys, hypocrisy kills; it kills persons, it even tears out a person’s personality and soul. It kills communities. When there are hypocrites [‘false brethren’] in a community there is a great danger there, there is a very ugly danger”43. It can even go so far as to decide on “the illegal, cruel and iniquitous death of a man,” Fr. Castellani points out, which “is resolved in meetings where the Law is invoked with the texts in hand, in grave religious conclaves, dialogues, phrases in which almost nothing speaks but Sacred Scripture and the most sacred words that exist on earth are used. Truly I tell you that, if a dead man raised to life came to testify, you would not believe him44”45.

And since for the Pharisee anything goes, “all means are good as long as they are stealthy: slander, bribery, fraud, distortion, false testimony, threats”46, dropping into their speeches the names of great personalities, as one who boasts of the backing he receives from them and of the impunity they believe they enjoy. “Caiaphas killed Christ with a summary of the prophecy of Isaiah and with the dogma of the Redemption. Is it not expedient that one man should die for the welfare of a whole people?47.

If we look closely at the case of Christ, for example, He was judged a heretic for breaking the Sabbath, and therefore his fate was sealed. Afterward there would come in succession, as anger and envy at his successes grew, the labels of madman, magician, possessed by the devil, and then blasphemer, seditious, and finally conspirator against Caesar. As we said above: anything goes. It is an accusation that keeps growing as time passes, without the accused being asked for a defense or explanations; on the contrary, every defense he gives becomes a new charge. The process is secret. When the judges intervene in public, it is no longer an accusation but a sentence. They make slanderous assertions and try to catch the accused in a slip in order to make the slander stick48. That is why the Pharisees secretly rejoice in the slow fermentation of slander among the people.

That was the drama of Christ. Thus the Savior died. All his meekness, all his most adorable sweetness, his benefits, his tears, his Precious Blood, his warnings, his prophetic threats were to shatter against the hardness of heart of the Pharisees. That continues to be the drama of his Church.

Christ himself complained to St. Catherine of Siena about the wounds that this movement of internal persecution in the Church caused his Mystical Body: “I told you that they wounded Me, and it is the truth. Inwardly they persecute Me as they can. Not that I in Myself can receive any injury or be wounded by them, but they act like a stone which, when thrown, is not received but turns back against the one who throws it. Thus those wounded by their offenses, those who cast forth the pestilence, cannot harm Me; rather the poisoned arrow of guilt turns back against them. It deprives them of grace in this life, as they lose the fruit of the blood, and at the last moment, if they do not amend with holy confession and contrition of heart, they come to eternal damnation, separated from Me and bound to the devil.

[…] By this bond, the persecutors of the blood find themselves united with others, and, as members bound to the devil, they have taken up the office of devils. […] This bond is knotted with pride and one’s own reputation, with servile fear, for they lose grace out of fear of losing their positions. These bonds bear the seal of darkness, because they know into how many evils and miseries they have fallen and make others fall.

[…] O dearest daughter! Grieve infinitely to see such blindness and misery in those who, like you, have been washed in the blood and were nourished and grew up on that blood at the breasts of holy Church. Now, like rebels, out of fear and under the pretext of correcting the vices of my ministers –whom I have forbidden anyone to touch– they have turned away from these breasts. […] The worst of it is that, under the cloak of the defects of my ministers, they want to shelter and cover up their own, and they do not consider that there is no cloak that can keep Me from seeing them. They can hide from the eyes of creatures, but not from Me, from whom neither present things nor anything else is hidden.

One of the reasons why the wretched men of the world do not amend is that in reality they do not believe with a living faith that I see them; for if they truly believed that I see their vices and that every vice is punished, just as every good work is rewarded, they would not do so much evil, but would correct themselves of what they have done and humbly implore my mercy. […] No persecution must be carried out on account of any sin seen in the ministers of the blood”49.

It is clear that Pharisaism is not a thing of the past; we find it also in our own days, because it is a specific vice and a grave disease of the true religion50. “The chair of Moses is still the chair of Moses. We must do what they say without doing what they do; and say a number of things that they keep silent about, which must be said, and which will make them leap like vipers: bear witness to the truth”51.

“If over the course of the centuries an enormous mass of sufferings, and even of blood, had not been rendered by other christs in resisting the Pharisee, the Church would not exist today. Pharisaism is the greatest evil that exists on earth”52. And today, far more than yesterday, “we need someone to remind these modern Pilates that there is another Power up above”53.

3. What is to be done?

We do not intend here to give a recipe for a solution. But one thing is evidently clear: we must not be Pharisees. Lest it happen to us as it did to those who were on Calvary, but on the wrong side. They devoted a great part of their time and energy to the study and observance of the Law and, nevertheless, they missed the Messiah. We must be persons of integrity, free, not subservient54, and conduct ourselves through this life, in whatever circumstances Providence disposes, always with a princely spirit55. These are times of intense struggle; that is why we must have our priorities in order and remain loyal even if we are wounded in battle. These are magnificent times to be faithful to the Incarnate Word, to his Church and to the Institute in which we have consecrated our life both to the Lord and to the Church, which is to say the same thing.

It must be noted that if ever there was an innocent person who had the right to protest against an injustice, it was our Lord. And yet He forgave. He ignored the insults to his Person. Did not the Incarnate Word preach meekness? Must we not practice it?

Certainly, to see ourselves attacked, when it is unjust, grieves us. Certainly, to recognize our enemies in those very people who should be our friends causes pain and brings a great feeling of abandonment. Of course, to see some of the ‘brothers’ secretly ally themselves to fight against their own Family is unpleasant, fills one with helplessness, and wounds deeply. But at the same time, the lesson is clear: pain does not make us better56; it makes us fit to bear even worse things. Who has not experienced in moments of intense suffering the feeling that we cannot hold out one more minute? But that minute passes and we realize that we are touching new layers of endurance. Nevertheless, pain never becomes pleasant. But we must keep going forward, with our gaze fixed on heaven, where the Reward with a capital R awaits us. Everything depends on whether we unite our pain to Him who, for the joy set before Him, endured the Cross, disregarding its shame57.

And ultimately we must rejoice if they persecute us. Because if our faith must be “perfected by persecutions”58, these will only serve to wound our pride, but not our mettle; they will cauterize our arrogance, but will not stain our soul, since the very insult on the part of the world is the consecration of our goodness59.

We know that praying for those who nail us to the Cross is not of this world. But that is precisely the point: our faith is not worldly; our faith turns the values of the world completely upside down.

We know that loving our enemies is not according to “common sense,” because loving our enemies would mean hating ourselves (according to what the world thinks); but that is the intent of Christianity. Very often our executioners are our allies; those who crucify us are our greatest benefactors.

Thus St. Thomas More wrote in the margin of his breviary when he was imprisoned in the Tower of London: “Grant me the grace, good Lord… to fix my mind firmly on You and not to weigh the words that come out of the mouths of men; to be content to be alone… to be glad in tribulations… to consider that my enemies are my best friends, because Joseph’s brothers could not have benefited him as much by their love and goodwill as they did by their malice and hatred”60.

It is the attitude of thoroughgoing Christians of all times, who with holy pride died at the hands of their enemies for the glory of God and of his Church.

Let us think, for example, of the martyr Tomás de la Mora, killed on August 15, 1927 in the city of Colima, Mexico, a victim of the persecution unleashed by President Plutarco Calles. Tomás, who had been a seminarian, wrote a year earlier: “We must no longer ask God to stop the persecution, but that in every Catholic there may be a hero as in the times of Nero.” After being arrested for the sole crime of being a Christian, and having learned his sentence, he asked that it be carried out immediately: “The delay bothers me,” he said. On seeing the tree where Benito Juárez had once rested61, he asked the head of the escort to be hanged there: “This,” he said, “is a place of ignominy; hang me here so that this place of curse may be changed into a blessing.” With a cry of “Viva Cristo Rey!” he was hoisted up at midnight on a Saturday. He was only 18 years old.

To have this fortitude and strength of soul we must at the same time pray, and pray much, asking to remain faithful, keeping our conscience intact and persevering in the holy vocation God has granted us. And pray also for our Institute, as St. Peter Julian, for example, prayed for the Blessed Sacrament Fathers: “Keep away from your Eucharistic family false, deceitful and impure vocations; never allow this poor and humble family to fall into the hands of a proud man, an ambitious man, or any hard and wrathful man. Do not hand over to unclean and perverse beasts the souls that confess You and hope in You. Preserve your Eucharistic family from all scandal, keep it virgin of every vice, free from all worldly servitude, a stranger to the world, so that it may place all its joy in serving You holily and freely, in peace and tranquility”62.

And when the struggle grows even fiercer we can put into practice the advice St. John of Ávila gave to a lady: “It is not fitting, madam, to lose heart because of the greatness of the enemies, nor because of their cunning, nor because of the torments they inflict; for you will be all the more acceptable to your Lord the more persevering you are in greater torments for Him. We must stay on the Cross until we give up our spirit to the Father; and while alive we must not come down from it, however much scholars and Pharisees tell us to come down and that profit will follow from coming down, as they said to the Lord63. The Cross was taken up for Him, and He has helped you carry it until now; and if at times it is so heavy that it makes you fall to your knees, so it did to our Lord too; and He will not be surprised that our weakness should kneel, since his great strength knelt; which He willed to do so that the weak might not lose heart when, under the weight of their labors, it sometimes seems to them that, being unable to bear so much, they are bogged down in sadness and as if with some lack of confidence, and without the joy in suffering that they had at other times. The Lord well knows our clay, well knows our stain, for we carry it written on our forehead before Him; He is not surprised at our weaknesses, and He loves our humble confession of our fault more than our conceit about our righteousness64.

Moreover, we must “pray much for the Church of Jesus Christ, so tried and so persecuted, that God may defend her from her enemies, who at the same time are her own children, and that He may touch their hearts, convert them and lead them, humble and penitent, to the foot of the throne of mercy and justice”65.

And when the hours grow dark and the file of abandonment makes itself felt even more strongly, let us make room for confidence. Every act of confidence implies that one cannot see. If we saw, if we had certainty, there would be no room for confidence. Now, to trust in God means to cling to the truth that his designs are good and holy, not because we see it clearly, but in spite of all appearances to the contrary66. It is the practice of the theological virtue of hope, which looks to the omnipotence, to the infinite goodness of God, to his providence! This attitude of soul is fundamental in Christian life and is part of that non-negotiable element attached to the charism which must characterize us and which we call ‘having a providential view of life.’ All tribulations, down to their smallest details, are woven into the fabric of that mantle of holiness which God wishes to grant to us who believe in Him. Let us be “ready to sacrifice everything without reserve, persuaded that nothing is so advantageous as to abandon oneself into the hands of God in all that He is pleased to ordain”67. And, finally, let us give thanks to God, who has found us worthy to suffer something for his Kingdom.

In this month in which we celebrate the Solemnity of the Sacred Heart of Jesus, let us nestle in our soul the thought that “the Heart of Jesus guards us… to defend us from our enemies, like a mother who, to save her child from a danger, clasps him to her heart, so that the child cannot be wounded without the mother also being struck”68.

May the Virgin Mother shelter us all, united, under her mantle and lead us to a safe harbor. She is our hope, because she is the Mother of the One who is Hope.

House of Formation of Our Lady of Sheshan – Philippines

Beginning of the 2017 academic year

[Exordium] Dear Fathers, seminarians and novices,

First of all, I would like to say that I am very happy to be here. It is the first time I have come to the Seminary (at least in this place; I visited it in the beginning, when you were in another place) and it is certainly a joy to meet all of you. I must say that it is a joy for me to see that the call of Christ is bearing fruit in your hearts, and I give thanks to the Lord and to Our Lady for the gift of the vocation of each one of you to our Institute. I also want to thank Fr. Diego for inviting me to preside at the Mass and preach on this occasion of the beginning of the academic year.

The beginning of a new academic year in the seminary –and in reality, in any of our houses of formation– means another opportunity to “configure ourselves to Christ.” Because that is what every seminarian and every member of the Institute of the Incarnate Word is called to be –even from the Novitiate–: “to imitate Jesus Christ as perfectly as possible”1; to put on Christ2, configuring oneself to Him3, as we read in our Directory of Spirituality.

And if we are called to be “other Christs” –as we proudly confess in our Constitutions– we must imitate our Lord, who came to die so that others –and that includes you and me– might have life.

Seminarians often say: “I am studying to be a priest.” But how many times does a seminarian say, or even think: “I am studying to be a priest and a victim”?… and yet, that is what we do. Because, if we are called to imitate the Incarnate Word, that is precisely what we seek to imitate: Him who was Sacerdos-Victima. He united in Himself the priesthood and the condition of victim, and we cannot conceive of our priesthood without becoming victims in pursuit of our specific end: the prolongation of His Incarnation.

Our Directory of Spirituality, speaking of the earthly life of our Savior, goes on to say: “He offered himself without restriction as a victim to the Father; he sacrificed himself in expiation; he gave himself to the Father without reserve”4. And it continues: “Such must be the priestly attitude of all the members of our little Religious Family”5.

Hence the beginning of this new academic year means a new opportunity to configure ourselves to the Incarnate Word as Priest-Victim.

That is why, in this homily, I would like to speak to you –even if only briefly– about what we mean when we say that the Incarnate Word is Priest-Victim and about how we must imitate Him.

1. First, the Incarnate Word is Priest-Victim

The texts of our Constitutions and Directories contain much material on this subject, which I encourage you to read.

Nevertheless, let us begin by saying that our Lord differs from all the other priests –pagan and Jewish– of the Old Testament. All the other priests offered a victim distinct from themselves; for example, a goat, a lamb, an ox; but Christ offered Himself as the victim. He offered Himself without blemish to God, a spiritual and eternal sacrifice 6, writes St. Paul. All others who have come into this world came to live; He came to die. A sacrificial death was the goal of his life, the gold he was seeking: I have a baptism with which I must be baptized, and how distressed I am until it is accomplished! 7.

Now, pay attention to this: our Lord was always Priest and Victim, from his Incarnation. Not only a priest, nor only a victim. Christ was numbered among sinners because he was the Victim for their sins. But he was separated from sinners because he was a sinless priest. He ate and mingled with sinners, he shared their nature and looked upon their sins. But he was separated from them by his innocence.

“Jesus was at the same time priest and victim of his own sacrifice”8. He is at once the Offerer and the Offering; the Priest and the Victim. And although the Incarnate Word did not speak of this profound secret of the Suffering Servant to the crowds, he reserved it for his disciples and future priests, whom he called Friends9. To them alone he unveiled the prophecy of Isaiah 53, and to them alone he interpreted his death as a ransom for sinners10. And he wants to entrust the same to you, through the moments of prayer and in the various aspects of formation.

As Priest he was sinless, and Sacred Scripture has more than enough material to prove it. The Archangel Gabriel proclaims it at the Annunciation: The angel answered: “The Holy Spirit will come upon you and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the one to be born will be holy and will be called Son of God”11. He himself said it when he asked the Jews: “Which of you can prove that I am a sinner?”12 And later he said to his apostles: “I will no longer speak much with you, for the Prince of this world is coming. He has no power over me 13. Even the devil confessed it: “What have we to do with you, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are: the Holy One of God.” 14

As Victim he identified himself with sinners: “Him who knew no sin he made sin for us, so that we might become the justice of God in him.”15. As Priest, he was holy with the Holiness of God; as Victim, he was made sin. As Priest he was “set apart” from the world; as Victim he entered into it to fight against the Devil, the Prince of the world.

Look at the beautiful Crucifix you have here: on the Cross, he stood upright as Priest; on the Cross, he lay prostrate as Victim. As Priest, he interceded for us before the Father; as Victim, he mediated for the sins of men. Before Pilate, he spoke seven times as Priest-Shepherd; before Pilate, he was silent seven times as Victim-Lamb. As Priest he had dignity; as Victim he suffered indignity. As Priest he begged the Father to let the Chalice pass; as Victim he drank it to the dregs. When his ministry reached its culminating moment, he insisted more and more that victory over the principalities and powers had to come through his sacrifice and his death. And although he was personally free from sin, he was considered guilty. Christ was innocent of sin, says St. Paul, and yet “Him who knew no sin he made sin for us, so that we might become the justice of God in Him.”16.

He died in our place because he loved us and, in doing so, “reconciled the world to himself, not counting men’s transgressions against them, but entrusting to us the word of reconciliation”17. “What [Christ] is by nature we are by communication; He is so in fullness, we are so by participation; He is the Son of God by generation, we are so by adoption.” 18

Here is the key to the victimal priesthood of Christ and of all of us who are called to be priests. As priests, we must be holy. It is not that by our priesthood we are bound to the world; by the priesthood we are bound to God the Father! Yet as victims, we take the place of sinners, assuming their guilt and their poverty and interceding for them.

Thus, just as Christ is a Priest-Victim, we –who aspire to be “other Christs”– must follow the same path. Our seminarians and novices are preparing not only to be preachers but also “sin-bearers,” not only to be offerers but also to be offered, not only to attend to the needs of men as social workers do, but also as redeemers. And as the Capitular Fathers remind us: “If we do not form religious who are deeply convinced of this, we will not form religious of the Incarnate Word.” 19

Being victims means feeling the guilt and sin of the world as if they were one’s own, and, through constant union with Christ, seeking to reconcile all humanity with Him.

2. So now the obvious question arises: How do we prepare ourselves to be priest-victims?

a. In the profession of our vows we can clearly see this aspect of victimhood of which we have been speaking, as we make ourselves voluntary victims in order “to accept death to this world so as to be united totally to Christ and to share in his kingdom”20. Indeed, our “religious profession constitutes a true holocaust of oneself, since we offer ourselves and all our goods totally and without reserve to God”21.

Notice that we have said: totally and without reserve. We, like Christ, freely enter religious life in order to die to ourselves, completely. And like Christ, who was always priest and victim, we do not want to turn back. Our self-giving is forever, that is, to the very end. St. John Paul II wrote: “Those who are preparing for the priesthood must realize that their whole priestly life will have value to the extent that they are able to give themselves to Christ and, through Christ, to the Father”22. And this brings me to the second element that helps us prepare ourselves to be priest-victims.

b. Because the profession of our vows demands of us: fidelity.

Fidelity means, of course, that we are faithful to the nature and the demands of our call, that we live a life of integrity flowing from the interior conviction of who we are in the eyes of God, that we fully carry out the duties associated with our vocation, that we are men of our word, who can be counted on to do what we say and to live as we profess.

Fidelity is the essential thing in the whole life of a man who desires to be, or who already is, configured to Christ in the depths of his being through the sacrament of orders.

Our houses of formation must be that “family atmosphere” where our religious are formed in this virtue: fidelity. Fidelity to the commitment to Christ and his Church through the three vows, fidelity to one’s own vocation, fidelity to the command of Christ: Go into the whole world and preach the Gospel to every creature 23. And as our Directory points out, “a true religious is faithful and shows great love not only for his religious life, but also for his own Institute, which includes in a very particular way its Founder and its Constitutions.” Then, quoting Vita Consecrata, it says: “fidelity to the founding charism and to the subsequent spiritual heritage of each Institute is necessary. It is precisely in this fidelity to the inspiration of the founders, an inspiration which is itself a gift of the Holy Spirit, that the essential elements of consecrated life can be more readily discerned and put into practice with greater fervor.” 24

Why? Because it is our “charism that will lead us to belong entirely to God, to speak with God or about God in every situation. Because it will also foster an intimate and joyful communion of life with the Incarnate Word, in the school of his generous service to God and neighbor. And so we will be conformed to Christ progressively” 25.

Learn to be faithful. Make it a habit. So that later in life, when the “external structures” of seminary life are removed, you will persevere in your fidelity. Fidelity to your life of prayer, where the Lord himself will whisper in the depths of your souls what it means to be a victim. Fidelity to your studies, to your apostolates, to the duties assigned to you, to the timetable, to the traditions of our Institute… Not like robots, not only outwardly, but deeply convinced that this is God’s will for you. Be faithful even if you do not have the best means, or in the face of exterior difficulties and interior trials, because that is when you best show that you are authentic religious of the Incarnate Word, that is, when you “work in utmost docility to the Holy Spirit and following the example of the Virgin Mary so that Jesus Christ may be Lord, even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”, 26as our charism says.

Fidelity in action springs from a trusting, grateful and humble awareness of our vocation. Be faithful to Christ who has called you. In the end, everything else is spiritual commentary.

And not only be faithful—be joyfully faithful! That is –as you know– one of the non-negotiable points of our charism: joy! It is not that you must force yourselves to fake laughter at every moment; that would be ridiculous. But rejoice in the fact that, even in difficult situations, God helps you to do what is best for you. God chose suffering to redeem us. So, if suffering is “borne with patience, accepted as coming from God, and sanctified by uniting it to the pain of Christ”27, you will save countless souls.

c. Third, the So that “you may grasp the ‘style’ of our Lord Jesus Christ, that is, his attitudes and sentiments. [Discipline is the fundamental attitude of the disciple. It is a submission to the rules of life so that the truth may become flesh in the life of the disciple. For us, the truth is Christ, and to be docile to discipline means to let Him be our Lord”28. Always keep this in mind.

d. Fourth, study. We cannot love what we do not know. That is why, if we do not study, if we do not read Scripture, if we do not immerse ourselves in the study of the teachings of the Church, how can we give reasons for our faith to others? For it must not happen that someone becomes “an empty preacher of the word of God to others, without hearing the word of God in his own heart”29, says John Paul II, quoting St. Augustine.

e. Finally, perseverance. As we have been saying: ours is a formation aimed at configuring us to Christ Priest-Victim, which necessarily points to the Cross in our life. Therefore, we cannot conceive of a priesthood without the cross, without enemies, without trials. All of that is nothing but fine, individual opportunities to make our life more like that of Christ. Yes, the priesthood is a great dignity, which brings with it the glorious suffering of ignominy for the sake of Christ. And that makes perseverance all the more necessary. “The value of good works lies in perseverance,” said St. Gregory the Great.

Your preparation as priest-victims here in these houses of formation means that you are preparing to be witnesses. In Scripture, witness means martyrdom30. But even if none of you sheds his blood for the sake of Christ, witness always implies separation from the world. You must be the salt of the earth: by your works bringing people to God, by the proclamation of the truth denouncing the spirit of the world, etc.

That is why you must always remember what our rule says: “‘Being in the world’ only makes sense when it depends on ‘not being of the world.’ [Which is exactly what our Lord, Priest and Victim, did]. Only thus can one truly be salt of the earth and light of the world (Mt 5:13ff)”31. Again: this will necessarily bring the cross into your lives. Therefore, you must expect to share in his Passion: ridicule, mockery, persecution, ingratitude, etc. But you must persevere. Our disposition must be that of a martyr. Keep this in mind as you advance through this academic year.

St. Isaac Jogues, one of the North American martyrs, after many years in his mission, having suffered much at the hands of the natives and feeling that he was not really advancing toward this configuration with Christ Priest and Victim, once wrote to his superior: “Dear Father, when shall I begin to serve and to love him whose love for us had no beginning? When shall I begin to give myself completely to him who has given himself to me without reserve? Although I am extremely wretched and have misused the graces Our Lord has given me in this country, still I do not lose courage. He takes the trouble to help me do better; he still provides me with new opportunities to die to myself and to unite myself inseparably to him.” And he added –listen carefully–: “My trust is placed in God, who does not need our help to accomplish his designs. The only thing we have to do is try to be faithful to him and not spoil his work with our defects.” How important it is that we live by that!

[Dear brother priests, seminarians and novices: It is my wish for all of you that this new academic year you are now beginning may be an opportunity to configure yourselves to the Incarnate Word, Priest and Victim.

You too are called to imitate Him who, as Priest, was holy with the holiness of God, “through the holiness of your lives”32; to imitate Him as Victim, who was “made sin,” through the “holocaust of yourselves” in the faithful living of the religious vows.

You too are called to imitate Him as the Priest “set apart” from the world who came to fight with the Prince of this world, by giving public witness to your consecration through the way you live, which “includes detachment from the world”33, and by “becoming a fool before the world in order to convert that same world by the foolishness of your preaching”34.

Know that you too are called to imitate the Incarnate Word who, as Priest, asked the Father to let the chalice pass, but who, as Victim, drank it to the full, “loving the living cross of labors, humiliations, insults, tortures, pains, persecutions, misunderstandings, annoyances, contempt, shame, calumnies, death… and being able to say with St. Paul: I die every day in order to nail into their hearts Him who was nailed to the cross for you”35. In fact, we call ourselves ‘co-redeemers’!”36.

I encourage you all not to skimp on any activity, any lesson, any task as you strive to attain this sublime Ideal. He came to you first. Let yourselves be formed by Him. Value and be grateful for the magnificent call you have received, that of being “other Christs,” through your fidelity to it.

May our Most Holy Mother, Mother of Christ Priest and Victim, educate you with her maternal love, so that the authentic image of her Son may be reflected in you.

To her I entrust you and all who will come after you to study in her house, the House of Formation of Our Lady of Sheshan.

God bless you all!

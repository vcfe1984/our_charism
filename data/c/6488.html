The Apostolic Zeal of a Priest of the IVE1

Introduction

One aspect of the life of St. John of the Cross that few know is that during the 28 years he lived as a religious of the Carmelite Order, he exercised not infrequently the “sacred office of governing”1, and he did so with admirable virtue, great discernment and ingenious apostolic zeal. It is this last aspect that I would like to highlight: his apostolic zeal; as a manifestation of that principle which says “love ought to be placed more in deeds than in words”2.

Many of you know the Saint’s extensive spiritual work through his numerous writings, but perhaps few know that he wrote the greater part of his spiritual work while he was provincial of Andalusia3, and that he wrote because he was asked to: “only because they are souls created for heaven”4.

Fray John of the Cross, for all his love of solitude and retreat, possessed great apostolic zeal, as is shown by the enormous number of works, projects and labors in which he became involved in order to do good to every kind of soul, and by the testimonies of many of those souls. Thus, for example, during his terms of government he stood out for his fatherliness in the exercise of his office; he excelled in his apostolate of letters and in his oral and written teaching, as we have said; and in his charity toward the poor (they say that once a poor woman who always used to come begging did not come, so he sent a religious to look for her and find out why she had not come, and at the same time to bring her that day’s meal). St. John of the Cross also traveled a great deal: he traveled more than we could imagine: 27,000 km! almost always on foot or riding a donkey.

Likewise, this great Doctor of the faith, while superior, devoted himself fervently to the construction, remodeling and improvement—we might say—of every convent over which he presided. Thus, in addition to his great activity of governing, he himself toiled at the construction work, because, as he himself said: “God wants souls that are not lazy or delicate, much less fond of themselves”5. He was a man skilled in building: laying floors, putting up partition walls, making adobe bricks, planning structures, etc., so he helped with everything. He even built an aqueduct of 12 arches—which was a colossal feat of engineering at that time—and which is still standing, to bring water to his convent and irrigate its fields. He was a man of solutions.

He also stood out for the great vocational movement he brought about, chiefly thanks to his witness of life as a religious, but also through his exhortations, his writings, and his apostolate with the young, whom he would gather in the convent and to whom he would explain the psalms.

It must also be said that John of the Cross greatly loved the silent labor of the confessional. When he was rector in Baeza, one of the witnesses recounts “that the said holy father had great zeal for the profit of souls, and so very ordinarily he went to the confessional to hear the confessions of and deal with many people, in whom he brought about much profit and much change of life”. So much was this the case that, in order to attend to the people as much as possible, Fray John of the Cross, so faithful to the timetable, changed it with complete freedom of spirit, so that he made his two hours of prayer early in the morning in order to be able to attend to the people afterwards, and likewise the whole afternoon.

Let us add to what has been said that John of the Cross ‘spent’ a great deal of time in the spiritual direction of priests, not only Carmelite religious, but also all kinds of canons and parish priests.

Likewise, the apostolate he carried out with women religious was unparalleled: as chaplain, spiritual director, exorcist, etc. One of his biographers, Alonso de la Madre de Dios, says: “The Reform has not had, nor will it have, anyone who has loved and sought the perfection of its discalced nuns more”6. So true was this that St. Teresa said that, for the perfection of her monasteries, she would like to have in each convent a priest like the holy father Fray John of the Cross7.

His apostolate with lay people was also very fruitful, whether with families, or with the children whom he catechized, or with gentlemen, or with young people in the colleges where he lived, or with those he met along his way in the street. A layman recounts that “he exhorted everyone to endure hardships for God… and to have great confidence in His Majesty, who would deliver them in all their trials”8. Many testified, saying that in Fray John of the Cross love of neighbor was born of the most ardent love he had for God9.

In short, no matter what kind of work it was, what office he held, or what kind of souls he had before him, in everything his magnanimity and great apostolic zeal shone forth. Because “the soul that walks in love neither tires others nor grows tired” (a saying of the saint quoted by the Directory of Spirituality in number 108); what is more, “great works done for the Beloved it considers small, many as few, the long time it serves Him as short”10.

That is why, after recommending to a religious resignation, mortification, the exercise of the virtues and bodily and spiritual solitude, he makes clear to him: “I do not mean by this that you should cease to carry out the office you have, or any other that obedience may command you, with all the diligence possible and necessary, but that you should do it in such a way that no fault clings to you in it, because neither God nor obedience wants this”11.

We, as religious of the Institute of the Incarnate Word, are commanded to live by the “impulses of ‘zeal for souls’”12, and we are constantly told about apostolic enthusiasm and commitment13 and about the great need for apostolic work14 as a service to God and to neighbor. It is an integral part of our life. Our Institute and the Church need a genuine army of superior men, of men who truly are “God’s unconditionals”, capable “of suffering in silence and of giving their lives for their sheep”15: not mere soldiers, not “tributaries”16, not hirelings17 or amateurs, but men who are “capable of bearing the weight of responsibilities”18, “true Pastors of souls in the most sublime sense of the word, who know how to form Jesus Christ in souls from the overflowing of their treasure of grace and virtue”19.

That is why we are going to treat this topic of apostolic zeal in several points:

  • To define what apostolic zeal is
  • The obligation of all priests to work to save souls;
  • The joy that the priest who works for his neighbor gives to God;
  • The salvation and reward he will have;
  • On the end, the means and the works of the priest zealous for the salvation of his neighbor.

1. What apostolic zeal is20

The most beautiful expression of the priest’s pastoral charity is zeal, the flower of his charity.

The word ‘zeal’ has a double meaning. First of all, according to its Greek and Latin etymology, it means something like ‘fever’ or ‘ardor’—in this case, of the spirit. Zeal for souls would be a kind of priestly fever. St. Paul relates it to charity21. Priests who truly love boil with fervor in the service of the Lord. Pastoral zeal is an impetus of charity that comes from the sacramental grace of the priesthood and unfolds throughout the whole apostolic ministry.

If Christ affirmed that He had come to the earth to bring fire and that He wished nothing but that it be kindled22, it falls to us priests to spread this blaze. “Zeal for the glory of God and for the salvation of souls must consume the priest, make him forget himself and all earthly things, and drive him powerfully to consecrate himself fully to his sublime mission, seeking ever more effective means to carry it out with ever-increasing extension and perfection”23, wrote Pius XI to priests.

Let us now say what some of the characteristics of this zeal are:

Creative zeal: The mission we receive from Christ is not for resting. Christ wants us to be on the move for souls. That we not settle into the routine of our apostolate, or better, that we not make our apostolate a routine, but that every day we think up new initiatives. That we always be asking ourselves: What more can I do for Christ? How can I bring Him closer to souls? How will I manage to convince them that He is the only one who deserves to be loved without reservation? Think: which proper apostolates are missing in my parish: An Oratory? A youth group? The good press? Altar servers? How long has it been since a popular mission was held? Do we organize camps for our children? How am I going to improve the next patronal feast, the next Holy Week, the next Christmas? And if I teach classes: How am I going to ‘hook’ my students more? Or am I perhaps still teaching from the same notes of 20 years ago with the excuse that doctrine hasn’t changed? How much am I doing for vocations? How can I improve the organization of my parish, of my community? We have to ask ourselves these things… “We must think through the new problems and creatively seek effective solutions, with great confidence in the power of God who continues working tirelessly in the world. We must not be afraid of unprecedented forms of pastoral work, provided they are according to God”24.

Universal zeal: The priest’s zeal must be universal, catholic. One must not have a corner-kiosk mentality, thinking “that the Church is exhausted in one’s parish, city, province or country”25 or in one’s own rite. Before being pastors of a localized group of souls, know that you are pastors of the whole universe. In your heart must beat the catholic heart of Christ who tells us: Go into the whole world. “That is the heart of an authentic priest. It is a universal heart that encompasses and embraces every human being; and just as it encompasses and embraces every human being, it encompasses and embraces everything that is authentically human: social, political, economic problems—such as the lack of work, unemployment, the need to have one’s rights defended—; the advance of science, of technology, of art, of culture; it encompasses and embraces everything that has to do with families, with the homeland, with the world. So much so that none of these authentically human things is foreign to him”26.

Magnanimous and generous zeal: In imitation of the Apostle we must commit ourselves to the salvation of souls, spend and be spent for them27. Look: the spirit of poverty, of which we have spoken, must not be understood only with regard to material things. We priests must also have a spirit of poverty with regard to our time and our own tastes. How sad it is to hear a priest say: “Why do I have to be available all the time if everybody else works only 8 hours?” These things happen. And so he only attends to people during office hours; after that, they’d better not bother him. And don’t think this happens only to those on the outside; it happens to us too. Sometimes we make ourselves a schedule for resting, for taking a nap, for reading, for going out for a walk, an untouchable schedule, and if anyone has the audacity to alter it, woe to him. It is good to keep to a certain schedule, above all to safeguard the substantial things, those that pertain to the interior life, and which deserve a certain untouchability. But such a schedule, in all its details, cannot be placed above the needs of souls. I invite you to see for yourselves that priestly happiness lies in spending and being spent, as St. Paul says. What could be better than that!

St. Manuel González used to say that “the priest’s door should be the most pounded-on in the neighborhood, the priest’s office the most frequented with delicate and unpaid matters. All trades have their working hours; the priest’s has no hours; over his door one must put, as on the fourth pages of newspapers short of advertisements: AVAILABLE…, always available at any hour of the day and night”28.

Authentic zeal: Let us say finally that the apostolic charity of the priest, which is expressed in his ministry, needs to be corroborated by example. It is not only the positively bad priest who wreaks havoc among the faithful; the merely lukewarm priest in the long run exercises an equally baneful influence. A mediocre clergy begets an irreligious people; a merely ‘believing’ clergy in the long run begets a perfectly unbelieving people, wrote Fr. Sáenz. “Why does our witness sometimes prove vain?—asked John Paul II—. Because we present a Jesus without all the attractive power that His Person offers; without making manifest the riches of the sublime ideal that following Him entails; because we do not always manage to show a conviction made life regarding the stupendous value of our self-giving to the great ecclesial cause we serve”29.

2. The obligation of all priests to work to save souls

It is said that: “There are many priests and there are few; many in name, but few in deeds”30, and St. Alphonsus adds: “The world is full of priests, but few are those who strive to be priests in truth, that is, to fulfill the office and dignity of the priest, which is to save souls”31. Few strive to save souls: “the most divine work among divine works is the work of the salvation of souls”32. Jeremiah calls them fishers and hunters33. St. Clement says: “After God, he is the God of the earth”34, since it is by means of priests that saints are formed here below. “Without priests, there would be no saints on earth”35, says St. Ignatius of Antioch, martyr.

Very great is the dignity of priests; but very great also is their twofold obligation: For every High Priest is taken from among men and appointed on behalf of men in the things that pertain to God, to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins; and he can feel compassion for the ignorant and the erring, since he too is beset with weakness36.

“His office is to win souls, not money”37, teaches St. Ambrose. His very name, priest (sacerdote), expresses the nature of his functions: sacra docens (according to St. Antoninus), or sacra dans (according to St. Thomas), and presbyter, from praebens íter (according to Honorius of Autun), the one who opens to the people the road that leads to heaven. Therefore, the priest must be the guide and support of souls along the way of salvation.

St. Jerome teaches: “If you wish to carry out the office of priest, act in such a way that you save your own soul by saving those of others”38. But if the salt does not salt, what good is it?

He is a physician (Origen, St. Jerome), but “if the physician flees from the sick—asks St. Bonaventure—who will care for them?”39. Priests have the mission of uprooting vices and pernicious maxims from peoples and of making the virtues and eternal maxims flourish. God imposes on him the same obligation as on Jeremiah: This very day I give you authority over the nations and over the kingdoms, to uproot and to destroy, to ruin and to overthrow, to rebuild and to plant40.

In the book Sacerdotes para siempre we read: “Often the priest is very busy, but he must learn to busy himself with the principal things of the ministry, not with accidental things or with secondary things. Souls need to talk with the priest and need to tell him their concerns, because the priest is also the physician of souls”41.

How can the negligent and lazy one be excused from sin? Will he not perhaps hear: take the talent from him, therefore, and give it to the one who has the ten talents. For to everyone who has, more will be given and he will have abundance; but from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away. And as for this useless servant, cast him into the outer darkness. There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth (Mt 25:28-30)?

“For the priest, living holily will not suffice to be saved, because he will be lost along with those who are lost through his fault”42, says St. Prosper, whether through negligence or through fear of sinning (a vain fear). Negligent priests will be held accountable before God for all the souls they could have helped and which were lost through their negligence, taught St. Gregory the Great43.

The great St. Luigi Orione, whom our Proper Law quotes so copiously, insisted greatly to his followers on the importance of working tirelessly for the salvation of souls. “Souls and souls! This is our sigh and our cry: souls and souls! And to work with humility, with simplicity and faith, and then forward in the name of the Lord, never becoming disturbed; forward with confidence, for it is God who does everything, He who alone knows the hours and the moments of His works and holds all people and all things in His hands. Forward with the most lively faith, with total and filial confidence in the Lord and in His Church, because poor indeed is the man or the human institution that thinks it is doing something”44.

“When in a house—the saint goes on to say—idleness begins to creep in, or little desire to work, or one is not as hardworking and active as one should be, that house is ruined. If, on the contrary, we work hard and work so that the talents bear fruit, and under the gaze of God, and to fulfill the will of the Lord and follow the example of the Lord, the work will be worthy of us and of God: work will be the great remedy against concupiscence, and a powerful weapon against all the snares of the devil and the temptations of the world and the flesh.

Work, work, work. We are the sons of faith and of work. And we must love and be the apostles of work and of faith. We must always run to work, and work more and more.

Take care of your health, but always work, with zeal, with ardor for the cause of God, of the Church, of souls. Look to heaven, pray, and then… forward with courage and work”45. So far Don Orione.

3. On the pleasure given to God by the priest who devotes himself to the salvation of souls

To realize how much God desires the salvation of souls, it is enough simply to consider what He has done in the work of human redemption. Jesus Christ made this desire of His very plain when He said: I have a baptism with which I must be baptized, and how great is my anguish until it is accomplished!46. He seemed to faint with the longing He had to see accomplished the work of redemption that would save mankind. From this St. John Chrysostom rightly infers that “there is nothing dearer to God than the salvation of souls”47. St. Lawrence Justinian, speaking to the priest, said: “If you are concerned for the honor of God, you cannot seek it in a better way than by working for the salvation of souls”48. St. Bernard says that in the eyes of God one soul is worth as much as the whole world; hence Chrysostom wrote that “whoever converts a single soul pleases God more than if he distributed all his goods in alms”49. Tertullian assures us that “God esteems the salvation of one little sheep that has strayed from the path as much as the salvation of the whole flock”.50 That is why the Apostle said: He loved me and gave Himself up for me51.

The ecclesiastical spirit, writes Louis Habert, “consists precisely in ardent zeal to promote the glory of God and the salvation of one’s neighbor”.

After our Lord had asked Peter, as many as three times, whether he loved Him, being now sure of his love, He recommended to him as proof of that love nothing other than that he take care of souls. St. John Chrysostom comments: “He could have said: if you love me, detach yourself from money, fast, sleep on the ground, wear out your body with labors. But no; He only said feed my sheep”52. And St. Augustine comments on the word my, supposing that the Lord meant: Feed them as mine, not as yours; in them seek my glory and not yours, my profit and not yours53.

“There are in the Church some prelates of whom the apostle St. Paul says that they seek their own interests and not those of Jesus Christ54. By which he means that they do not love Christ freely, that they do not seek God for God’s sake, that they pursue temporal comforts, greedy for gain and desirous of human honors”55. Of such as these, St. John of the Cross says: “that they will find no reward in God, having sought to find it in this life, in joy or consolation, or in the interest of honor or in some other way, in their works; regarding which the Savior says that in that they received their pay56. And so they were left with only the labor of the work, and confounded without reward”57.

Notice how different is the attitude of the one who knows himself to be a shepherd of souls: St. Paul, for example, affirmed that to obtain the salvation of his neighbors he would have accepted being separated from Jesus Christ (for some time, as the interpreters explain): for I could wish that I myself were accursed by Christ for the sake of my brethren according to the flesh58. St. John Chrysostom “desired to be regarded as an object of excrement provided that his subjects were converted”59. St. Bonaventure declared that he would suffer as many deaths as there were sinners in the world, so that all might be saved. St. Francis de Sales, finding himself among the heretics of the Chablais, did not hesitate, in the depths of winter, to cross on all fours a frozen beam that spanned the river, exposed to suffering and danger, in exchange for being able to go and preach to those people. St. Cajetan, being in Naples in the year 1547, when that terrible revolution broke out, on seeing so many souls being lost, was so deeply affected that he died of sheer grief. St. Ignatius of Loyola said that, even if he were certain of his eternal salvation should he die at that hour, he would nevertheless choose to remain on earth, even in uncertainty about being saved, if by doing so he could continue helping souls. Here is the zeal for souls that animates all priests who love God; and yet there are priests who, on the slightest excuse, so as not to expose themselves to a little bit of work or out of fear of some illness, neglect helping souls. And in this, those who at times have the care of souls also fail. St. Charles Borromeo said that the parish priest who wants to adopt every kind of comfort and use whatever he can to favor his bodily health will never be able to fulfill his obligations well. And he added that the parish priest should never take to his bed until after three bouts of fever (something more to be admired than imitated).

Fulton Sheen affirmed that every priest should ask himself how many adults he has baptized in recent years and how many non-practicing Catholics he has brought back to the Church. “Why do some priests—he asked—never convert anyone, while others convert hundreds of souls? Could it not be because some took the title of “father” seriously and others did not? […] Administration is absolutely essential; to ignore it would be to overestimate the fact that each member has a function in the Mystical Body of Christ. But the Holy Spirit did not call us to be bankers, or real-estate agents or architects. Those activities are ‘accidental’. The Holy Spirit was not given to us so that we might sit at tables counting money. […] It is not enough to be sacristy priests”60.

4. How the priest who works for the salvation of souls secures his eternal salvation, and of the extraordinary reward he will have for it in heaven

The priest who in life sacrificed himself for the good of souls will hardly die a bad death. When… you give your bread to the hungry and satisfy the humbled soul, your light will shine in the darkness… And Yahweh will guide you continually… and will strengthen your bones61, says Scripture. If you spend your life, says the Prophet, helping the needy soul and consoling it in its afflictions, when the darkness of your temporal death comes, the Lord will fill you with light and deliver you from eternal death. This is what St. Augustine said: “If you have saved a soul, you have predestined your own”. And before him the Apostle James had said it: let him know that whoever brings back a sinner from the error of his way will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins62.

The priests who sacrificed themselves for souls will hear God Himself announce eternal rest to them at death: Yes—says the Spirit—, let them rest from their labors, for their works accompany them63.

“If he who frees a man from temporal death deserves a great reward, says St. Gregory, how much greater will be the reward deserved by him who frees a soul from eternal death and secures for it a life that will have no end?”64. The priest who is damned is not damned alone; but the priest who is saved certainly is not saved alone.

Blessed Allamano used to tell his followers: “When you think of paradise, do not think of it in an abstract way, but of the paradise of the missionary man and woman who are faithful to their vocation. The Lord said: I go to prepare a place for you65. But for this it is necessary to work, and to work hard. It would be too comfortable to have paradise now, so soon! No, no; to work forty, fifty years, even more. It seems to me that this thought of paradise should console us. Our reward is there, and it is very great! Let us think of it often”66. “Remember that it is not enough to preach; we must also commit ourselves in everything we do and accept all the sacrifices of the apostolic life, whatever they may cost. ‘Let us work, let us work—exclaimed Joseph Cafasso—we shall rest in paradise!’”67.

Let not the priest who, after working to bring souls to God, does not see his efforts crowned with success, be downcast or give up so important a mission. My priest, St. Bernard tells him to encourage him, in spite of this do not lose confidence, and believe firmly in the reward that awaits you. God does not demand of you the healing of these souls; you need only try to heal them, and He will reward you, not according to the result of your efforts, but according to the efforts themselves68. St. Bonaventure also confirms this, adding that the priest will not merit less for the efforts expended on those with whom little or no success is achieved than for those in whom success is complete69. The same saint adds that the farmer who cultivates arid and stony ground, even when the yield is meager, deserves a greater reward; by which he means that the priest who toils to bring some obstinate soul to God, even if he does not bring him, will see his reward grow in proportion to the growth of his labors.

St. John of the Cross says in one of his letters: [remember that] “he who stores up treasure out of love stores it up for another, and it is good that He keep it and enjoy it, since it is all for Him; and as for us, let us not even see it with our eyes nor enjoy it, so that we may not deflower for God the delight He takes in the humility and nakedness of our heart and in our contempt of the things of the world for His sake”70.

Therefore, “nothing ought to diminish our apostolic zeal. A priest who grows tired, who becomes discouraged, who lets his arms drop—has he seriously reflected on the value of a soul? The charity of our pastoral zeal, like a flame of the heart, needs to be preserved and fed. An excellent means of rekindling it is to recall from time to time the many and pressing motives the priest has for loving souls. Those souls were made by God, they have as their last end the joyful possession of God, they have been bought at the price of the blood of Christ. Let them not have to complain, like the man in the Gospel who lamented: I have no man71, by saying: ‘I have no priest’”72. We must do them good, as the Master of the faith said, “only because they are souls created for heaven”73.

5. On the end, the means and the works of the zealous priest

1.º On the end he must set before himself.

If we wish to receive from God the reward for our toils for the salvation of souls, we must do what we do not out of human respect, nor for our own honor or for earthly gain, but only for God and for His grace; if we do not do so, instead of a reward we shall bring upon ourselves punishment. St. Joseph Calasanz said that our folly would be great if, wearing ourselves out as we do, we were to expect an earthly reward from men.

And as St. Prosper writes, there are those who strive not to become better, but to grow rich; not to acquire holiness, but to enjoy honors. Peter of Blois says: “When a benefice is to be filled, does anyone perhaps ask how many souls there are to win for God? No; what is inquired into is what its revenues are”. Many, says the Apostle, seek their own interests, not those of Jesus Christ74. “O detestable abuse, to subordinate heaven to earth!”, said St. John of Ávila.

The author of the Opus Imperfectum writes: “We are hired workmen of Jesus Christ; and just as no one hires a workman to do nothing but eat, so we have not been called by Christ only to look after our own interests, but for the glory of God”75. From this St. Gregory concludes that priests must not take pleasure in being set over men, but in doing them all the good possible76. The only end, therefore, that the priest who works for the good of souls must set before himself must be the glory of God.

2.º On the means he must employ

As for the means to be employed to win souls for the Lord, here is what must above all be done:

– First of all, one must attend to one’s own sanctification. The principal means of converting the souls of sinners is the holiness of the priest. St. Eucherius says that it is priests who, with the strength that holiness gives them, sustain the world. And St. Thomas: “The priest, as mediator, is charged with uniting men peacefully with God”77.

St. Philip Neri used to say: “Give me ten priests animated by the Spirit of God, and I will answer for the conversion of the whole world”. What did St. Francis Xavier not do in the East! They say that he alone converted thousands of unbelievers. What did St. Patrick or St. Vincent Ferrer not do in Europe! A moderately educated priest who loves God much will convert more souls to God than a hundred priests of great learning but little fervor. That is why John Paul II, in his first letter to priests (Holy Thursday 1979), told them: “to be up to date (aggiornati) is to be saints”78.

– In the second place, to gather a great harvest of souls one must devote oneself greatly to prayer, because in it one must receive from God the lights and fervent sentiments, so as to be able afterwards to communicate them to others: What I tell you in the darkness, speak in the light of day79. In this sense, Fulton Sheen affirmed that a priest can save souls without having eloquence, but he cannot move them without prayer and without the Holy Spirit80.

3.º On the works of the zealous priest

Here are some works to which the zealous priest must consecrate himself:

– He must attend to the correction of sinners. Priests who see offenses against God and remain silent deserve to be called what Isaiah calls them, dumb dogs, unable to bark81. To these dumb dogs will be imputed all the sins they could have prevented and did not prevent. A strange thing!, exclaims St. Bernard: “a donkey falls, and not a few are easily found ready to lift it up; a man is lost, and there is no one to lift him up…”82. Yet, says St. Gregory, the priest is especially appointed by God to show the right road to the one who has gone astray; and that is why St. Leo adds that the priest who does not point out to the faithful their straying shows that he himself has gone astray. St. Gregory writes that we, “the priests of the Lord, kill as many souls as we see perish without working to go to their aid”83.

– The zealous priest must work in the ministry of preaching. It was by means of preaching that the world was converted to the faith of Jesus Christ, as the Apostle said: faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ84. By preaching, faith and the fear of God are preserved in the faithful. Let priests who do not feel capable of preaching at least try, whenever they can, in their conversations with relatives and friends, to say something edifying, to recount some edifying example practiced by the saints, or to suggest some maxim.

It is said of St. John of the Cross that he preached very rarely at his Masses. Yet every day he gave his subjects admirable talks, with which he encouraged them to be perfect and taught them that, in order to ascend to perfection, they were to desire neither goods of earth nor of heaven, but only to desire and seek nothing other than to seek and desire in all things the glory and honor of God. They say that those who dealt with him most made the most progress85. In the same way he taught catechism to children, gave talks to young people, etc.

– The priest must assist the dying, since this is the work of charity most pleasing to God and most useful for the salvation of souls, because at the moment of death sick people are, on the one hand, more tempted by the devil and, on the other, less able to help themselves. There are those who, in going to help the dying, encounter the death of their own soul. Moreover, whoever cannot preach should at least teach doctrine to children and country folk, many of whom will live in the fields, unable to go to the churches, and therefore vegetating in ignorance even of the principal truths of the faith.

– Finally, let us be persuaded that the principal exercise for the good of souls is to hear their confessions. The Ven. Fr. Luis Florillo, a Dominican, used to say that preaching is casting the nets, whereas hearing confessions is hauling the catch on board.

Before receiving the priesthood, says St. John Chrysostom, you should have examined whether you would dare to carry out this ministry; but now that you are already a priest, there is no option for examination, but only for work, and if you are not capable, make yourself capable. To allege ignorance as an excuse now—the holy doctor continues—is equivalent to accusing yourself of a second fault in order to excuse yourself of the first.

There are priests who give themselves to the study of a thousand useless things and neglect the study of the things necessary to work fruitfully for the salvation of souls. In sum, it must needs be that the priest seek solely the salvation of souls. That is why St. Sylvester wanted the days of the week to be called by no other name than ferias, that is, holidays. By which they are to learn (these are his words) that they must set aside anything else, in exchange for being free solely for the things of God.

We have already mentioned how St. John of the Cross loved the silent apostolate of the confessional. Let me add this testimony of a friar who lived with him in Baeza: “And this witness also said that, having lived many years with the said holy father in the College of Baeza, confessions have never been so continuous as in the time he was in the said college, although many people ordinarily go to confession there; but during the time he was in the said College of Baeza as prelate, every day, both in the morning and in the afternoon, the confessors were in the confessionals, and they could not finish hearing the confessions of all the people who came”86. We must be diligent in this apostolate.

St. Manuel González expressed it thus: “A church open from very early, at least one hour before the people’s work begins, with a priest who is the first to enter and the last to leave, and who waits seated in the confessional, and this in a constant and fixed manner, is a church that cannot be long in seeing itself well attended”87. Is it so or not? Where people know they can go and there is a priest in the confessional regularly (at the same hours), that is the church they go to for confession. “A priest seated in his confessional from very early, even if he has no penitents, is always a sweet and overpowering violence upon the Heart of Jesus to pour out extraordinary graces. It is a stimulus and a powerful example for his good parishioners (few or many), so that they do not let themselves be overcome either by discouragement or by the pretext of many occupations. It is a convenience for poor and busy parishioners. It is an awakener of remorse for sinful and even hardened parishioners”88.

If we observe carefully, the whole force of [St. Alphonsus’s] argument lies in the weight of eternity. This is so true that when eternity ceases to count for a priest, he becomes sterile, since it disappears from the horizon of his life. What matters, in order to have zeal for souls, is the weight of eternity. If this is lacking, everything is lacking. That is why we must always guard in our conscience the eternal weight of incalculable glory89. And Holy Mass is the privileged school where one learns to value eternity.

The pastoral importance of eternity is inescapable, as we have always been taught. When the priest loses sight of eternity, it is because he is already in the abyss. He has lost his priestly identity and has become salt that loses its savor and lacks zeal for souls. A priest without zeal is a priest who has first neglected the Eucharist.

On the other hand, so that we may avoid the errors that tend to occur in the field of the apostolate, it is good for us to remember the following:

-since some refuse all apostolate because they believe it is activism (or that they may fall into it) and so fall into quietism: against this, devote to the apostolate ALL the time assigned.

– Others, very restless, carried away by disordered zeal, fall into activism: against this, devote to the apostolate ONLY the time assigned.

– We are SHEPHERDS. Therefore we must give our life for the sheep, spend and be spent. The apostolate is part of our life; we cannot opt out; it is not an appendix in our life. We have read and heard it thousands of times: pastoral work for us is the Cross90.

– Nor must we think that because we do apostolate this hinders contemplation (as those think who confuse the contemplative life with a vegetative life). The same objection was made to Blessed Allamano and he answered them: “Let us not lament being somewhat dispersed by having to fulfill our missionary commitments. Let us only pray a great deal, as St. Francis Xavier did”91. Clear enough, isn’t it?

Conclusion

It must be said that in many places, in more or less developed countries, both yesterday and today, there happens what St. Manuel González said of some parishes in his time: “It is true that the circumstances could not be worse. How sad that is! The priest who, as the fruit of a life of sacrifices and bitterness, reaps a church almost always empty; five percent, if that, of the faithful fulfilling the Easter and Sunday precept; most of the sick dying impenitent or, at most, with conditional holy oil; the very men he has favored with jobs, favors or alms turning their faces away so as not to greet him; those who do frequent the church, bored perhaps; and to cap and crown it all, his works, the best ones, those done with the best intention, misinterpreted or slandered. The priest, I repeat, who sees all this truly needs all the heroism of a martyr to wake up each day with a smiling face, a hopeful heart and a spirit tranquil enough to keep plowing his furrow, without fainting and without falling. And I do not exaggerate if I say that this is the state of many parishes, and that with these colors, and darker still, if possible, one must paint the picture they present.

[Here comes the message. Pay attention:] Heroes! That, that is what the priests of such parishes must be, and if they are not, first the discouragement that disheartens; then the tedium that holds back; and finally the pessimism that renders everything useless, will take hold of their hearts and close their valves, paralyzing the flow of blood, and, as a consequence, inactivity, inertia for everything good, and may the Lord grant that after inertia there not come moral death with all its rottenness…!

[In the face of this panorama, the saint says] I limit myself to presenting to my brothers […] the magic prescription, which invents, suggests, enlivens and carries out all those means that serve to put the Christian spirit into the hearts of the multitudes, today unfortunately so lacking in it and, in general, so opposed to it.

That prescription is expressed in a single word: zeal.

Zeal invents and stimulates and produces disinterestedness [regarding oneself and ‘one’s own things’], which attracts and softens hearts, and the kind manner that binds them to the heart of the priest.

It is with this zeal that unsettles, that troubles, that robs one of sleep, that makes the souls of the parishioners an obsession for the priest, that he learns to become all things to all, and it is this zeal that gives that adaptability to trades, conditions, characters and circumstances so necessary for one who acts as father to so many kinds of children. It is this zeal that gives the priest who feels it that skill and flexibility to become a farmer with farmers, a lawyer with those who are in lawsuits, a carpenter, a bricklayer and any other trade, when some such work has to be done and there is no money to pay for it; a child with children, a youth with the young, an old man with the old. This zeal, finally, is what puts on the priest’s face that unalterable smile with which he welcomes everyone and everything, the pleasant and the unpleasant, and on his lips that ever calm and affectionate word; it is what moves his hand to reach many times into his own pocket and then empty it into someone else’s.

Yes, zeal works true miracles of initiative, of improvisation, of self-mastery, of generosity without conditions and without limits.

Wherever zeal nests in the heart of the priest, there an exuberant and rich parish pastoral life will flourish”92.

Let us never forget that missionary impetus is part of our DNA as members of the Institute.

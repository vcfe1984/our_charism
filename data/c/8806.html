Our Directory of Spirituality, quoting the Mystical Doctor, says: “Let those, then, who are very active, who think to encircle the world with their preaching and exterior works, take note here that they would bring much more profit to the Church and be much more pleasing to God if they spent even half the time in being with God in prayer”1.

They say that when St. John of the Cross was free from other occupations he would be found in a little corner of his cell, or commonly in the choir before the Blessed Sacrament. They say that most of the siesta hours in summer, and two or three hours of the night in winter, he spent in prayer in the church or in the choir. And the Saint recommended that same spirit of prayer to other religious: “try to be continual in prayer, and in the midst of bodily exercises do not leave it. Whether you eat, drink, or speak or deal with lay people, or do anything else, always go about desiring God and attaching your heart to him, which is a very necessary thing for interior solitude”2.

That is what we are going to treat now: prayer.

Only for reasons of simplicity and order we will divide this talk into four points:

  1. Mental prayer
  2. Liturgical prayer
  3. The most excellent prayer, which is Holy Mass
  4. Marian devotion

1. Mental prayer

“In doing our works well lies our spiritual commerce, and in doing them according to the rule, our holiness. Perfection consists in doing them with a spirit of prayer.” Who said this? St. Peter Julian Eymard, founder of the Blessed Sacrament Fathers.

Definitely, that is the idea. Prayer must permeate all our acts. As St. John of the Cross was also just saying: …whether you drink, eat, or deal with lay people, you must always have your mind and heart on God alone.

As you well know, part of the discipline of our Institute is “the exposition and adoration of the Blessed Sacrament for one hour daily”3, because we are aware that “a while of true adoration has more value and spiritual fruit than the most intense activity, even if it were apostolic activity itself”4. That must always be kept in mind and must be guarded very carefully.

Fulton Sheen, as you perhaps already know, was a great apostle of Eucharistic adoration. He himself made it daily. To tell the truth, in his time Eucharistic adoration was not something so common, and that is why he insisted a great deal, above all to priests and to those preparing for the priesthood, that they devote an hour to adoration daily. Then some objected to him that an hour a day was very difficult. So he told them that what is done once a week is an interruption in the week, that an hour of adoration once a week never becomes a habit, that an hour a week is not a gift that shows a very deep love. Because: What mother is content to see her son only once a week? What wife is content to see her husband once a week? Love is not intermittent. Adoration must be done every day, he said, because our crosses are daily, not once a week; because the children, the sick, the missions, the families, the dying need our intercession every day5; because the enemy does not rest6.

We have the ease and the grace that in our communities and in most of our parishes Eucharistic adoration is daily and something ‘familiar’. Nevertheless, there is always the risk of not doing it with the excuse that one is ‘very busy’, has to travel, is on vacation, is alone for a while, and who knows what else. Not to mention interior dryness, tiredness, ‘meetings’, soccer matches, the siesta, our own lukewarmness and a host of other things that are always trying to make us set adoration aside. Fulton Sheen himself affirms that the hour of adoration faithfully kept throughout priestly life is a sign that the priest is a true victim. I do not remember where I read it, but he tells how many times he had to get up earlier in order to make his adoration, and sometimes that meant very few hours of sleep, but he made his hour of adoration all the same. And elsewhere he tells that once, being in Paris, he had two hours between trains, so he went to the Church of Saint-Roch to make his holy hour. And he says that although there are not even ten days in a whole year on which he sleeps during the day, that day was one of them, because he was utterly exhausted. He says he sat down at about two in the afternoon (he was too tired to kneel) and fell asleep. He slept soundly until three in the afternoon. On waking, he says, he asked our Lord: Did I make the hour of adoration? (as if he had not even noticed) and he says Christ answered him: Yes, that is how the apostles made their first hour of adoration. The point is that thus, tiredness and all, between trips or before trips, at home or away from it, he would look for an adoration chapel and there make his hour of adoration, but he did not fail to make it. A temptation that is always there.

You know very well all the reasons why it is more than fitting to make adoration. Here I would like to add or specify some others, besides the principal reason, which was that the Incarnate Word himself asked his priests, the apostles, to do it: Watch one hour with me.

  1. The hour of adoration combats priestly weariness. As in a marriage, after some years in the priesthood one loses the sensitivity of love. Therefore we must pay closer attention to what we have heard, lest we drift away, as the Letter to the Hebrews says7. Our souls are lost not only because we do evil things, but also because we are negligent in doing good things: we bury the talent, we do not walk the extra mile, we pass by the wounded man the Samaritan helped. How often in the gospel condemnation follows precisely upon not doing: you did not give me to eat, you did not give me to drink, you did not welcome me, you did not visit me8…

We who have the enormous privilege not only of being priests but of having in our houses the Divine Presence of our Lord, how much greater is our responsibility to adore him and keep him company.

If I ask you: How many retreats have you made in your life? Ten? Twenty? Forty? How many resolutions made in all those retreats have you kept? There it gets more difficult. Perhaps many of those retreats were like those congresses held about health: everyone wants health but no one makes a concrete proposal. Eucharistic adoration is something concrete, a practical resolution that helps me confront the apathy and decline that corrode our souls.

Usually the main reasons why we give up the hour of adoration are: 1. Time; 2. The weakness of our will.

We say we do not have time. How much time do we spend reading the newspaper on the internet, or watching a match, or going to the gym, or watching stupid little videos on YouTube? But we are intelligent, so sometimes the excuses take on a more religious tint: ‘I have to plan the mission’, ‘I have to prepare a sermon’, ‘the novices are coming to the parish’, ‘today I have to teach six hours of class’… The excuses we sometimes give are as silly as the ones given in the gospel to that king for not attending the banquet: I have bought a field and I must go and see it (imagine, he bought it without seeing it); I have bought five yoke of oxen and I am on my way to ‘try them out’; I have just gotten married, well then, why don't you bring your wife too?! And so we argue that we have no time, which leads us to the second real reason for not making adoration, which is the weakness of our will.

We live in an age in which our will collapses under emotion; we have no determination. How many have abandoned the self-discipline that is the condition for our priestly victimhood. We become like children: not a second must pass between our demands and their satisfaction.

How, then, do we overcome the excuse of time and apathy? By taking control of a segment of time, of one hour, and redeeming it. It is for our Lord, it is for the Church, it is for the world, not for me. Period. We must kill acedia by displacing it with a new love. We cannot expel our vices all at once, but we can hem them in by deepening our love for Christ.

  1. The hour of adoration is a sign of our victimhood in the work of redemption. Because it incorporates us into the intercessory work of Christ. We are bound to humanity, to the nations, to the missions, to our homeland, to our diocese, to our parish, to our Institute, to our friends and to our enemies. Our victim-priesthood commits us to intercede for them and for their salvation. Perhaps one of the cruelest words a priest can say to those who suffer is ‘Pray.’ Are we not their intercessors? We must pray for them and with Right there. The agony of Christ continues in marriages going through difficulties, in girls who have lost their purity, in religious who are tempted, in children of addicted parents, in abandoned elderly people, in priests who have lost their faith, in enemies who fight against us with malice, in catechists who teach everything but Christ, in defections…

That is why another motivation for the hour of adoration is also that of reparation. The Proper Law says it explicitly: we “adore Jesus Christ for those who do not adore him, who abandon him, forget him, despise him and offend him”9. How many of my adorations do I offer for that intention of reparation?

  1. Lastly, the hour of adoration is necessary as a form of authentic prayer. Our world is incredibly fast. The other day I had to prepare a talk on technology. Do you know what happens in one minute of internet? I will give you some figures so you can realize the speed of the age in which we live. For example: in one minute, Netflix users watch 694,444 hours of video. 277,777 posts are made on Instagram. Every minute YouTube users watch 4,500,000 videos. 511,200 tweets are sent. 188,000,000 emails are sent. Every minute more than 18 million text messages are sent. Skype users make 231,840 calls. 55,140 photos are uploaded to Instagram. 4,497,420 searches are made on Google. Every minute 1,389 bookings are made on Airbnb. 9,772 Uber rides are taken. Every 60 seconds 390,000 apps are downloaded. We are included in all those numbers, eh?

Add to that the noise that drowns out the voice of conscience; get on a train and see how many do not have earphones: most do. We live in a world where activity kills the knowledge of oneself and of God that contemplation brings. Even in many seminaries, in many communities, there is a sort of fragmentation of spiritual exercises, that is, now we do adoration, at noon we pray the Angelus, at night I pray Compline, done. But the spirit of prayer that ought to be present throughout the whole day is lacking. Fulton Sheen says: hell is full of clocks, and so is our priestly life. And he proposed as a solution making an uninterrupted hour of adoration, an hour that begins along with the day, early in the silence of the morning, and is prolonged throughout the day. Being faithful to that hour of adoration and not letting anything interfere with it is definitely, he says, a sign of our victimhood with Christ.

God has not called us to lead the penitential life of the anchorites, but our hour of adoration is our sacrifice united to that of Christ. Many of you, I know for a fact, make it with great fidelity: Congratulations! Because the pastoral work you are doing proves that you have first been in familiar converse with the Incarnate Word. And that must be maintained with all fidelity.

In one of his books Sheen recounted the following: “I know a priest who has never failed to make his hour of adoration in 54 years. His daily petition is to drop dead in the presence of the Blessed Sacrament on a Saturday, on a feast of our Lady. He is not sure the Lord will grant it to him after having asked for that grace for so long, but he always says that if the Lord does not grant it, he will be ashamed when he sees him.” He was speaking of himself. He died on Sunday, December 9, 1979, the day after the solemnity of the Immaculate Conception. This past year (2019) marked 40 years since his death, and since December 8 fell on a Sunday it was moved to December 9. It seemed that God was fulfilling his wish.

So far we have spoken of the formal hour of adoration, which is part of our rule and which we must not neglect. An hour of prayer that we must prolong throughout the whole day, however busy we may be. We must live as if clothed with the spirit of prayer. We pray in the morning, at night and several times throughout the day; but these are acts of prayer, not habits that form the spirit of prayer. Why, after so many days, months, years of performing these acts of piety, are we still so far from perfection? And we do not say this only out of humility, but starting from reality. The answer can only be this: either we do not do them well, or we do not try to profit from them. “Recollection is absolutely necessary in order to profit from what one does; otherwise we are left with those kinds of oases [of intermittent acts] that are the spiritual practices, but outside of them, everything is arid. When, afterwards, we cannot keep our mind fixed on God, it is enough to refer our actions to him and everything becomes prayer. In this consists the spirit of prayer, which helps the interior life a great deal. A missionary must be able to maintain recollection in any place; to know how to pass from study or work to prayer; to remain united to God with a permanent, or at least frequent, elevation of the heart; in short, to work with great dedication and, at the same time, to pray. If you do not have this spirit, you will never be good missionaries. You may believe you are, but in reality you are not. Happy are you if you always try to advance in the interior life, with the spirit of recollection and prayer!”10, said Blessed Allamano.

If we maintain this spirit of prayer, of recollection, it becomes not only easier to discern the will of God, but also to safeguard oneself against a host of temptations and dangers.

I give you as an example St. John of the Cross, who was a man of well-settled categories and demanded of himself what he taught others. He said: “What does it profit you to give God one thing if he asks you for another? Consider what God will want and do it, for in this way you will satisfy your heart better than with that to which you are inclined”11. That is, faced with one task or another, having discerned the will of God, the saint immediately did what was asked of him and knew how to leave contemplation for adobe bricks, spiritual direction for the broom, poetry in order to attend with all attention and patience to some poor man who knocked at the door. All of which was possible because he lived in the spirit of prayer. And we must do the same “so that we may proceed from the lesser to the greater and from the more exterior to the more interior, until we reach the intimate recollection where the soul is united with God”12.

It is no news that priestly life, missionary life, is strewn with great graces but also with great trials and much work. “Hence the need for long periods of prayer, of concentration, of adoration; the need for an assiduous and meditated reading of the word of God; the need for a contemplative and therefore calm and relaxed rhythm in the celebration of the Eucharist and of the Liturgy of the Hours; the need for silence as an indispensable condition for achieving a deep communion and thus making our whole life a prayer. As consecrated persons we must not only pray, we must be a living prayer. One could also say: we must pray while apparently not praying. We must pray while apparently not having time to pray, but we must pray. It is another paradox [of our priestly life]. Humanly this is impossible. How can one pray while not praying? But St. Paul says that the Spirit prays in us, and then the matter turns out somewhat differently…”13. This was said by our beloved John Paul the Great. To be a living prayer… to pray while apparently not praying… because the Spirit prays in us.

That is what I would like to refer to now. To those unspoken, silent prayers; to the prayers made of our crosses and labors.

  • The unspoken prayers of the priest:

Since we as priests are not free from the infirmities of our fallen nature, notwithstanding our sublime call to the priesthood, Sacred Scripture often calls us to pray. But the truth is that many times we are not exactly very inclined to pray. And we forget the role that only the Holy Spirit plays in our sanctification.

Bad habits, acedia, lukewarmness, tiredness, all of that, as we have just said, can conspire to prevent us from raising the level of our prayer. But even in those cases the Holy Spirit, who is God, can enlighten the darkest soul and purify it thoroughly. The same happens with good souls, with souls who make spiritual exercises, who make an effort but are not completely detached. We must realize that the Holy Spirit is not indifferent to the obstacles created by man's carnal nature. And just as a nurse gently lifts the patient from his bed, so too the Holy Spirit sustains the priest in his weakness, and “puts him under cure so that he may obtain his health”14.

How many times our soul, plunged in spiritual nights, in the midst of deep afflictions, of painful anguish, rather than speaking to Christ lets out a groan. That is a good sign, a sign that one is making progress in the spiritual life. According to St. John of the Cross it is passing from the dark night of sense to the dark night of the spirit. Let me explain:

That passage the saint speaks of is marked principally by three well-defined characteristics in our prayer:

  • The first is seeing in oneself that one can no longer meditate or reason discursively with the imagination, nor take delight in it as one used to before; rather one now finds dryness in what before used to engage the senses and yield juice15.
  • The second is when one sees that one has no desire at all to fix the imagination or the senses on other particular things, exterior or interior16.
  • The third and surest is if the soul delights in being alone with loving attention to God, without particular consideration, in interior peace and quiet and rest, and without acts and exercises of the faculties, memory, understanding and will −at least discursive ones, which means going from one thing to another− but only with the general loving attention and knowledge we speak of, without particular understanding and without understanding about what.

The saint clarifies that these three signs must be present together for one to be able to be sure that one should venture to leave the state of meditation and of sense and enter that of contemplation and of the spirit. It has nothing to do with the distractions that so often assail us, nor with the lukewarmness that feels distaste for spiritual things −because here “neither does it find consolation in created things” − nor does it have to do with the melancholy that sometimes gets into the heart and does not let us think of anything, or makes us not feel like thinking about it17.

It is what in Sanjuanist language is called the night of sense, which in prayer is seen manifested by these three signs: Lack of savor in spiritual things. Not finding pleasure or consolation in the things of God implies, as we have said, “not finding it in created things either.” And that is proof that the dryness or apparent tedium does not come from sins or imperfections. There is, moreover, a painful solicitude and care about the things of God. There is a sort of constant thinking about God and that one is not serving him as one ought, and that instead of advancing one is going backward, on seeing the lack of spiritual pleasure. They are eminently spiritual crosses. We must say, for the peace of the soul passing through this night, that the dryness and lack of savor do not proceed from lukewarmness or slackness, because in fact this soul “has with it an ordinary solicitude to serve God”, but it seems to it that it is not achieving it, that it is not doing it. And in reality the cause of this dryness is that God is transferring the goods of sense to the spirit, and since the senses and natural strength are not capable of them, they remain fasting, dry and empty18. But without realizing it this soul goes on strong and more solicitous in its care not to fail God.

What is the attitude to have in these cases? The saint explains it very well: to know how to be quiet, unconcerned about any interior or exterior work, without solicitude to do anything there, [because] then in that unconcern and idleness they will delicately feel that interior refreshment19. What he means here is not to be troubled, to let God do his work: because “if it wants to work with its faculties, it rather hinders the work that God is doing in it than helps it”20. That is why he says further on that we must persevere in patience, trust in God21 and be content with a loving and calm attentiveness to God, and remain without care and without effort and without desire to taste him or feel him; because all these pretensions disquiet and distract the soul from the calm quiet and gentle idleness of the contemplation that is given here22.

I would like to emphasize that loving attentiveness to God. St. John of the Cross mentions that at first this loving attentiveness is not much noticed, but later, “the more the soul grows accustomed to letting itself be calmed, the more that loving general knowledge of God will always grow in it and be felt, which it enjoys more than all things, because it causes it peace, rest, savor and delight without labor”23. Perhaps it was for this reason that he wrote that beautiful saying: “O sweetest love of God, so little known! He who has found its veins is at rest”24. As if denoting the repose and refreshment the soul finds in that loving attentiveness to God.

Let us say it plainly: This loving attentiveness is when the soul places itself before God in an act of confused, loving, peaceful and calm knowledge, to drink wisdom and love of God, practically without doing anything other than remaining in quiet. That is when the soul passes to contemplation. In which one must apply the Sanjuanist principle that here “to enter upon the way is to leave one's own way”25.

Know that the moment will come when no effort at all is necessary to maintain this attitude: “when the soul feels itself being placed in silence and listening, even the exercise of loving attentiveness… the soul must forget, so that it may remain free for what the Lord then wants of it. For that loving attentiveness it should use only when it does not feel itself placed in solitude or interior idleness or forgetfulness or spiritual listening, which, so that it may understand it, whenever it occurs, comes with a certain peaceful calm and interior absorption”26. Notice how the Master of faith uses the principle of tanto-cuanto (insofar as): “that loving attentiveness it should use only when it does not feel itself placed in solitude or interior idleness or forgetfulness or spiritual listening”, for we must be detached even from that.

Then comes the groan. One reads in the Spiritual Canticle: “And so the peace and tranquility and satisfaction of heart which the soul can reach in this life is not enough for it to cease having a groan within itself, though peaceful and not painful, in the hope of what is lacking. For the groan is annexed to hope; like the one the Apostle27 said he and the others had, though perfect, saying: We ourselves, who have the first fruits of the spirit, groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption of sons of God. This groan, then, the soul has here within itself in its enamored heart; for where love wounds, there is the groan of the wound, crying out ever in the feeling of absence, especially when, having tasted some sweet and delightful communication of the Bridegroom, and he having gone away, it is left suddenly alone and dry”28.

Once the soul has passed from discursive meditation to contemplation, that does not mean that the work is already finished and that from now on everything is sweet and rose-colored. Here too the soul must undergo its purification. I do not know how familiar you are with the Sanjuanist writings, but as soon as one begins to read his major works, especially the Night and the Ascent, one realizes that the saint establishes a sort of parallel between night and contemplation. But one must not identify night with contemplation, because contemplation also occurs in forms that do not produce night (in the purgative sense of the word). You will have read or heard it said: “the night of contemplation”29 or “night of purgative contemplation”30 or “this night, which we say is contemplation”31, underlining that it is night because it is contemplation. For darkness is a characteristic note of contemplation during a certain period on the mystical or spiritual way, but it is not coextensive with it. I mean that one can speak of contemplation, for example, in the unitive life, or of the “now clear and beatific contemplation” of the blessed, and in so many periods in which contemplation is experienced luminously and delightfully.

But here we will speak very briefly of this contemplation in the purgative sense, of the night of contemplation.

We must say that this night of purgative contemplation disposes the soul for union with God32. It is important to keep that in mind (don’t panic). But we must also add that this night of contemplation has two principal effects on our soul: to purge and to illumine33. The well-known definition of contemplation is the one that says: “this dark night is an inflowing of God into the soul…”34 and therefore, although it is “secret”35 and “loving”36, it is also defined as “dark”, “gloomy and horrible”37 or painful at the same time.

Such a paradox gives the night of contemplation some characteristics of its own. Pay attention: “for two reasons this divine Wisdom is not only night and darkness for the soul, but also pain and torment: the first is because of the height of the divine Wisdom, which exceeds the capacity of the soul, and in this way it is darkness to it; the second, because of the soul's lowliness and impurity, and in this way it is painful and afflicting to it, and also dark.”

So it must be said that, since the light of divine Wisdom is so great and supernatural, it deprives and darkens the act of natural intelligence. That is why one speaks of the night of contemplation, because it is dark, and as dark as darkness itself. Is that clear? The pain comes, says St. John of the Cross, not from the side of God's inflowing, for there is nothing on his part that could cause pain, but the cause is the weakness and imperfection that the soul then has, and its contrary dispositions for receiving the divine communications38. Are you following me?

That is, in the light of God the soul discovers its miseries, and indeed they are greater than it thought. The soul realizes how it clashes, so to speak, with the holiness of God. “It brings out its ugliness and makes it black and dark, and so it appears worse than before and uglier and more abominable than it used to be […] it seems clear to it that it is in a bad state, that it is not only in such a state for God to see it, but for him to abhor it, and that he already abhors it”39, as St. John says in the Night. The suffering is terrible. And what is suffered in prayer is prolonged in the common fabric of life. The uncertainty, the bewilderment, the discouragement, the immense abandonment the soul experiences is unspeakable. In fact, it is one of the most horrendous and tempestuous trials of the purgation of the night of contemplation. “To find no consolation or support in any doctrine or spiritual master”; one may have read St. John of the Cross from top to bottom, have the holiest spiritual director, but that does not exempt one from that great desolation, because, as the Proper Law says, only God knows “the secret springs that must be moved to bring us to heaven”40. Even more: “the soul is so absorbed and immersed in that feeling of evils in which it sees its miseries so clearly” that others try to console it without feeling what it feels, and “instead of consolation, it rather receives new pain, it seeming to it that that is not the remedy for its ill, and in truth it is so. Because until the Lord finishes purging it in the way he wishes to do, no means or remedy serves it or profits it for its pain.” It feels “as if in a dungeon, bound hand and foot, without being able to move or see, or feel any favor from above or below”41.

In a certain way everything comes down to the violent confrontation of two contraries: God with his purity and man with his misery. And all of this, as we were saying, is manifested in daily life:

  • As an immense pain over one's own impurity: the soul knows that “it is not worthy of God nor of any creature”42.
  • Pain over its own natural, moral and spiritual weakness: it feels as “if it were under an immense burden; it is suffering and agonizing so much that it would take dying as a relief and a bargain”43.
  • It experiences pains like the shadow and groans of death through the clash of its own misery with God. It feels itself being undone, feels itself without God and punished, and cast off and unworthy of him44.
  • It also has pain because of the profound emptiness and extreme poverty in which it sees itself. It is a very great suffering that brings great anguish to the soul45. In fact it feels as if it were coming to an end, so acute are the sufferings.

But God, in his merciful and fatherly pedagogy, sends us relief; otherwise we would succumb. So, after all this we have mentioned, there follows an interpolation of reliefs46 in which the soul experiences peace and gentleness, and it even seems to it to be a sign of health and that its trials are over, but that sensation lasts only a short time, and the so sudden change in the soul even causes amazement in spiritual directors. But St. John of the Cross and other authors based on him say that the purgation, if it is to be radical, lasts “some years”47, and so after the calm the storm rages again, perhaps more intensely than before. And sometimes the pains are so many that the afflictions and straits they experience “pierce the soul in the sudden remembrance of the evils in which it sees itself, with the uncertainty of the remedy”48 –‘there is no solution to any of this’, it constantly repeats to itself− and to that evil is added the memory of past prosperity.

Abandonment on the part of creatures is particularly painful, especially if it is a matter of friends, but it is necessary so that the soul may be purified in solitude, dryness and emptiness. However, there is something that afflicts and desolates the soul even more, and that is not being able “to raise its affection and mind to God or to pray to him”49, thinking that there is a cloud in front and that its prayer does not reach him. If at some time it does pray to him, “it is so without strength and without juice that it seems to it that God neither hears it nor pays any attention to it”50. And it thinks that it is lost and its blessings finished forever51. In short, the interior combat is so great “that it brings into the spirit a pain and groan so deep that it causes it strong spiritual roarings and bellowings, sometimes uttering them with the mouth, and dissolving into tears when there is strength and power to be able to do so, though this relief is seldom given”52.

You see, then, how that groan, those interior crosses so great, so deep, how those heartfelt tears are also prayer. I knew a soul who, experiencing this inability to raise the mind to God or to pray to him, would rest her head on the joined hands of an image of Our Lady and interiorly, with that gesture, say to her: pray for me, my Mother.

The Mystical Doctor strongly recommends: “to persevere with patience and humility”53, knowing that “abandonment is a file, and suffering darkness is for great light”54. Since “the immense goods of God do not fit nor fall except into an empty and solitary heart, that is why the Lord wants you so, because he loves you well, well alone, wishing himself to be all your company”55, as the saint wrote to one of his directees.

We must not lose sight of the goods that await us: “Deep is this war and combat, because the peace it awaits must be very deep; and the spiritual pain is intimate and delicate, because the love it is to possess must also be very intimate and refined; for the more intimate and finished the work is to be and remain, the more intimate, careful and pure must be the labor, and the stronger, the more solid the building”56.

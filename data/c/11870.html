“We are not ministers of empty gestures that have no effect,”1 Fr. Buela affirmed. That is why the priest must take care that his gestures and attitudes always express a true, shared prayer of adoration, praise, offering or petition, rather than a mere performance.2

In this regard, Benedict XVI emphasizes the importance of liturgical gestures for the ars celebrandi. His published English text speaks of “The simplicity of its gestures and the sobriety of its orderly sequence of signs” and contrasts these with unnecessary embellishment.3 Indeed, “all the actions, gestures and words of the Mass are charged with profound meaning.”4

The Institute’s Directory of Liturgical Life—which we warmly recommend reading—devotes an entire section precisely to gestures at Mass. It states: “Liturgical gestures must be full, not unnecessarily repetitive, orderly and human, rather than mechanical. They must not be omitted.”5

Moreover, among the rights the faithful enjoy in relation to liturgical life, the Church includes the right to a celebration that expresses the Eucharist’s unity rather than divisions within the Church. The Institute’s proper law reproduces this teaching.6 The General Instruction of the Roman Missal likewise directs the gestures and posture of the priest, deacon, ministers and people toward a dignified, simple celebration in which the meaning of each part is clear and everyone can participate.7 The notes link to the complete published English passages.

According to Cardinal Gomá, the various gestures at Mass include the following:

Genuflection. At Mass the celebrating priest makes three genuflections: after the elevation of the Host, after the elevation of the chalice, and before Communion.8 A genuflection, made by bending the right knee to the ground, signifies adoration.9

The liturgical kiss. As a sign of veneration, the priest kisses the altar twice, at the beginning and end of Mass, and also kisses the Gospel book.

Movement of the hands. “Let us lift up our hearts with our hands to the Lord in the heavens” (Lam 3:41, Douay–Rheims), said Jeremiah. Saint Paul writes: “I will therefore that men pray in every place, lifting up pure hands” (1 Tim 2:8, excerpt, Douay–Rheims). The early Christians prayed with their hands raised, imitating, according to Prudentius, Christ’s posture on the Cross. This symbolizes the soul’s expansion and confident prayer: “Lift up your hearts” (Sursum corda), says the celebrant, raising his hands and setting his heart on God. By contrast, joining the hands, Saint Thomas says, is a sign of humble and resigned prayer. Fr. Buela develops this question of the position and movement of the hands during Mass at length in his book Ars celebrandi. There he says:

“Positions of the hands with the fingers together:

  1. Hands joined. They are placed this way when the presider addresses the assembly, for example, when saying ‘Let us pray’ before the Collect, or when introducing the Prayer of the Faithful.
  2. Hands extended in greeting. This gesture is made when greeting the assembly, for example, when saying ‘The Lord be with you.’ It is a human gesture similar to the one a person would make before embracing a friend.
  3. Hands extended in prayer. This posture is adopted for the presidential prayers. This gesture must not be made for private prayers, such as the prayer before Communion, or during the invitation addressed to the assembly before certain prayers (‘Let us pray’). This gesture must also be perceived as a human gesture directed toward heaven, with the palms almost upright and facing upward. The hands are more or less at head height, and the arms midway between the front and the sides.
  4. Hands extended in the laying on of hands. This is the gesture made for blessings and consecrations. It is a stylized form of the human gesture of gentle contact. It is used especially in Ordination, in the Reconciliation of sinners, in the Anointing of the Sick and in Confirmation. In the Eucharist, this gesture is made over the gifts when reciting the pre-consecratory epiclesis of the Eucharistic Prayer, and also over the assembly for the solemn blessing and the Prayer over the People.”10

Striking the breast. “For from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts …” (Mk 7:21, excerpt, Douay–Rheims), Jesus says. By striking our breast with our hands … we signify repentance, chastising the body at the place where the offense arose.

Thus far Cardinal Gomá. Fr. Buela also adds another gesture, among the many that could be listed:

“The gestures or ceremonies performed by the priest that accompany the account of the consecration are the very actions Christ performed.”11 That is why he instructed us as follows: “When taking the bread and the chalice, hold them slightly raised—the rubrics say this, which amounts to about a hand’s breadth. Then the rubrics say to ‘bow slightly’ (Eucharistic Prayer I, 104, etc.). Some, at that point, lower the bread and seem to hide it behind the chalice; likewise, when bowing, some lower the chalice. This seems to interrupt the act of offering that begins when taking the bread, ‘accepit panem,’ and when taking the chalice, ‘accepit calicem.’ This movement must continue after the consecration, manifesting, even visibly, the sacrificial offering’s character as an oblation. That is, it is an oblative gesture.”12

We have already said that “liturgical gestures must be full.”13 The Institute’s proper law itself gives three examples of “full gestures”:

  • Concelebration, which expresses “the unity of the sacrifice and of the priesthood,”14 and also “signifies and strengthens the fraternal bonds among priests.”15
  • The procession of the gifts, which is an “excellent moment to exercise the baptismal priesthood. Indeed, the spiritual sacrifice takes on a ritual form when the gifts of bread and wine are carried in procession to the altar.”16
  • Communion under both kinds, because “the sign of the Eucharistic banquet appears more perfectly, and the will by which the new and eternal Covenant is ratified in Christ’s Blood, as well as the link between the Eucharistic banquet and the eschatological banquet in the Father’s Kingdom, are expressed more clearly (cf. Mt 26:27–29).”17

After all that has been said, we do not wish to conclude without setting down four practical counsels from the Founder concerning priestly gestures during Mass:

  • “Do not make gestures like an automaton. Do not sway your body either.
  • Take care not to omit any gestures.
  • Never bow toward the tabernacle when reciting the prayer before proclaiming the Gospel.
  • Do not omit the gestures of the Gospel: the initial greeting, and tracing ‘the cross with the thumb on the book and then on his own forehead, mouth and breast, which all the others also do …’ (GIRM, nos. 134, 175).”18

Finally, let us always keep in mind “the doctrine of the New Law: the letter killeth: but the spirit quickeneth (2 Cor 3:6, Douay–Rheims). To place the emphasis solely on gestures, songs or outward attitudes would be like taking the life out of the celebration.”19

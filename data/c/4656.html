Every now and then people come across counterfeit dollars, and yet we do not hear anyone say that American currency is worthless. Astronomers have spotted spots on the sun, and yet we have never heard anyone deny that the sun is the light that illuminates the world. Nevertheless, we know many who seize upon the falls –not seldom scandalous ones– and the sins of a few Catholics to say: “They don’t tell you everything! The Church is the work of the devil!”

This extreme point of view begins with a fact: there are scandals. Just to illustrate, let us mention, for example, politicians who call themselves ‘Catholic’ and have an agenda quite contrary to the doctrine of the Gospel and who, in spite of this, come forward to receive Communion; high-ranking churchmen involved in cases of corruption; ‘Catholic’ women religious who support women’s ordination… and we could go on with the list. Nothing new under the sun. Already in 1937, Pius XI spoke of “that inconsistency and discontinuity in Christian life which we have lamented several times, and which causes some, while they are apparently faithful in fulfilling their religious duties, afterwards, in the field of labor, or of industry, or of their profession, or in commerce, or in their employment, through a deplorable split of conscience, to lead a life too much at odds with the clear norms of justice and Christian charity, thus giving grave scandal to the weak and offering the wicked an easy pretext to discredit the Church herself.”1

What does all this prove? It proves that our Lord espoused humanity just as it is. Christ did not expect His Mystical Body to be free of scandals, because He Himself was a stone of stumbling and was surrounded by weakness2. Venerable Fulton Sheen expressed it this way: “If our Lord’s human nature could suffer physical defeat and be a scandal, why should there not be scandals in His Mystical Body, made up of poor mortals like us?”3

We have to be realistic: “The experience of the Church is also the experience that there is evil among the men of the Church. Jesus Himself said it: there will be wheat and weeds4. If we were all wheat, the whole world would be Catholic. But there is wheat and there are weeds […]. Thus, for example, we see that in the Apostolic College there was Judas. Wheat and weeds! And it will be so until the end of time, and whoever thinks otherwise is a utopian. There is no Church of the good only. The Church is holy because her principle, her means and her end are holy. But the Church holds within her bosom sinners, who are ourselves. That is why we have to pray the ‘I confess’ at the beginning of every Mass, that is why we have to go to confession often; we are not angels, we were born with original sin, we commit many sins every day, the just man sins seven times a day5. And precisely seeing evil in the Church, which is one of the greatest temptations a Christian can have, must lead us to have more faith in Jesus Christ, because He already prophesied it, He said it two thousand years ago: there will be wheat and weeds.”6 That is, we must understand well that, as Fr. Castellani said, “in the future and perennial Church there will always be not only the just and sinners, but even heretics.”7

Even so, “hardly a week goes by in which someone does not say that the Church or one of her representatives has disappointed or seriously hurt him.”8 And although these Christians who feel wounded often become saddened, what is most frequent is that they become angry and above all feel seriously disappointed and even betrayed, because such people forget that no priest, nor religious, nor bishop, nor cardinal represents the whole Church. It is painful, no one denies it, to see members of the Church –some high-ranking churchman of the Roman Curia, some priest, religious orders– appearing in the headlines of newspapers, blogs, etc., as protagonists of some scandal, and the resulting disappointment of the people and even of the clergy themselves. And the truth is that –as Fr. Groeschel used to say– it is very likely that the closer one is to the Church, the more opportunities one has to be hurt or let down by her9.

Then one may ask oneself –whether layman, bishop, seminarian or parish priest–: “How can the Church disappoint us so often and still be the Mystical Body of Christ?” Because obviously we have sufficient reason to expect better care and a better example from the one who is the historical representative of our adorable Savior in the world.

Part of the problem is that we use the expression ‘the Church’ to describe a countless number of things that are related but that, in varying degrees, are very different from one another. Thus, for example, church can indicate a material building. It can also mean a particular Christian denomination, such as the “Orthodox Church.” It can refer to a particular parish or a diocese, and so someone may say: “I have problems with the local church.” It can refer to all those in the world who are Christians, or to all the Catholics in the world, or to a member of “the Catholic Church.”

Another source of confusion is what we understand by “member of the Catholic Church.” People often speak of the Catholics in this or that country, and we often read that a percentage of those Catholics do not agree with the Church’s norms on priestly celibacy or with what the Pope says about contraception, to give an example. And the newspapers publish these percentages in big headlines. Now, we have no idea whether those ‘Catholics’ go to Mass, or whether they contribute to the support of the Church, or what kind of catechetical education they have received. Fr. Groeschel cites the example of a young woman, a relative of his, who went to a Catholic school where she was taught not only that God was a woman, but that Jesus was a woman. And he adds: “her teachers needed therapy, long-term therapy.” The point is that you can already imagine the kind of ‘Catholicity’ of this young woman.

So one must be careful when one says “I am a member of the Catholic Church.” Because what meaning does it have when some corrupt politician or a journalist without a drop of love for the truth says: “I am Catholic”; or when some blasphemous actress says: “I am Catholic”? They are abusing the Catholic Church and claiming to have an active participation in her greater than they ever had.

1. What, then, does Church mean?

Our Proper Law defines the Church in a beautiful paragraph, saying: “The Church is Jesus Christ continued, spread abroad and communicated; she is like the prolongation of the redemptive Incarnation, continuing His threefold office: prophetic, priestly and kingly; she is the new People of God; ‘she is in Christ like a sacrament or sign and instrument of intimate union with God and of the unity of the whole human race’10; she is an organically structured community11, ‘a people brought into unity from the unity of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit’12.”13

Therefore, all the scandals –the loss of faith of religious and the conduct that flows from it, sin, corruption, heresies, schisms, sacrileges, the abuse of power by churchmen, etc.– in no way prove that the nature of the Church is not intimately divine, just as the crucifixion did not prove that Jesus Christ was not God. If our hands are dirty, it cannot be said for that reason that our whole body is. We must be convinced that the scandals that may be noticed in the Mystical Body of Christ cannot destroy the “substantial” holiness of the Church. The Church is holy and is not the subject of sins or of scandals: men are, but not the Church as such.

Ven. Fulton Sheen cites one of the greatest scandals of the Church: that of Pope Alexander VI14. And he comments: “How could a man like Alexander VI be the infallible Vicar of Christ and head of His Mystical Body? For an answer –Fulton Sheen goes on to say– let us go to the Gospel text where our Lord changes Simon’s name to Rock, and makes him the Rock on which He will build ‘His Church.’ On that same occasion our Lord made a distinction which very few people think about: He distinguished between infallibility, or immunity from error, and impeccability, or immunity from sin. Infallibility is the inability to teach what is wrong; impeccability is the inability to do what is wrong. Our Lord made the Rock infallible, not impeccable.”15

In any case, most of the time when people say they feel disappointed by the Church they are referring to the visible, exterior Church, guided by the bishops and the Pope and also by all the other persons who have some responsibility in the Church: the clergy, religious, missionaries, some institution of the Church, some parish group, etc. And it may often happen to us that we too fall into the temptation of saying: “the Church has disappointed me… the Church has failed me… or even, has betrayed me.”

According to Fr. Groeschel, it is something that happens to all of us sooner or later. “Almost every priest or religious can say the same thing, and they have legitimate complaints or grievances about something that has happened to them in their long life of service to the Church –some place where they were set aside, overlooked, despised, misunderstood, or unjustly accused–. I have now been a religious for 45 years, and I can tell you –Fr. Groeschel continues– that I often find myself annoyed with a certain sector of the Church. The possibilities of being hurt are enormous, and they are greater the more committed one is. For example, very generous people approach the Church looking for an opportunity to serve, to give of their time and energy. Perhaps they give their lives in the religious vocation. For years things go well; they are appreciated, or at least they are given the opportunity to work hard and accomplish something. But at a certain point there is a change of ‘guard.’ New leaders appear, and those of the ‘old guard’ leave. No importance is given to all they have done with minimal or almost no personal recognition. And they are overcome by the feeling that God Himself takes no account of what they have done. Then, understandably perhaps but mistakenly, they become angry with God, or with the whole Church, from the Pope on down. It is a terrible feeling. I know it. On a smaller scale this can happen to any faithful parishioner or member of the Church. They have been generous to the point of sacrifice. They have given even until it hurt, but a new pastor or administrator comes, and they are completely forgotten. They know that God did not do that, but emotionally they feel they have been rejected.”16

Perhaps one of the worst experiences in this sense is when ‘someone from the Church’ teaches one of our loved ones something contrary to the teachings of the Gospel and of the Church, and that loved one of ours finally ends up going astray. How many were taught that there was no need to go to confession, or were encouraged to embrace contraception ‘to be careful,’ to cite just a few examples, or were told that it was all right to enter a new union after the failure of their own marriage, the marriage being valid.

It must be admitted that those who occupy some position of authority in the Church are sometimes quite limited in what they can do, more than ordinary people assume. We see it, for example, in the Roman Curia, where alongside much holiness in many there is also a remarkable incompetence in some. But even so, the feeling is that the Church has disappointed them, or let them down.

2. Wounded by “the Church”

We must be very clear that when we say that ‘the Church’ or the leaders of the Church disappoint us, we obviously do not mean the Mystical Body of Christ. It is not the Incarnate Word, who is one mystical person with His Bride, who fails us. It was perhaps someone who exercises some authority in the Church, but not the Church.

And the reason why ‘the Church’ disappoints us is simply because she is made up of human beings. Indeed, if we think about it, the Church is a great community of people with original sin and with personal sins. We are not referring here to the Church triumphant, nor to the saints, nor to the Magisterium and Tradition of the Church, but to that human side of the Church which can wound us and which in fact often has done so and does so. It happens to all of us; it has happened to the saints too, and the testimonies of events of this kind in the history of the Church are overwhelming.

“One example is enough to show it,” so let us consider some examples of people who at one time or another were seriously wounded by “the Church.”

Padre Pio of Pietrelcina, for example, remained practically under house arrest for decades by order of the Holy See. He never left the little village where he lived, San Giovanni Rotondo. Never! He was even forbidden for years to celebrate Mass in public.

Another example: Blessed Solanus Casey17, a Capuchin priest, was never able to hear confessions. And he preached a sermon only once or twice. He first entered a diocesan seminary, and after four years of formation he was dismissed because they thought he did not have the intellectual capacity to be a priest. He did not contradict them and went back home. Then he entered the Capuchins and also did poorly in his studies (both seminaries gave their classes in German and Latin, and he knew only English, since he was the son of Irish immigrant farmers), and his superiors too thought that he could not be ordained a priest, but his spiritual life –as they perceived it– was quite advanced. That led his superiors to decide to admit him as a candidate for the priesthood, but not as Solanus would have wished. He was ordained a simplex sacerdos, as priests who only said Mass were called at that time. He could not preach or hear confessions or give absolution, nor could he ever be a superior or vote in the chapters of his order. People knew him as ‘the brother who says Mass.’ A thousand times in his life he must have had to explain why he could not hear confessions. His first assignment was to a parish in Yonkers, New York. The pastor, not knowing how to employ a priest who could neither hear confessions nor preach, gave him the office of sacristan and porter. Those who knew him say that it was the acceptance of that humiliation that made him a saint and turned him into the best Capuchin who ever lived on American soil.

Going back in time, we find St. Alphonsus Liguori, now honored as a Doctor of the Church, who was forced out of the order he himself founded, the Redemptorists, so that it would not be suppressed. And no small detail is the fact that he was not allowed to celebrate Mass in the Papal States even though he was a bishop. Incredible! St. Joan of Arc was burned at the stake by sentence of the Bishop of Beauvais and 11 theologians. In the prison tower of Rouen one can see, on one side, the decree of condemnation that led to the execution of St. Joan, and on the other side of the tower is the papal decree which, 20 years later, exonerated her and condemned the judges. Although she appealed to the Pope, the bishop did not grant her request, and he himself incurred an ecclesiastical censure.

And how many more! So many! Countless examples we could give; in a particular way this has happened to a great many founders of religious orders.

3. An incredible story

Fr. Groeschel relates “an incredible story,” as he himself titles it. It is the story of a bishop who was “terribly wounded by the Church for 30 years and who lived in New York: Bonaventure Brodrick18. He worked as vicar for religious in the Archdiocese of New York from 1940 to 1943. However, Bishop Brodrick earned his living for most of his life by running a gas station. Until the new, super-modern gas stations appeared, there used to be a nice little device at the end of the pump nozzle that automatically stopped the gasoline when the tank was full. This device was invented and patented by Bishop Bonaventure Brodrick. He lived in part on what he earned thanks to this invention.

This story goes back to the time after the Spanish-American War, during which the United States occupied Cuba. For some reason it was decided to make an American priest, Father Brodrick as he was until then, auxiliary bishop of Havana. Bishop Brodrick went to Cuba, and shortly afterward the Cubans decided that they did not want an American bishop. So they sent him back to New York, but no one needed an auxiliary bishop. The archdiocese then had to find him a job. They put him in charge of the annual collection for the Holy See, but no one wanted a bishop in that post, so he was left without work. After a long wait he wrote a letter suggesting that it might be scandalous for a bishop to be without work. The answer came: ‘Wait.’ And he waited. To earn a living he opened a gas station.

Decades later Francis Spellman was appointed Archbishop of New York. As the story goes, Pope Pius XII asked him to find out what had happened to Bishop Brodrick. No one in the archdiocese had any idea what had become of him, but they found an old address in a town north of New York. Archbishop Spellman drove there himself, to that address. It was a gas station. When he arrived he got out and asked the young man who was attending the station: ‘Who owns this gas station?’ The young man answered: ‘Doc Brodrick.’ The archbishop asked where he lived. The young man pointed out a little house nearby. The man who would soon be ‘Cardinal Spellman’ went there and knocked on the door, and an old man dressed in overalls came out. ‘Bishop Brodrick?’ The man answered: ‘Yes.’ He said, ‘I am Archbishop Spellman, and I have come to see if I can do something for you.’ To which the old man replied: ‘Come in, I have been waiting for you for thirty years.’ Archbishop Spellman made him auxiliary bishop of New York and vicar for religious.” “I do not know of any other person who was so hurt by the Church,” states Fr. Groeschel.

The obvious question is: how can the Church of Christ disappoint us? How can this happen in the Church founded by Christ? If the Church is supposed to nourish and guard our faith, how is it that she herself becomes a trial for our faith? We find the answer in the Gospel. One need only look at what happened to the Apostles: they failed Christ when He most needed them. On the very day He made them His sacred representatives, they fled from Him. Every year, on Holy Thursday, we celebrate the day of the Catholic priesthood. And if we think about it, it is also the day on which the first priests terribly failed Christ. On the very evening on which they were instituted alter Christus, on that same evening they fled. Does that not tell us something about “the Church” (that is, about the men of the Church)? As we mentioned before, the Catholic Church is made up of more than a billion people with original sin19. That is already a lot of sin. And those people do extraordinarily good things, and some of them do extraordinarily bad things. Only in eternal life are the members of the Church perfect, reflecting with that same perfection what the Church, the Mystical Body of Christ, really is: as St. Paul says, without spot or wrinkle20. Not so those of us who are still wayfarers or pilgrims.

This world is full of situations that are simply absurd, and here we mean the world of believers and of non-believers. “In my life –states Fr. Groeschel– I have known foolish Jesuits, confused Dominicans, proud Capuchins, rich Franciscans and Salesians who could not stand children. I have known Sisters of Mercy without mercy, Missionaries of Charity without charity, and stupid Daughters of Wisdom. […] Go and visit Rome. They say it is the city where the communists pray and the prelates do not. Everything in this world is a bit of a mess, and sometimes it is better to laugh than to cry. When you think you have everything perfect and planned out in life, you are surely missing some important pieces of the puzzle. Because life is mysterious.”21

4. So, what is to be done?

The vast majority of us –at the level of the Institute and at the personal level– have at some time felt let down and perhaps even ‘betrayed’ by ‘the Church,’ that is, by members of the Church, as we have explained. And when that happens, the first thing to do is to calm down. That is a very good rule to keep in mind when we are hurt by anyone: the superior, the sacristan, the lady from the Legion of Mary, my companion on mission, the bishop’s secretary –in short, anyone. The Irish have a saying: “take counsel with your pillow” (Take counsel with your pillow). And once we have calmed down, then we should ask ourselves: Do I really have to make a problem of this? Am I not perhaps expecting too much from mortal and fallible beings like myself? Is what I am seeking or expecting from those men of the Church really legitimate? The answer will very probably be “yes.” And it will very probably be reasonable and even just. But the point is that I cannot demand absolutely kind, respectful or attentive treatment, because Christ Himself did not receive it from the first members of the very Church He established. Moreover, Jesus Christ Himself warned us: in the world you will have to suffer22.

On the other hand, does our Proper Law not tell us that “we must consider ourselves ‘deserving of all afflictions’”23? Well then, among those afflictions are also included ill treatment, injustices, slander, unjustified suspicions, people speaking ill of us, the misinterpretation of our intentions, humiliations at the hands precisely of those who ought to help us, opposition to or obstruction of all our undertakings… and each one could add something to the list.

We have said it before: the Church is composed of weak and fallible individuals like ourselves. And therefore the members of the Church can be very good one day and very bad the next. Even on the same day and in the same parish, in the same diocese, in the same Vatican office, we can find people who are incredibly charitable and others who are terribly cruel.

Moreover, it is true –and we mention it so that we too may educate others in this– that these ‘disappointments’ on the part of ‘the Church’ are necessary as a purification of our faith. Many –truly many– were educated in Catholic schools, made the Spiritual Exercises, went on mission to this or that place, and did extremely well. We ourselves –as an Institute– have been helped by many in the Church –St. John Paul II, to look no further, and so many others like him– and the experience was more than positive and hopeful, and that is very good. But we must not think that this will always be so. All earthly things pass away. And just as when we experience any consolation we must apply St. Ignatius’ 10th rule of discernment for the First Week of the Exercises, so also when we are consoled by ‘the Church’: “let the one who is in consolation think how he will behave in the desolation that will come afterward, gathering new strength for that time.” God permits these trials so that we may love Him more and be of greater worth. We depend on God –not on a person, an office, a section of the Church– but on the very Incarnate Word Himself.

How many religious, how many priests become exceedingly distressed over ‘the contradictions of the good’ –and although their concern is quite justified– fall into the temptation of seeing only the evil ‘that they do to us’ or how ‘bad off the Church is,’ losing sight of the forest of things that are going well24, which ends in the terrible experience of feeling greatly let down or disappointed. It is a problem, a temptation against faith in the Church, the Mystical Body of Christ indissolubly united to her Head, who has espoused her25. When this temptation draws near to the doors of our soul and faith is more sorely tested, it is good to reread that passage from one of the letters of St. John of the Cross where he says: “What do you want? What life or way of proceeding do you picture for yourself in this life? […] what is there to get right but to go by the level road of the law of God and of the Church, and only to live in dark and true faith (and certain hope and whole charity, and to await) our goods there, living here as pilgrims, poor, exiles, orphans, dry, without a road and without anything, hoping for everything there?”26

The case of St. John of the Cross is truly paradigmatic and exemplary. After initiating the Carmelite reform under the direction of St. Teresa of Jesus, his life “gives the impression of a man continually displaced and marginalized. A continual pilgrimage in solitude: ‘exiled and alone,’ as he would say in a letter of 1581 to Mother Catalina de Jesús from Baeza.”27

“To this day the specific offense is unknown with which Fray Juan de la Cruz was charged and for which he was given the treatment prescribed in the Constitutions for ‘those guilty of the offense of rebellion,’ but very probably it was because he was judged an accomplice in the rebellion of the nuns of the Incarnation when they elected St. Teresa as prioress against the instruction and command of the provincial who was presiding over the election.”28 Many of you already know that during his imprisonment in Toledo “they would bring him down to the refectory, while the friars were there, three or four times, so that he might receive the discipline there.”29 After escaping from the Toledo prison, speaking with Ana de San Bartolomé about his unjust imprisonment, the saint stated: “When God wants to make saints, He makes executioners of their own brothers, or of the sons against the fathers, so that they may become saints. And in these times He has used these means, as is manifest.”30

Not for nothing does our Proper Law also warn us, saying: “Woe to superiors who destroy by example what they preach by word!”31, and the same can be said of any subject. For “every counter-witness, every inconsistency between how values or ideals are expressed and how they are actually lived, every seeking of oneself and not of the Kingdom of God and His justice32, every falsification of the word of God”33 not seldom demoralizes, scandalizes, and makes the perseverance of others difficult.

Even so, St. John of the Cross never held a grudge against the prior. Indeed, he himself, as one who has suffered the file and abandonment at the hands of superiors, would later write in the third precaution against the world –which can very well be applied to any member or sector of the Church, including our own congregation, and helps us know how to conduct ourselves in these cases we have been discussing–: “never be scandalized or astonished at things you see or hear, striving to keep your soul in forgetfulness of all that. For if you want to look into anything, even if you live among angels, many things will seem not right to you, because you do not understand their substance […]. [And] even if you live among demons, God wants you to live among them in such a way that you do not even turn the head of your thought to their affairs, but leave them entirely, striving to keep your soul pure and whole in God, without a thought of this or that hindering you.”34 And in the second precaution against the devil he says: “never look upon your prelate with any less regard than upon God, whoever the prelate may be, for you have him in God’s place. And so watch with great vigilance that you do not look at his temperament, nor his manner, nor his bearing, nor other ways of proceeding of his; for you will do yourself so much harm that you will come to change obedience from divine to human, being moved only by the ways you see visibly in the prelate and not by the invisible God whom you serve in him. And your obedience will be vain, or all the more fruitless, the more you are weighed down by the prelate’s adverse temperament or lightened by his good temperament.”

There is more than a grain of truth in all that the Master of Fontiveros says. For often it is the people we love who hurt us the most. Those we do not love cannot hurt us much. And that is why we must not think that we are exempt from hurting others. Our Proper Law draws our attention to this when it speaks of the damage caused by grumbling among the members of the Institute: “The brother defamed before those of the world can console himself with the satisfaction he feels in enjoying the esteem and trust of his brothers; but if he sees himself denigrated among his own, among those with whom he is obliged to live, community life becomes unbearable for him, unless he is endowed with extraordinary virtue.”35

Does the counsel of the Mystical Doctor mean that we must renounce the moral sense, renounce discerning moral good and evil? No. “Nowhere in the Gospel message does forgiveness, nor even mercy as its source, mean indulgence toward evil, toward scandal, toward the injury or the outrage committed.”36 “If an injustice is done to me, I have to see that it is an injustice; what must not be done is to condemn the other person straightaway to hell. […] Before granting trust or distrust to a person one must think; and for that one must suspend judgment, not rush it; that is, one must exercise precaution.”37

St. John of the Cross did not die an embittered or sad man, even though, after having been first general councilor of the order, his brothers –those to whom he himself had given the habit– gravely discredited him. No one defended him. The very superior of the friary of La Peñuela, where he went to die, removed from his office by a formal precept the infirmarian who was caring for him, because he did not like the attentions this friar showed to Fray Juan. He also forbade the religious at that time to visit him often, so that there would be no lapse in ‘recollection and silence,’ and he did nothing but complain about the poverty of the friary, as if to say: and on top of it all they bring me this sick man here.

I suppose we can say that ‘the Church’ –or that little part of the Church that was most important and most dear to him– disappointed him or, in a certain way, betrayed him. The saint suffered so much that it could well be said that his religious life was a martyrdom, as painful as the one he so fervently desired. But he remained at peace, and so it was that during this time he wrote to a nun: “Love very much those who contradict you and do not love you, for in this way love is begotten in the breast where there is none; as God does with us, who loves us so that we may love Him by means of the love He has for us.”38

Great dangers are currently looming over the Church. Therefore, we need to see things in their real perspective. There are those who complain about the music at Mass, or go about worried about forests and plants, or about whether friars wear patched habits or not, while the Church faces a hurricane that shakes her on all sides, before which she will never fail, because she is indefectible39. The conduct of such people resembles that of those passengers who were playing on the deck of the Titanic while it was sinking. This is a time in which one must be faithful to the Church. This means that, as we have said at other times, these are wonderful times to be alive. “The Church is the incarnation of Christ as the incarnate Christ is the incarnation of God. […] Christ loved the Church and gave Himself up for her, to consecrate her, cleansing her with water and the word, so that He might present her to Himself all glorious, without spot or wrinkle or anything of the kind, but holy and immaculate. […] Christ relives His life in the Church. […] We do not see the beauty hidden in His Mystical Body any more than we would have seen the glory hidden in His physical Body.

The Body of Christ has today become the target in a game of theological darts. While the secularists say ‘God is dead,’ the necro-ecclesiologists solemnly proclaim: ‘The Church is dead.’ For some she is too holy; for others too human; in the same way that Caiaphas rejected Christ because He claimed to be Divine, and Pilate crucified Him because He was not sufficiently one with Caesar.

[…] The law of the Body is the law of the Head: Crucifixion and the Empty Tomb. […] Now Christianity is under attack. That means that these are wonderful days to be alive40. Now we have to stand up and be counted. It is easy to float downstream. Corpses float down the river. But living bodies are needed to resist the current. […] True Catholic life is not made up of routine acts of piety, but of a crisis that presents us with a great choice […] That is why these are splendid days to be alive. We can make decisions that will have repercussions in eternity.”41

5. Working to be wheat

For this reason it falls to us to work to be wheat, not weeds. Moreover, our Proper Law explicitly prescribes that “for our part, we must do everything possible so that the face of the Church may be without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but holy and blameless42.”43 Faithful to God’s gift, we must know how to ‘bear fruit.’ We must be attentive, because the Lord wants us to be fruitful. We must take care of the wheat but not lose our peace over the weeds. The sower, when he sees the weeds sprouting in the midst of the wheat, does not react with complaints or alarm. He finds a way for the Word to become incarnate in a concrete situation and bear fruits of new life, even though in appearance they may be imperfect or unfinished. The disciple knows how to give his whole life and stake it even to martyrdom as a witness to Jesus Christ, but his dream is not to fill himself with enemies, but that the Word be welcomed and manifest its liberating and renewing power44.

Let us learn the lesson, then! All the members of the Institute, whether still in the houses of formation, in a monastery or on some mission, have the privilege of dealing with many people, many of them with different lifestyles, with different levels of education, with different problems, and who approach us for different reasons. And it is true that some people are more difficult than others, just as there are also people who are very virtuous and holy. In any case, for most of them we are the face of the Church… and we cannot fail them. If we do not treat them well, it may seem to them that the Church does not treat them well; if we show no interest in them, it is as if the Church showed no interest in them or in their worries and ailments; if we do not do our work, it is as if the Church did not do her work. We cannot let any of the souls who come to the doors of our parishes, our missions, our monasteries, our seminaries, etc., feel abandoned, ignored, misunderstood, or be sent away without further explanation. Because most of the time we are dealing with the deepest part of their souls: their longing for God… their faith. And this also applies among ourselves, within our Religious Family: we must live charity with one another to the point of giving our lives for others, as St. John exhorts us: by this we have come to know what love is: that He laid down His life for us. We too must lay down our lives for the brothers45.

Fulton Sheen said: “Love and service are inseparable. Service to others is the highest service to oneself, and the best way for any man to grow in grace is to advance along the path of serviceableness. The mill wheel stops grinding when the waters of the torrent are cut off; the moving train comes to a halt when the incandescent heat hidden within the chamber cools; and charity in this world will degenerate into mere professional timetables and statistical averages, without inspiration, without power and without love, if we forget the inspiration of Him who said: No one can have greater love than to give his life for his friends46.”47

Therefore, at the same time that our Constitutions point out that “the Institute of the Incarnate Word recognizes in the Supreme Pontiff the first and supreme authority and professes to him not only obedience, but also fidelity, filial submission and adherence” 48, they express our “availability for the service of the universal Church.”49 And later the Directory of Spirituality, quoting St. Teresa of Jesus, tells us: “For this (the salvation of souls) He has gathered you here; this is your vocation and your desire, this is the reason for your tears and your prayers… The day on which your prayers, disciplines, longings and fasts are not devoted to what I have told you, you will not attain –know it well– the end for which the Lord has gathered us here.”50 This means that our prayers, our holiness of life and all our missionary work are directed to the good of the universal Church, and in particular there is entrusted to us the Church in the land where we carry out our mission and toward which we have duties and responsibilities. Therefore, we must contribute not only with the strength of prayer but with arduous missionary work to cultivate the local Church and to gladden her with the fruits of a loving faith. This being wheat in the Church of our Lord implies bearing witness to the primacy of Christ, to that “primacy which Christ has over the souls and over the bodies of the members of His Mystical Body and also over all men of all times.”51 This implies bearing witness to the primacy of the charity of God wherever we may be and whatever office may have been assigned to us in the Mystical Body of Christ. And as we know very well, witness means ‘martyr,’ and the Church is faithful in martyrdom, in witness. That is why we must feel ourselves sharers in that mission, especially when it falls to us to suffer those lacerating setbacks from ‘the Church’ that so bewilder us, when ecclesial scandals pile up in the newspaper headlines, when the one we expected to guide us leaves us even more confused and perplexed…

Yes, Fr. Groeschel states, ‘the Church’ has wounded or disillusioned many who were close to her throughout her existence –not the whole Church, but part of her, members of her. However, we must not forget that we are part of the Church militant, not of the Church triumphant. Now we are preparing ourselves for that, to be one day part of the heavenly Church. But if we do not strive to be faithful to the Church in this world, and to be faithful even when others are not… then everything else we do is of no use, since we simply will not be working for the service and honor of God. The service of Jesus Christ is the same as the service of the Church, which is He Himself prolonged in history.

These are difficult times and we must be “ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God,”52 states our Directory of Spirituality. Sometimes we imagine that martyrdom must come to us in a bloody form, as it did to the martyrs of Barbastro, to Jerzy Popiełuszko or to St. Maximilian Kolbe. However, let all be persuaded that remaining faithful to the Church, to her Magisterium, to the living Tradition of the Church, to the charism of the Institute approved by the Church herself, implies nowadays its share –at times a very great one– of martyrdom.

St. John of the Cross himself teaches this: “A soul has great desires to be a martyr. It may happen that God answers it, saying: You will be a martyr, and gives it interiorly great consolation and confidence that it will be one. And nevertheless, it may happen that it does not die a martyr, and the promise will still be true. How, then, was it not fulfilled in that way? Because it will be fulfilled, and can be fulfilled, according to what is principal and essential in it, which will be by giving it the love and reward of a martyr essentially; and so He truly gives the soul what it formally desired and what He promised it. For the formal desire of the soul was not that manner of death, but to render God that service of a martyr and to exercise love for Him as a martyr.

For that manner of dying is in itself worth nothing without this love, which love, and the exercise and reward of a martyr, He gives it very perfectly by other means; so that, even though it does not die as a martyr, the soul is very satisfied in that He gave it what it desired. For such desires, when they are born of living love, and others like them, even if they are not fulfilled in the way people picture and understand them, are fulfilled for them in another way, much better and more to the honor of God than they knew how to ask.”53

May the Most Holy Virgin, Mother of the Church and Queen of martyrs, grant us the grace to guard ourselves against the enemy who harms and deceives. It is true that we cannot prevent the enemy from getting up to his tricks, but we can prevent his tricks from becoming ours.

Introduction

  1. When St. Thomas speaks of the evangelical counsels, he insists on the adverb “totally” as what distinguishes counsels from precepts. The history of the Apostles, the first religious, opens with a relictis omnibus –having left all things–, which the Gospel of St. Luke underlines (cf. 5:11). Turning aside toward worldly things poisons the love that we must direct to God in all its purity. Always in this negative, ascetical aspect of religious life, there is only one attitude: it is necessary to destroy totally the attachment to the things of the world; in other words: attachment to earthly things holds back the impulse toward the ideal, preventing man from giving himself totally to the service of God. The step grows lighter by getting rid of that ballast: one arrives more quickly by totally renouncing all the goods of this world. No other is the function reserved to the three vows, which impose detachment from creatures: to abandon totally the three kinds of things that bind us to the world, insofar as possible, belongs to the evangelical counsels.
  2. It is not enough to imitate halfway the example of the Twelve: as St. Thomas Aquinas says, “to abandon all things is not enough for perfection; one must abandon everything in order to follow Christ.” Now, if the abandonment is total, the self-giving must be total: “those who live in the world give to God part of their things, keeping the rest for themselves… But those who live in the religious state consecrate themselves totally, in person and in goods, to God”1. But since man cannot dispose at one moment of the totality of his life, being subject to the laws of time, so as to offer it all at once to his God as a holocaust, he strives to overcome the rhythm of time and to fix his will by means of the vow: “This immobility in the following of Christ is secured by the vow”2.

1. Nature

  1. Since what has been said above is proper to religious life, let us now look at the objectives proper to its initial stage, the novitiate.

Canonically: “The novitiate, with which life in an Institute begins, has as its purpose that the novices understand more fully the divine vocation, particularly that which is proper to the Institute, that they experience its way of life, that they conform their mind and heart to its spirit, and that their intention and suitability may be tested”3.

  1. Two groups of very precise ends can be seen: on the part of the novice and on the part of the Institute.

With the novitiate the life of an Institute begins, the life of the novice begins, but the Institute also grows and is enriched with the life of its novices.

a) The novitiate is a time of beginnings

5. This is historical-temporal but, above all, pedagogical and progressive. As a beginning, it implies a break with one’s previous life. This break is carried out through the commitments that the novice has taken on: beginning to live in a place designated for this purpose4, the reception of the habit, the unity of the time of novitiate ensured by its duration5, by the formation program6 and, above all, by the doctrinal and ascetical-religious load to which the novice will be subjected7.

6. Objection: Could not the beginning of religious life, the search for God, the knowledge of oneself and of the Institute be attained more perfectly by living in a community already established, already under way? Would it not be better to begin religious life by observing the conduct of other, senior religious? To see their example, their way of understanding and living the laws and customs. There, activities are already under way, and one would only need to fit into them. Would one not save the time lost in teaching the customs, the schedules, the way of living silence and prayer? Would one not avoid a great number of the improvisations typical of the early days?

7. The objections seem to be confirmed during the first days of the novitiate, in which progress is very slow and it takes much effort and patience to bear the failings and mistakes proper to the beginning. There is still no religious style. Silence is not yet appreciated, there are no study habits, the liturgy is not very solemn, the schedules are not kept exactly, and there is improvisation in the work, the kitchen, etc… Depending on the temperament of the candidates there are many faults, disorders, lack of punctuality, lack of responsibility in the duties and in the use and care of things; which causes annoyance to those who are more organized, discouragement, etc. Normally this is what happens in the first days.

8. Response: But the good of the novitiate must not be sought in its beginnings; rather, it is a longer-term work. In the novitiate what is sought is the beginning of religious life and its maturing. In the supernatural life progress is slow; development happens in the manner of a seed, in the manner of leaven. It is a maturing, and to want to hurry it is like picking a fruit from its tree and forcing it from outside to ripen. Inside it will remain green, and the outside will be only a peel or a façade, but without substance.

9. In a senior house, everything is already functioning, the laws are kept, the schedules carry one along, the bells signal, and the religious show how the works are to be done. It may happen that some candidate believes that by adapting to what is already established everything is done; that by acquiring the manners and behavior of the others he is already a religious; that by the faithful fulfillment of exterior works he is advancing by leaps and bounds toward holiness. These things are not bad; the evil would lie in neglecting the search for God and self-knowledge and settling for “doing things well.” One goes about dressing oneself as a religious, but it may happen that the interior remains still immature.

10. Interior maturing: The novitiate seems to be the most suitable place to begin this interior maturing. In this house the novice is waiting for what his Master is going to teach him. As for the exterior works, they are generally riddled with imperfections and defective. It will be the task of the formator to point out very frequently that holiness does not lie there, that doing things well comes in second place and will be the consequence of the love of God that is gradually acquired. He must repeat the end of the novitiate: the search for God; and the two wings of that search: prayer and penance in the atmosphere of fraternal charity in which one must live. The gaze of the novices must be fixed on this, knowing how to transcend exterior appearances.

11. Nevertheless, the Novice Master must be very attentive to everything that happens, for he must not neglect or let pass the opportune moment for teaching. The mere passing of time does not form; on the contrary, it can lead people to think that disorder is accepted, that things are done halfway, that sloppy behavior is the style, and this breeds laziness and gradually undermines great ideals.

12. Exclusive concern: Let us look at other advantages of the novitiate. The Code of Canon Law8 asks for a house distinct from the other houses of formation. The purpose is to live dedicated purely and exclusively to beginning religious life. The concerns must not be the same as those of the rest of the religious. It is fitting that one know what will later be done or studied, the virtues lived in the major Seminary, and even that one know the problems that may arise, but without suffering them; that the atmosphere be one of tranquility, that one long to move on to the major seminary, but that in the meantime one live with one’s eyes fixed on one’s own objectives.

13. In order to procure the necessary tranquility and calm for the novice, it should be noted that “it is inadvisable for the novitiate to take place in an environment foreign to the culture and native language of the novices. Indeed, small novitiates are preferable, provided that they are rooted in this culture. The essential reason is not to multiply problems during a stage of formation in which the fundamental balances of the person must find their proper place, and in which relations between the novices and the Novice Master must be easy”9.

14. It is also an occasion to call forth generosity and maturity, since responsibilities must be taken on from the first moment. A religious atmosphere of holiness must be created, and if the novices do not do it, no one else will.

15. There is the continual assistance of the priest, who, being dedicated exclusively to this, can observe all things and point out in due time the defects he sees, and give the encouragement and suggestions suited to each person and moment.

16. Finally, the novitiate is a well-defined stage, in which the candidate must be filled with the desire to become a senior religious. Living it well and completing it produces psychologically in the young man a conviction of strength and a healthy confidence that he will be able to face any later stage of his formation.

b) First objective: a better knowledge of the vocation

17. Search for God: Life must be directed to an exclusive and supernatural search for God who calls, and to Whom one must respond. “Si revera Deum quaerit,” St. Benedict admonishes10 those who asked to enter his monastery. This is the principal occupation of the novice: to seek God, the God revealed by faith. One does not enter in order to dedicate oneself to Science, to Philosophy, to Theology, nor to Art, nor to train oneself for the apostolate or for educational work. It is true that we must serve God with the talents He gives us, but these works are only means to a higher end, and our end is God in Himself.

18. Bearing in mind what has already been said, this first House of formation must not be, from any point of view, a mere boarding house for students. Nor does study have a principal role in it –its place is important in order to know Christ and the foundations of the spiritual life; the subjects taught are of a clearly spiritual character11–. Nor is its strong point the apostolate12, or manual work. All these are means for finding God.

19. Not even perfection in action should be set as the goal: doing everything perfectly, organized, finished… That is an ornament of exterior action, but the end is more interior: it is to know and love Jesus. The end is not to form well-mannered or very obedient people; nor to form them according to the taste and manner of the Superior. Each one must know Christ in order to imitate Him in what God has given him as his particular vocation or his own way of holiness. Vocations cannot be treated as though they were identical or carbon copies.

20. Furthermore, one must not be confused by setting as the ultimate end the practice of the counsels, as if we entered religious life in order to be poor, or to live chastity, or to renounce our own will. These elements untie us from earthly loves so as to allow us to love God alone. But to place exclusive emphasis on poverty, chastity or obedience as ultimate objectives is to confine souls to works that are negative in themselves, to divert their spiritual aims.

21. We insist a little more on the search for God. For it to be sincere it must be exclusive; God must be sought for Himself, not for His gifts or His consolations. God wants us to taste how sweet it is to serve Him (cf. Ps 33:9). To seek anything else is not to find everything in God; it is not to be able to say with St. Paul: I count all things as loss… and I consider them rubbish in order to gain Christ (Phil 3:8).

22. In every community there are various kinds of souls. Some live joyfully, radiating around themselves the holy joy they enjoy. Not the merely sensible joy, which may depend on temperament, but the interior joy that is a prelude to eternal happiness. Others do not enjoy it; within they are troubled, restless, unhappy. Why this difference? Because, in the first case, the one who seeks God in all things always finds Him and everywhere, and with God he acquires the supreme good and the happiness that is not lost. In the second case, on the other hand, the soul clings to creatures, feeds self-love, and in seeking itself heads toward emptiness, and this cannot content it. It will give itself entirely to exterior occupation, but that distraction will be illusory and insufficient; it will find distraction but not consolation, and it will feel its life to be heavy and hard to bear.

23. St. Bernard asked himself: “ad quid venisti?”13, why have I left the world, separated myself from what I loved, renounced my freedom, made so many sacrifices? Inventa una pretiosa margarita vendidit omnia quae habuit et emit eam (Mt 13:46).

c) Second objective: knowledge of oneself

24. Knowledge of oneself: Self-knowledge is of capital importance at the beginning. In the novitiate the spiritual edifice must be begun, and it is fundamental to know with what materials one is building.

This knowledge has two moments: knowledge of one’s defects and limitations; and knowledge of the gifts received.

  1. Of one’s talents: As for the recognition of the gifts received, talents and potentialities, it is necessary that the Master know how to open fields of action, doors, paths along which the novices can travel. In the intellectual order, to put them in contact with great works, great writers, encouraging them to read them, showing them in various ways the importance of knowing them.
  2. To introduce them to the knowledge of the different branches of learning: Philosophy, Scripture, Theology, Moral Theology, through the established subjects and, in addition, through participation in conferences or other activities which, even if they exceed their capacity of understanding, open up horizons for them.

There should be planned: convivium, disputatio, study groups and various means of intellectual motivation.

  1. In the order of conduct it is also necessary that they take flight, valuing and using their moral gifts. Since the novitiate is a family composed exclusively of novices, besides its few formators, it is they who will have to carry out the various activities: work, kitchen, finances, etc. Here it will depend on the ability of the formator to find the right person for the right post and then to delegate responsibilities to him. He must show trust so that each one may grow firm in his responsibilities. Seeing everything, he will know how to intervene only in what is important.
  2. Of one’s defects: But perhaps in the early period it is more important to try to know one’s own defects in order to pull up the weeds.

For this the novitiate is a more than favorable time. The community is still small, and if not in number, at least all are dedicated to the same thing, without as great a variety of objectives as in another religious house. It is a family, and all are in view of the Superior. It is very difficult for him not to find out what is happening. Besides, he has considerably more time to attend to them spiritually and show them their errors or deviations. He can use more personal means in teaching. The situation of novices is different from that of a senior religious. They have not yet formed their judgment and are therefore in need of everything, ready to receive any counsel or call to attention.

  1. But, above all, the greatest good with regard to knowledge of one’s own faults and defects is provided by daily life in community itself. In a novitiate one always starts from zero; nothing is set up, the human group is not at all homogeneous: different origins, families, customs, cultures, which become evident within a few days. Having to make decisions, act on one’s own, organize activities14 which in other religious houses are reserved to someone with several years of experience; when all this happens, one very soon runs into failures, friction with companions, discouragement…, which gives occasion to touch one’s own personal limitations. It is the duty of the Superior to keep his finger on the pulse of the situation and to be very attentive to everything, so as to know how to stop whatever might be serious and to help them draw experience from the rest. For this he must not rebuke the bad deed in an agitated way, but point out the root from which it proceeds, so that one may thus come to know one’s own limits and defects.

d) Third objective: formation of the mind
and heart with the Institute

  1. This objective is a sibling of the preceding ones, because life is the manifestation of the spirit. It concerns the totality of the person, according to his cognitive and affective, mental and heartfelt, theoretical and experiential, interior and operative dimensions.
  2. Forming privately: The Novice Master will give formation in two ways. In personal dialogue he will be very clear and will teach firmly that, although the important thing is to find God in prayer and to offer oneself in penance, that encounter and identification with Christ must be practiced in daily life. Identifying ourselves with Christ must lead us to act as He did, of whom it is said that He did all things well (Mk 7:37). There is no room, then, for doing things halfway, nor for living in neglect; rather, one must be master of what surrounds us. Still less may one be careless with what belongs to others, causing harm to third parties or to the common good. In this the Master will be clear and demanding, and will give him material to work on in shaping his habits. In private he may call his attention, even scold him when carelessness, listlessness or negligence is seen, and impose punishments.
  3. Forming in community: But the way of forming is different with regard to carrying the community forward. There he must be much more patient. He must indicate the way to do things, show the advantage of doing them well, the advantage of being orderly or punctual… He may, jokingly, ridicule defects in order to make them contemptible. But it is not advisable for him to use scolding or anger, spending his authority in correcting defects at every moment; on the contrary, his insistence will be on pointing out that we are here to learn to pray and to suffer for Christ.

e) Fourth objective: verification of the intention and suitability of the novice

  1. Intention: The word “verification,” juridically, means approval of a matter in all its parts, the detailed examination of something, its confirmation… until reaching the certainty of a resolution. Subject to verification are: the purpose, the intention, his vital desire, his fundamental option under grace. It includes a project of life forever, taken on with firmness, clarity and resolve. One who does not manifest a true desire for holiness or perfection cannot be a novice.
  2. Suitability: Suitability is also verified, that is, its present possession, not future or uncertain. One continues to observe those requirements that were demanded at the moment of admission.

f) Fifth objective: experience of life in the Institute

  1. The novice is not a mere spectator, nor a mere critic of the life of the Institute, which he would tolerate or confront critically. He is an active protagonist of it, even if a beginner. Living as such, he gains a living experience of the Institute. He must know the Institute, as will be said later, in order to conform his mind and heart to its spirit.

2. Admission to the novitiate

  1. Right of the major Superiors: “The right to admit candidates to the novitiate belongs to the major Superiors, according to the norm of proper law”15.

This is the formulation of a right and not of a duty; a pure right, for Institutes of religious life are free societies and as such they accept as members whomever they wish.

  1. This right belongs to the major Superiors, understood according to can. 620 of the Code of Canon Law16. Local Superiors, councilors and chapters are excluded. This right may be delegated, wholly or in part, for one act or for all cases in general17.
  2. The latest date of admission will be determined bearing in mind the beginning of the novitiate and of the academic year of the major Seminary, in such a way that the candidate is provided with the appropriate structure, as regards time, so that his novitiate may be twelve months spent in the same novitiate community18.
  3. Vigilant care: “With vigilant care, Superiors are to admit only those who, besides the required age, have the health, suitable character and sufficient qualities of maturity to embrace the life proper to the Institute; these qualities of health, character and maturity are to be verified, if necessary, with the collaboration of experts, without prejudice to what is established in can. 220”19.
  4. The norm is clearly imperative and excludes loopholes, although failure to fulfill it does not cause invalidity. The Latin clause has the marks of a severe precept: the imperative of the verb, the strict measure of vigilance and the exclusive particle: “with vigilant care […] are to admit only…”20 mark its gravity. This care is very important because of the risks that a mistaken admission would bring for the Institute and for the candidate, and also because of the very difficulty of verifying the required qualities (health, character and maturity).
  5. Moreover, it must be borne in mind that “the new vocations that knock at the doors of consecrated life present profound differences and need personal attention and suitable methods in order to take on their concrete human, spiritual and cultural situation. For this reason it is necessary to set in motion a serene discernment, free from the temptations of numbers or of efficiency, in order to verify, in the light of faith and of possible contraindications, the truthfulness of the vocation and the rectitude of intentions”21.
  6. Postulancy: This obligation is the basis for the existence of the postulancy: “The postulancy takes place in the prior contacts that the candidate has with members of the Institute, in order to learn about its charism and the obligations of the novitiate. This contact is in the charge of one or several priests, designated by the Provincial Superior, who must know the candidate, his vocation and his suitability, in order to present him to the novitiate”22.
  7. “The duration of the postulancy is not fixed, but will depend on the preparation of the candidate, on his degree of human and Christian maturity, on his basic general culture, on the balance of his affectivity and his capacity to live in community, etc., since ‘most of the difficulties encountered in our days in the formation of novices come from the fact that they do not possess, at the moment of their admission to the novitiate, the minimum of necessary maturity’”23.
  8. At the end of the postulancy the candidate will submit a request to the Provincial Superior for his admission to the novitiate, indicating the reasons that move him.
  9. Required qualities: The first required quality is the minimum age, 17 years, under pain of invalidity of the act of admission, of the beginning of the novitiate and of the other subsequent acts24.
  10. The second is health: we speak not so much of the absence of illnesses as of a subjective state of general and integral well-being of the person, in his psychological, physical, spiritual, social and family dimensions.
  11. The third is a suitable, stable and constant character that allows him to face the obligations to be taken on, both communal and personal.
  12. The fourth quality is psychological and moral suitability: “The Church… cannot admit to the Seminary and to Holy Orders those who practice homosexuality, present deep-seated homosexual tendencies or support the so-called gay culture. Such persons find themselves, in fact, in a situation that gravely hinders a correct relationship with men and women. In no way can the negative consequences that may derive from the Ordination of persons with deep-seated homosexual tendencies be ignored”25.
  13. Finally, sufficient maturity to integrate himself suitably into the functioning and strong demands of religious life.
  14. When it is considered necessary, recourse may be had to the collaboration of experts, but in such a way that the good reputation that someone enjoys and his own privacy are not unlawfully harmed.
  15. Supernatural judgment: But the principal criterion, to which the others are ordered, is necessarily supernatural, for the mystery of vocation is supernatural. Where it can reasonably be perceived that God has touched a soul with the vocational call, the Superior must be moved by faith. Here it is no longer a matter of a first impression, pleasant or not, of a personal liking or hunch, of human criteria, but of being respectful of the divine choice, for God is the dispenser of all graces (cf. Rom 1:5; 1 Cor 7:7).
  16. Invalidity: As for the invalidity of the admission, can. 643 of the Code of Canon Law is to be taken into account.

3. Different stages

  1. Plan of formation: “The purpose of the novitiate demands that the novices be formed under the direction of a Master, according to the plan of formation which proper law must determine”26.
  2. The direction of the Master is subordinated by the norm to a statute or plan of formation, provided for by proper law. It is not, then, an authoritarian or arbitrary direction, but one shaped normatively by the content and orientations of that plan.
  3. “It belongs to the Master and his collaborators to discern and test the vocation of the novices, and to form them gradually to lead the life of perfection proper to the Institute”27.
  4. The discernment and testing of the vocation is the primary objective, which implies study, distinction, elucidation, purification, selection. It leads organically to the verified confirmation of what has been discerned, which is the vocation. This testing proceeds, not in the abstract, but on the basis of real facts, gestures or signs.
  5. Gradual formation: This discernment must be carried out taking into account its complement, namely the gradual formation of the novices. The adverb gradually (gradatim) is accompanied by another adverb: properly (rite). The whole life of perfection, or the absolute perfection of life in the Institute, cannot be demanded of the novice; nor can there be imposed on him, from the very first days, the concrete perfection required of him as a novice, since he must be such for at least a year.
  6. This perfection is embodied in the fulfillment of the whole of proper law, correlatively to the way in which the vocation is a divine vocation but quidem Instituti propriam28. The religious is obliged to order his life according to proper law so as, in this way, to strive for the perfection of his state29, and he accepts as the supreme rule of life the following of Christ proposed in the Gospel, but expressed in the Constitutions.
  7. In our Institute the time of novitiate is one year. But it is not a uniform year as regards activities and demands. In them there is a progression; a pedagogy is attempted. It is fitting to divide the year into three well-defined stages. The first from entrance until the imposition of the cassock, the second and the third divided proportionally and as is judged fitting according to the attainment of the objectives. It is fitting to conclude with the month-long Spiritual Exercises, which properly prepare for first temporary profession.

a) First stage

  1. Entrance: This first stage coincides with entrance into religious life. This first period would be the continuation of the postulancy, for a better knowledge of the young men, above all so that the Superior may have more freedom to send away those who are not suitable for this life.
  2. Entrance is very hard for the young man and demanding in itself. “The way of salvation cannot be begun except by a narrow beginning”30, says St. Benedict in the “Prologue” of his Rule; and this because it is a conversion, in which one must leave behind one’s own way of seeing and acting; deny oneself, renounce vicious habits or the impulses of concupiscence; work to uproot vices… We are like shapeless blocks of marble that must be carved.
  3. In this first stage the Superior must be very close to the young man. The temptations are many; uprooting oneself from family and surroundings has consequences for the affections and feelings, which one must know how to reorder. He should be warned that this is not the time to think about his vocation or to discern about it. There will be time and suitable occasions for that.
  4. In practical terms it is necessary to begin activities as soon as possible. Present the plans of study, work and the calendar. Begin classes immediately, leaving no room for melancholy.
  5. But, above all, the work of the Master will be to fill with courage the souls who are beginning their journey. To instill confidence that God can be reached, holiness, confidence in perseverance… To show by his life the happiness of being consecrated to God.
  6. Discretion in demands: It is necessary to understand that in this first stage nothing “harsh or burdensome” should be ordered. The Superior must have the heart of a father and great discretion. The young man must be shown that if something seems rigorous or difficult he should not immediately flee in terror.
  7. It is very wise not to demand extremely hard things, harsh humiliations, ill treatment, dry or rigid… The one who enters must gain confidence in his Superiors and in his own strength. Through teaching, the goal must be set very high, the ideal very noble, without being stingy with resolutions of holiness. But demand steps that can be taken, concrete goals that can be achieved and that encourage one to seek more. In this way the weak are not discouraged and the strong always aspire to more: ut sit quod fortes cupiant.
  8. It is also very bad to overload with laws and regulations to the point of exhaustion. Above all, do not multiply obligations and prohibitions in trifles or in exterior works. This makes the soul small, for looking at small things makes souls petty. It is fitting always to emphasize the great ideals and invite them to attain them. As for other actions, go slowly, demanding little by little, patiently bearing with mistakes. It is better to say things than to command them.
  9. It often happens in the first days that there is a desperation for perfection, and since things do not turn out entirely well, souls fall into discouragement. Thus, it is very common in the first community chapters that, around the third point, which is devoted to the internal order of the community or to the activities of the novitiate, the novices begin to state and reproach all the things that go wrong: silence poorly kept, rooms not clean, study not respected, difficult community life, the liturgy poorly prayed, work poorly done, carelessness in the use of things… Things that are true are listed to the point of exhaustion, but said all together they do not produce an educational effect; no one will undertake their correction, it can occasion faults against charity and, above all, they paint a chaotic picture of the situation, which leads to discouragement. It is very important that the Master know how to rein in this point, allowing only what is fitting to be said and in due measure, and that, on the contrary, he point out the good things being done and highlight generous works, make concrete and feasible plans, present the ideals of the Congregation, missionary projects, apostolates, works undertaken…
  10. Patience in dealing with them: It is fitting to remember that the virtue that must temper the Superior in this stage is patience, and in his outward bearing he must show mastery of himself and of the situation. In this stage he must never scold the community in public; the private correction of a novice is another matter. Scolding at the beginning dejects the spirit, takes away courage. Therefore he must be very discreet in the things he commands. Order what it is possible to fulfill. Never issue an ultimatum about the fulfillment of obligations since, if they are not fulfilled, he will then find himself faced with the need to punish severely, or else to break his threatening word, losing authority.
  11. Time for teaching: This first stage is a time for teaching, for instructing in the knowledge of what constitutes religious life. Later there will be more work on the will in concrete matters.
  12. In public teaching, they will be instructed in the principal truths of the spiritual life and in the means within our reach for increasing that life. Moreover, because one does not enter religious life, a state of perfection, already perfect, but in order to attain holiness; and knowing how little awareness of sin exists among Christians today; for these two reasons it is fitting to clarify the doctrine on sin, even in its most elementary aspects: the distinction between mortal and venial sin, deliberate and indeliberate, first movements, imperfections…
  13. They must be taught to make a good confession, advising on the interval between Confessions and on the matter of the sacrament; teaching that the examination of conscience is not a mere psychological introspection, that first movements are not matter for the sacrament, that sorrow for sins is not reduced to a feeling of guilt, that sorrow must fall upon sins and not, as often happens, upon imperfections. In short, one must “form the conscience.”
  14. For this formation it is fitting to teach them to make the examination of conscience on a particular defect to be eliminated or a virtue to be acquired, according to the Ignatian method; for, rightly understood, not as a mere tally of faults, it helps us to see the causes or occasions for which one falls more often on one day than another, and to seek the remedies. Moreover, through the continual vigilance exercised over one’s own conduct, it puts us on guard to avoid faults; in this way one does not live in thoughtlessness, daily dissipation or frivolity.
  15. In another order of things, they must be taught to pray; it is a school of prayer: the novices need to know prayer, at least meditation and contemplation. One must be very practical in this, coming down to very concrete things at the beginning. If necessary, guide some first meditation, teach them to prepare it using the Ignatian method, which is the one that best serves as a walker, provide each one with a meditation book, ask about the outcome of the first ones. Let no time be wasted in prayer; let them learn to pray well as soon as possible.
  16. Liturgical prayer must be well known: its doctrine, historical development, theological content, the manner of praying it, even in practical matters.
  17. Encouraging recollection: To live in a climate of holiness it is fitting to insist on habitual recollection, that is, on the interior gaze upon our Lord, on continual companionship or the habit of doing everything in relation to Christ. Let them learn to make visits to the Blessed Sacrament, to live moments of silence… Teaching them to be recollected, contemplatives at every moment, is of great benefit. Thus St. Teresa says in The Way of Perfection: “Since nothing is learned without a little effort, for the love of God, sisters, consider well spent the care you devote to this; and I know that, if you take it, in a year, and perhaps in half, you will succeed in it, with God’s favor. See what a short time for so great a gain as laying a good foundation, so that if the Lord should wish to raise you to great things, He may find you prepared, finding you close to Himself… If in a year we cannot succeed in it, let it be in more. Let us not begrudge the time on something so well spent; who is coming after us? I say that one can grow accustomed to this, and strive to walk beside this true Master”31.
  18. Other great pillars of religious life that must be learned are silence and mortification.

Show the kinds of silence: exterior, interior, spiritual silence; teach its fittingness, its excellence, the benefits for the soul and the disasters caused by dissipation. Be clear in indicating the times of community silence, the great silence from Compline until the Angelus of the following day, silence in study, the silence useful for rest, etc. The means of putting it into practice must be sought, making use of the preventive method, avoiding occasions.

  1. As regards mortification, one must teach above all the reasons for which it is practiced, and have a firm hand in guiding them in it: avoiding the excesses typical of the beginning and demanding the fulfillment of what has been promised.
  2. Personal help: As regards the personal sphere, it will of course depend on the spiritual state of each novice, but close accompaniment is certainly required, constant dialogue in order to know him thoroughly and to suggest the spiritual tasks to be undertaken.
  3. What is indeed common to all, of great importance from the beginning, and a task best carried out in private, is to make them understand, above all the more capable ones, what the progress of a community consists in. Every stable group of people is divided into a few who seek holiness by every means, another few who drag everything down through discontent, mockery, irony, listlessness, doing things badly, etc.; and the great majority, who oscillate between the two poles and will move according to whichever weighs more. If those who manage to set the pace of the group are the saints, the community will seek holiness by following in their footsteps; otherwise it will fall into mediocrity. Making the more capable understand this, one must spur them on to be leaven, to be generous in sacrificing much for others, to forget themselves. They must not be content with being holy in a hidden way; they must create an atmosphere of holiness. In the words of Jesus Christ: let your light shine so that men may glorify God for your good works (Mt 5:16), or of St. Paul: be imitators of me as I am of Christ (1 Cor 11:1). For this one must be very generous and sacrifice time and tastes, overcome false humility, and be present at all possible community activities, creating a good spirit. Thus, however difficult it may be, it is of great benefit in formation. Few realize what this means. Many are content to pray, to mortify themselves, to do their works well, but then they hide, disappear, contribute nothing to the community.
  4. What has been said above about the three strata of a community must be kept very much in mind with respect to that small group of mediocre people who soil and drag everything down. They are not usually many, but one must be very attentive because of the harm they can cause. In the novitiate it is not customary to dismiss for imperfections; later on the cleansing will be greater, for example, of those lacking in will, the mediocre, those of worldly spirit… In the novitiate one is patient in this, since each soul has its own time for grace. However, there is one exception, and that is those who harm the group. They must be detected, and it is fitting to send them away, for the harm they cause, or the good that fails to be gained, is great.
  5. Time of great desires: These are the first spiritual counsels of the saints. It is St. Teresa who writes, advising souls in their beginnings: “To have great confidence, for it is very important not to diminish one’s desires, but to believe of God that, if we strive, little by little, even if not at once, we will be able to reach what many saints reached with His favor; for if they had never resolved to desire it and, little by little, to put it into practice, they would not have risen to so high a state. His Majesty wants, and is a friend of, courageous souls, provided they go with humility and no confidence in themselves; and I have not seen any of these remain low on this road, nor any cowardly soul—under cover of humility—that in many years covers the ground these others cover in very few. It astonishes me how much it does on this road to take courage for great things; even if the soul does not have the strength at once, it takes flight and arrives at much…”32.
  6. More explicitly she affirms in The Way of Perfection: “I say that what matters much, indeed everything, is a great and very determined determination not to stop until reaching it, come what may, happen what may, toil what one must, murmur who will, whether one arrives there, whether one dies on the way or has no heart for the trials there are on it, even if the world sinks”33.
  7. To this end it will be of great benefit in the novitiate to live profoundly united to Christ in His Passion, for this will move one to return love for love, repaying with works. Teach them to be generous in contemplation. To gaze gratuitously upon Christ, for He Himself will inflame our hearts.
  8. Foretasting virtue: As for the work of the Master, he must make very good use of the various means of formation, especially the “Good Nights” and other community conversations or meetings, to make them “foretaste the joy of virtue.” By this we mean making them savor in advance a virtue that is still being sought as if it were already possessed. That is to say: to rejoice in the poverty one is trying to live, to praise the good of humility, of sacrifice, to compare oneself with the saints, to see parallels with their lives… It is known that to acquire human virtues or to become prompt in the exercise of the infused ones, many acts must be repeated, something the novice has not yet done; but in the face of the first good works performed in view of a virtue, it is the work of the Superior to praise this kind of life.
  9. Another very praiseworthy means is to reward effort, whether by word or deed; kind treatment, the affection shown, the creation of an atmosphere of joy in community life, serve as reward and stimulus to continue along the path of virtue.
  10. It is very fitting that the Superior speak of all the good that happens: highlighting and valuing God’s gifts upon the community, His most loving providence over us, extolling how the virtues are lived, every time there are graces from God, spiritual or material progress… It helps a great deal to feel with the Congregation, taking part in the missionary works, reading the chronicles of other priests…
  11. On a more practical level, it is not fitting to begin with many community practices or devotions.

b) Second stage

  1. Reception of the habit: The point of transition between the two stages will be the Spiritual Exercises prior to the imposition of the cassocks.

These Exercises are of capital importance for a true conversion, perhaps a general Confession and, above all, a personal oblation to Christ the Eternal King, declaring that “I want and desire, and it is my deliberate determination, provided only that it be for Your greater service and praise, to imitate You in bearing all injuries and all reproach and all poverty, actual as well as spiritual, if Your most holy Majesty wishes to choose and receive me into such a life and state”34.

  1. The imposition of the cassock must be like a sensible sign of that interior disposition and its constant reminder. It makes a sensible impression and gives a longing to be identified with Christ. Making use of this, the Master will insist on something that is real: even though they are only novices, since they are dressed as priests, they have the obligation to behave as priests, to live the virtues proper to the priest before God and before men. For this, much work is needed. This is the moment, and their dispositions of soul are ready, to invite and move them to attain perfection. The perfect work is the work well finished, completed, down to its last details.
  2. Working on the will: This second stage is no longer so much for teaching or instilling desires, but principally for forging the will, strengthening it, compelling it to act. The predominant work is on the will: to make strong men. Religious life is the place of the strong; St. Benedict speaks of the “most strong kind, the cenobites”35. St. Thomas says that “he who enters religion should prepare himself for martyrdom.” Fortitude is a firm disposition of soul to endure valiantly any kind of evil, even the worst and the most prolonged.
  3. Secondarily, the weak in spirit are welcomed so that they may take shelter in the shade of the strong, as in a forest, where under the protection of the great trees countless weaker plants can grow. The weak must not be discouraged, as we have already said, but the great space for perfection must be prepared for the strong. Bernanos puts it well in the mouth of a Carmelite prioress: “It is a bad way to enter our Rule to throw oneself in blindly, like a man pursued by thieves… Our Rule is not a refuge. It is not the Rule that keeps us; it is we who keep the Rule”36. The Master must spend most of his time on the most capable and courageous; the others will follow in their footsteps. The temptation of the Superior is the opposite: out of feeling he tends to give more time to the weak or those with problems, since the others are going well. In the short term this solves some problems, but in the long run the level of the community declines. His obligation is to lead the strong to the highest degree of perfection.
  4. Forming in fortitude: The work on the will shall center on acquiring the virtue of perseverance in good action, which is a crown and adornment of the other virtues. It often happens that in the novitiate great works, very difficult ones, are undertaken with great spirit; the novice is strong to undertake and to attack. But this is still little. The virtue of fortitude is refined in perseverance. Here one must insist that they carry the good work through to the end, that they finish it, make it perfect.
  5. St. Thomas is very clear on this point. Of the two acts of fortitude, attacking and resisting or patiently enduring to the end, he says: “the principal act of fortitude is to endure”37. And he bases this on three reasons: he who attacks considers what he attacks to be weaker; moreover, he who endures has the danger upon him, unlike the one who attacks, who sees it as still in the future; lastly, enduring involves a prolongation of time, whereas the assault is sudden.
  6. In perseverance: In this stage the demands in the practices of religious life must be intensified in order to educate and refine the virtue of perseverance. The novitiate is very favorable for this for two reasons:

– the human will changes very quickly, and lingering a long time on something tends to wear down even the most resolute will;

– in the second place, man seeks distractions, variety, changes of occupation… In this stage activities tend to become monotonous, whether study, work, prayer or community. There are no ­external obstacles, many sins and vices have been overcome; it is as if the struggle were suspended, and this seems to be routine. To persevere in this monotonous state is a great virtue and very formative: Melior est patiens viro forti, et qui dominatur animo suo, expugnatore urbium (Prov 16:32).

  1. Here it is very important to demand the fulfillment of obligations, for they are a great help: by disciplining the will, one tempers it; and by ordering it to precise works, the waste of energy is avoided and it is withdrawn from distraction.
  2. In demands: The practical way to achieve this is the introduction of new demands, the maximum use of time so as to pour it into prayer, reading, study, etc.
  3. An increase in mortification, even communal mortification. Of course this, like everything else, must be suggested, pointing out that it has always been done and with great benefit, and free acceptance must be elicited. A day of silence is introduced, which will naturally include communal fasting, and the conclusion of this day of silence with a heroic night of adoration.
  4. Some other community prayers will be added, such as, for example, Midday Prayer, Compline, etc. They will rise earlier to allow for a longer preparation for Holy Mass.
  5. Efforts will be made to improve the work itself, setting common ends or purposes, weekly goals or whatever is required, which once achieved will give way to others.
  6. In preaching, there will be work in two ways: insisting on magnanimity, great undertakings, actual graces, etc.; and showing how humiliating it is to act with smallness, how humiliating laziness, disorder, how little the virtues and mortifications are lived; humbling them by comparison with the greater zeal of worldly people for their affairs than ours for the things of Christ. Courage must be stirred up to overcome oneself and act better.
  7. At these moments the Master may spend a few reprimands. We had strongly emphasized that in the first stage he should never scold the community in public; scolding is almost always counterproductive: it denotes a lack of authority, the good will feel hurt and become discouraged, and those who deserve the reprimand will not even take notice.
  8. However, now is a favorable time to castigate errors. But, above all, pained reprimands, in the manner of one who feels let down. There is no problem in expressing displeasure. Specific defects can be pointed out, stressing that, although small, they will later cause greater harm.
  9. It is even beneficial to impose some punishments on whoever commits faults, even through negligence. Some punishment, whether imposed by the Master himself or with the acceptance of the one punished, is of great benefit for him and for all.
  10. Trusting abandonment: After all that has been said, an important clarification must be made. This is a time of demand, of strengthening the will. But however much willpower we achieve, God is not reached by force of arms. God scorns the strength of man (1 Sam 2:8). The work is supernatural, and the means of reaching God must surpass willpower; they presuppose it, but transcend it. And it would be very pernicious to make of the novice a man full of himself, self-sufficient, proud.
  11. Great spiritual growth occurs when, after having demanded the utmost of oneself, after having fought to the limit of one’s strength, one runs into some failure or helplessness, and realizes that one’s strength is meager, that it is for a reason that God chose what is poor and despised. Consider, brothers, your calling: there are not many wise according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many noble; but God has chosen what is foolish in the world to confound the wise; and what is weak in the world to confound the strong; and what is base in the world and despised God has chosen, and even what is not, to destroy what is; so that no flesh may glory before God (1 Cor 1:22-29).
  12. The formative event takes place when the young man “breaks his will” and begins to abandon himself to God, to “do everything as if it depended on oneself, but knowing that everything depends on God”38.
  13. To lead him to this “breaking of his own will” it is fitting, after the normal demands, to begin to assign other activities, for example cultural, intellectual, the demand of extra work; but without dispensing him from any of his previous obligations and commitments. When it seems to the novice that this exceeds his strength, this moment will be used to teach the secret of trust and abandonment in God.
  14. This stage has a high point, which it may be very fitting to place at the end. We are referring to the making of the vow of Marian slavery. It is not a public vow, inasmuch as the novice is not yet a religious, juridically speaking. It will have the character of a public vow when the novice professes it for the first time together with the vows of poverty, chastity and obedience. In preparation for the vow of Marian slavery, the novices will read meditatively the Treatise on True Devotion to the Blessed Virgin by St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort.

c) Third stage

  1. Spiritual joy: If the novice has sought God sincerely, with generosity, his heart has gradually been enlarged, and God fills it with His grace. The fulfillment of the divine commands is easier, the efforts less painful and happiness nearer. Enlarge my heart, Lord, and I will run in the way of your commandments (Ps 119:32).
  2. The end of this stage is to teach them to live and act in the presence of God, with confidence in Him. Therefore to act with ease, enlightened by the rules and commands, but not enslaved to them, passing from servants to friends. To ride well, one must ride with ease. This makes the going more joyful and effective, as is also the case with journeying toward God. God blesses the one who gives himself with joy (2 Cor 9:7), St. Paul declares.
  3. An atmosphere of joy should be fostered, even outwardly, in which one savors having fought and won.
  4. Preparation for profession: As regards teaching, it is necessary to instruct with a view to the next stage. Life there will be very different. Throughout the year a desire to move on to the major Seminary has been instilled, its advantages and benefits have been shown. Now emphasis will be placed on the way to make the most of that life, taking advantage of the closeness of other priests, the experience of the older ones, participation in undertakings of greater scope, a more perfect organization, a more stately liturgy, etc. Some virtues can be clarified, such as obedience and the way to live it, eutrapelia and its benefit to community life, generosity…
  5. But it is extremely important to forewarn them about certain situations or about the most frequent temptations they will encounter.
  6. Because the house is larger and the number of religious greater, it is very easy to succumb to the temptation to hide, to keep apart from common activities, fulfilling one’s obligations but not contributing much more.
  7. Month-long Exercises: It is fitting to finish with the month-long Spiritual Exercises, which constitute the culminating moment of the third stage.

4. The formators

  1. A single Master: “The purpose of the novitiate demands that the novices be formed under the direction of the Master according to the plan of formation to be defined by proper law. The governance of the novices, under the authority of the Superiors, is reserved to a single Master”39.According to the Code, the doors are closed to what we might call collegial leadership in formation: several persons equally responsible. The governance must have a single direct and immediate head, that is, a single Master of novices.
  2. a) The Master of novices has the power to govern the novices with regard to the objectives of their condition as such, a power to be exercised in a spirit of service40 and within the limits of his office41.

  3. b) He will also be the local Superior when the novitiate takes place in a house designated exclusively for this purpose42. His power excludes the sacramental internal forum43.
    c) When the novitiate is made in another house of the Institute44, the power of the Master of novices will be exclusively and strictly over the novices, both individually and as a group or community. Whatever exceeds their condition as novices will fall under the true and proper power of the local Superior. The local Superior governs the novitiate house, not as a novitiate, but as a house.

  1. Requirements: “The Master of novices is to be a member of the Institute, perpetually professed and legitimately designated”45.

There are three requirements that the Code of Canon Law demands in order to be Master of novices:

  1. a) To be a member of the Institute, for otherwise he could not introduce the novices into the proper spirit and charism, nor judge the suitability of the novices.
    b) To be perpetually professed, for the same reason as above, though this is dispensable by the Holy See.
    c) To be canonically designated by the Superior General with the deliberative vote of his General Council46.

  1. Qualities: Bearing in mind that the community depends on the Superior, it is fitting to describe the virtues the Master must possess. We base ourselves on two great founders: St. Ignatius of Loyola and St. Benedict.
  2. He takes the place of Christ: St. Benedict establishes a supreme principle from which he deduces all his doctrine on this matter, and which will give unity, cohesion and supernatural fruitfulness to the society he wishes to found: “Abbas… Christi agere vices in monasterio creditur…”47; the abbot is regarded as the one who takes the place of Christ. “Creditur,” he is believed, not in the modern sense of “it is thought,” but grounded on the act of faith. Here his authority is founded, but also his obligations: “et sic se exhibeat ut dignus sit tali honore”; noblesse oblige.
  3. Shepherd: He is, in the first place, a shepherd. The first duty of the Good Shepherd is to feed the flock: is not the flock pastured by the shepherd? (Ezek 34:2); and what is that food?: Your shepherds nourish you with knowledge and doctrine (cf. Jer 3:15). The Superior must know doctrine and the divine law perfectly. “He must not teach, establish or command anything that is outside the divine precepts. Let him always remember that, at the dread judgment of God, he will be strictly examined on the doctrine taught and the direction given”48 to the monks: “However slight the harm suffered by the flock at the hands of the father of the family, let the abbot know that he will be held responsible”49.
  4. Pontiff: He is also a pontiff, and personal holiness is necessary for the pontiff, for he is the intermediary between God and men (Heb 5:1). He cannot draw near to God and intercede effectively for the people without purity, which makes him pleasing to God.
  5. Thus he must strive to live and to sanctify himself, not for himself alone, but for his sons. In the words of Christ: I sanctify myself for them (Jn 17:19). Greater holiness will make him more powerful in his prayer and more fruitful in the supernatural work of guiding minds and hearts.
  6. The Superior must be by his conduct the “living Rule.” In practice, this translates into continual companionship with the novices and giving example in everything. Let him not forget that he is the point of reference. He must know how to share everything except decisions. Any other work, prayer, recreation, etc., must be lived alongside them.
  7. All that having been said, however, we would have a very imperfect notion of the mission of the Superior if we were unaware of the other two principal qualities which St. Benedict inculcates so insistently: discretion and kindness.
  8. A man of discernment: The guidance of souls requires the exercise of discretion, the mother that begets virtue. Discretion is the supernatural art of discernment and of valuing each thing according to its end, adapting the means to the objective, according to our nature and to varied circumstances. What is the object of commanding? To lead souls to God, “ut animae salventur”50; and to lead them not in just any way, but by making them carry out their task wholeheartedly. For this it is necessary to temper everything opportunely: “omnia temperet”51; and, unfolding his thought further, he sums up the function of the Superior in a precise formula full of meaning: “let him adapt himself to the various characters” (“multorum servire moribus”52). This is the golden rule that defines the practical conduct of the Superior.
  9. On this point St. Benedict wants the abbot to have a well-balanced combination of diverse qualities: strength joined to gentleness, authority moderated by love. He wants the Superior to be zealous but without anxiety; prudent but without timidity; always seeking the Kingdom of God and His justice, but without neglecting the material good of the monastery, which he must administer wisely; to love the brothers but hate the vices; to use prudence even in correction, for fear that by scraping too hard at the rust the vessel be broken. He must vary his conduct with great flexibility, according to the circumstances and the disposition of each one: this one has an open character, that one is more reserved; in one the intellect prevails, in another feeling; now he finds him docile, now resisting; he must then bend to the various temperaments, combining the times, and coaxing with severity. To the undocile disciple let him show himself a stern master; to the upright soul who seeks God, a tender father: “pium patris ostendat affectum.” To one of capable mind, eager to find God, it will suffice to propose the doctrine: “capacibus discipulis mandata Domini verbis proponat.” To simple spirits or those of less gentle temperament, he must show the way by his example: “duris vero corde et simplicioribus, factis suis divina praecepta demostret.” Let him encourage this one, conform himself to temperaments, adapt himself to the capacity of each; for only in this way will he be able to rejoice in the increase of the flock and in the progress of his disciples in the good, and he will not have to weep over the ruin of the souls entrusted to him.
  10. The magnificent doctrine of the holy legislator on discretion is summed up in this formula: “The abbot must temper everything in such a way that the strong may find still more to desire, and the weak may not flee discouraged”53.
  11. A kind man: But discretion is not the only principal virtue required of the Superior. To it love must be joined. It is love that will give him the key to hearts, refining in him the supernatural aim. If he loves souls greatly and supernaturally, he will do everything to lead them to Christ.
  12. St. Benedict, in whom the spirit of the Gospel superabounded, inspired his whole Rule with mercy, but the precept of charity is especially inculcated in the chapter dealing with the abbot. “Diligat fratres,” he must love his brothers deeply, with equal love for all: “Non unus plus ametur quam alius”54; because we are all equal in Christ, in whom there is neither slave nor free; and we are all called to the same adoption, to share in the same heavenly inheritance.
  13. But just as God looks with greater pleasure upon the one who best bears in himself the image of His Son, so too the Superior may show greater love to the one who conforms more closely to the divine model through good works or through obedience.
  14. St. Benedict insists a great deal on the love the Superior owes his sons. He must strive to inspire more love than fear; thus his government will not be tyrannical. Love for the subject must extend as far as possible, even to the guilty, for the Good Shepherd leaves the ninety-nine sheep to seek the lost one. But kindness must not degenerate into weakness. Christ Jesus, so loving and merciful toward souls, no less profoundly hated evil: He forgave the Magdalene and the adulterous woman, He tolerated the defects of the disciples, but He was severe with the vicious and especially with the proud Pharisees.
  15. In this too the Superior must imitate Christ: “let him love the brothers, but hate their vices”55. If a novice needs correction, the Superior must reprove him with great charity and fatherly love: a very severe Superior can do much harm to souls; but it is also true that in a house where the Superior is so lenient as not to correct defects, or refuses no permission, fervor soon declines. Therefore he must always be moved by charity. It may happen that, for a certain time, a novice does not do all the good that might be expected: what is to be done? Set him aside? Quite the contrary. With great patience, the Superior will await the moment of grace, remembering the discretion of the patriarch Jacob, who said: If I overdrive my flocks, making them walk too far, they will all die in one day (Gen 33:13). Not all souls are called to the same degree of perfection; and he must show greater condescension toward those who walk with a slower and more labored step.
  16. Now, what is to be done when a truly wicked will is recognized? St. Benedict then commands rigor; he requires the Superior to use the iron that cuts off: “ferrum abscissionis,” so that a single infected sheep may not ruin the flock. But so that obstinacy may not become incorrigible, he himself must superabound in mercy, after the example of Christ, so as to obtain indulgence for himself in equal measure. Let the weakness of others help him to keep his own frailty in mind: “suamque fragilitatem semper suspectus sit.”
  17. Great is the counsel of the Holy Patriarch for government: “let no one be troubled or saddened in the house of God” (“ut nemo perturbetur neque contristetur in domo Dei”). All those of simple and upright heart, who seek God and live in His grace, must always be full of joy and, with joy, possess the divine peace that surpasses all understanding (cf. Phil 4:7).
  18. Let us turn to St. Ignatius and his Constitutions:

A man of God: “As for the qualities that should be desired in the Superior General, the first is that he be very closely united with God our Lord and intimate with Him in prayer and in all his actions, so that all the better from Him, the fountain of all good, he may obtain for the whole body of the Society a large share of His gifts and graces, and much power and efficacy for all the means that may be used for the help of souls”56.

    1. A virtuous man: “The second, that he be a person whose example in all the virtues may help the others of the Society; and in particular charity toward all neighbors should shine forth in him, and especially toward the Society, and true humility, which may make him very lovable to God our Lord and to men”57.
    2. “He should also be free of all passions, keeping them tamed and mortified, so that interiorly they may not disturb the judgment of his reason; and exteriorly he should be so composed, and especially so measured in speech, that no one may notice in him any thing or word that does not edify, both those of the Society, who are to have him as a mirror and model, and those outside”58.
    3. “With this, he should know how to blend the necessary rectitude and severity with kindness and gentleness in such a way that he does not fail to have the compassion that is fitting toward his sons, so that even those who are reproved or punished may recognize that he proceeds rightly in our Lord and with charity in what he does, even though it may be against their liking according to the lower man”59.
    4. “And likewise magnanimity and fortitude of soul are very necessary to him in order to bear the weaknesses of many, and to begin great things in the service of God our Lord and persevere constantly in them when it is fitting, without losing heart in the face of opposition (even if it came from great and powerful persons), and without letting himself be turned aside from what reason and the divine service require by their entreaties or threats; being superior to all events, without letting himself be lifted up by prosperity or cast down in spirit by adversity, being very ready to accept death, if need be, for the good of the Society in the service of Jesus Christ, our God and Lord”60.
    5. A prudent man: “The third is that he ought to be endowed with great understanding and judgment, so that he may not lack talent either in speculative matters or in the practical ones that may arise. And although learning is very necessary, still more so is prudence and experience in spiritual and interior things, in order to discern the various spirits and to counsel and remedy so many who will have spiritual needs; and likewise discretion in external things and in the manner of dealing with such varied matters, and of conversing with so many different persons inside and outside the Society”61.
    6. A man of action: “The fourth, and very necessary for the carrying out of things, is that he be vigilant and careful to begin and energetic in carrying things through to their end and perfection, not careless and remiss so as to leave them begun and unfinished”62.
    7. A well-trained man: To foster the appropriate qualities of the Master of novices, specific and up-to-date formation will be sought, following with discretion the suggestions of the Church63. “We must be extremely generous in dedicating time and our best energies to formation. Consecrated persons are, in fact, one of the most precious goods of the Church. Without them, all formative and apostolic plans remain theory, useless wishes”64.

b) Collaborators of the Master

    1. “The Master, if necessary, may be given collaborators, who are subject to him in what concerns the governance of the novitiate and the plan of formation”65.
    2. It is necessary to distinguish between direct collaborators of the Master and collaborators in formation. They do not always coincide, for the direct collaborators are necessarily subject to him as regards the governance of the novitiate, safeguarding the unity of government and of formation in the person of the Master.
    3. Indirect collaborators: The collaborators in formation, on the other hand, would be those who, as professors, spiritual directors, confessors, collaborate in the formation of the novices66. The functions of spiritual director, confessor and professor require strict, disciplined fulfillment. Absence, delay or infrequent visits create a very noticeable climate of disorder. The Master of novices must be very demanding in this; thus direction must be constant, attendance at the teaching of classes perfect, evaluation and grading continual… if the novice sees improvisation here, he totally neglects his formation as if it were something entirely secondary67. It should be noted that provision is made68 for the Master himself to carry out some or all of the tasks assigned to the indirect collaborators, without prejudice to what is indicated in can. 630 § 4.
    4. Direct collaborators: “For the formation of the novices, suitably prepared associates are to be assigned who, not impeded by other duties, can carry out their ministry fruitfully and in a stable manner”69.
    5. These are the direct collaborators. The Master needs their help for more particular matters: “For although he may sometimes attend to them directly, he cannot do without lower Superiors, who should be chosen persons to whom he can give much authority and to whom he can ordinarily refer such particular matters”70. He will try to help them with advice or corrections, if necessary, for it belongs to him to make up for the defects of his assistants and to perfect what they lack, with divine favor and help.
    6. The Master should not occupy himself with carrying out particular matters that others can do, such as the care of the house, temporal sustenance, the order of rooms, studies, work. In this way he delegates and frees himself from such duties. Giving orders is more properly his, and in the rest he will only oversee their execution.
    7. The collaborators, together with the Master, form a single head and share in his functions. Therefore there must be a great communion of ideas and sentiments: this is of great benefit, both in important matters and in small ones; and although in the latter there could be diversity, it is necessary, in order to relieve possible tensions, that as far as possible they strive to agree even in particular matters.
    8. The principal duty of the assistants is to collaborate with the Master; therefore they must maintain continual dialogue, which the Master must foster, so that the progress of the group may be discussed. For this, certain requirements will be taken into account: adequate preparation, not being impeded by other duties, and stability.
    9. Qualities: The collaborator must be an intelligent man and a man of decision, both of which pertain to prudence, so as to know how to support the Master in what he consults him on and to make personal decisions. It is of no use to consult one who is servile.
    10. They must be patient with the defects or imperfections typical of the early days of the novitiate, and enthusiastic in their comments, not continually highlighting defects, not dragging down morale, both in dealing with the novices and with the Master.
    11. They must be very kind to the novices so that these may gain confidence in them as companions in formation and desire to imitate them. In this respect, the greatest benefit they can offer is to be alongside the novices as much time as possible.
    12. They must be very enterprising, for it is very common to find that there is goodness in the novices, good compliance, but perhaps little initiative. Thus it is important that the collaborators talk about their studies, undertakings, apostolic works, books they have read, recreations, eutrapelia… They must teach the various ways of making better use of time and channeling energies and talents.
    13. These tasks can be divided into two offices, and thus distributed between two collaborators, whom we call “prefect of discipline” and “prefect of studies.”
    14. Discipline: The first mission of the prefect of discipline is to teach what must be done and how. Let him devote time and be clear in the organization of work, internal order, cleaning, kitchen. Let him be clear and explicit in everything, for the novice comes to learn, and it is not good to waste time sowing errors or allowing them to act by mistake, which is later hard to correct. Thus in the liturgy, in study, in work and in other practical matters.
    15. Besides this preventive aspect, he will have the mission of informing the Master when he sees some defect, consulting with him so as to share in his discretion, and calling attention when he advises it. Calling attention is not by way of scolding but of correction, to give the opportunity to begin acting differently. If the defect is repeated, he will be more severe. He will communicate to the Master the defects he has seen. If he knows how to correct well, he will spare the Master from spending his authority and will leave him the opportunity for further admonitions when greater seriousness requires it.
    16. Study: The task of the prefect of studies is important for creating study habits, teaching the way to study, and demanding discipline in its exercise.
    17. Given the diversity of backgrounds and studies with which the candidates enter, personal follow-up will be fitting in order to discover the needs and capacities of each of them. On this basis the most suitable and profitable readings will be recommended, to make up for deficiencies or to channel talents.

5. Contents and means of formation

a) Formative content71

    1. “The novices are to be encouraged to live the human and Christian virtues; they are to be led along a path of greater perfection through prayer and self-denial; they are to be instructed in the contemplation of the mystery of salvation and in the reading and meditation of the Sacred Scriptures; they are to be prepared to celebrate the worship of God in the Sacred Liturgy; they are to be formed to lead a life consecrated to God and to men in Christ through the evangelical counsels; they are to be instructed on the character, spirit, purpose, discipline, history and life of the Institute; and care is to be taken to imbue them with love for the Church and her sacred Pastors”72. For this, let there be offered “fundamental concepts of anthropology and psychology, which give the subject, at the beginning of his formative journey, the possibility of knowing himself better, particularly in the areas most in need of formation”73.
    2. Cultivation of the virtues: more than theory and research about them, what is required is daily work to embody them according to the proper manner of the Institute. This concerns especially the virtues necessary for one’s own sanctification and for one’s future life and ministry in the Institute.
    3. Prayer and self-denial. As for penance, it will conform to the custom and seasons of our Institute, whether communal or personal, with discretion and balance.
    4. As for study, they will be introduced to the mystery of salvation and the Bible: it does not therefore cover all the philosophical and theological disciplines, but the “Mystery of Christ and of the history of salvation”74. In a special way, if the novices are candidates for the priesthood, so that “the students may grasp the meaning and order of ecclesiastical studies and their pastoral purpose, and may at the same time be helped to ground and permeate their whole life with faith, and be confirmed in embracing their vocation with personal self-giving and joy of soul”75. In such a way that they “must not occupy themselves with studies or work that do not contribute directly to that formation”76.
    5. Liturgical worship: theoretical and practical preparation for participating in the celebrations with the greatest possible benefit.
    6. Consecrated religious life: hearing and learning the reason or the manner in which they are to lead their life consecrated to God through the evangelical counsels. Emphasis will be placed on knowledge of the theology of religious life, of its spirituality, and of our own Constitutions.
    7. The spirit, discipline and life of the Institute, detailing the above.
    8. Love for the Church and for the Pastors, in its doctrinal foundation and in the practical way of acting, whether through celebrations or the implementation of norms.
    9. As regards formation for the apostolate, it will be very useful for the novices to take part in some of the evangelizing tasks proper to the Institute, such as catechesis, the permanent mission, popular missions. Regarding the latter, it should be borne in mind that “‘to complete the formation of novices, the Constitutions can prescribe, in addition to the time established in paragraph 1 [that is, the twelve months spent in the same novitiate community], one or more periods of apostolic exercise outside the novitiate community’77. The purpose of these periods is to teach the novices ‘to bring about progressively in their lives that coherent and harmonious unity which must exist between contemplation and apostolic action, a unity which is one of the fundamental values of these Institutes’”78.

b) Means

      1. The Master and his collaborators will exercise this right and obligation toward the novices. Insofar as they are priests: Confession and spiritual direction, which are indispensable. Insofar as they are responsible for the novitiate: they will make use of other external means such as sermons, “Good Nights,” chapters. In sermons, spiritual support is given to the work of holiness. In chapters, doctrinal teaching is given with a view to the practice and progress of religious life, as well as practical regulations, schedules, resolutions, ways of acting. And in the “Good Nights” the pulse of the community is kept, encouraging, rectifying intentions, correcting what is crooked, appreciating good things.

c) Preaching

    1. Without dwelling at length on this point, since sermons will normally depend on the liturgical calendar, they will serve to understand and live well the sacred seasons of the Church, to solemnize the feasts and, on some occasions, a series of sermons will be fitting for teaching and appreciating mysteries or virtues. At the beginning of the novitiate it is fitting to teach and move them to Eucharistic worship, then to knowledge and love of the Holy Mass, to Marian devotion, and to whatever particular needs dictate.
    2. Joined to sermons is the preaching of novenas, for which no opportunity will be neglected, according to the importance the liturgy assigns to them and according to the proper devotion of the Institute.
      3. d) Good Nights
      1. They became classic and passed into the treasury of the Church’s goods, chiefly thanks to St. John Bosco, who described them thus: “Affectionate words in public to the pupils before they go to bed, to warn or advise them about what they must do or avoid. Let warnings or advice be drawn from what has happened during the day, inside or outside the school; and let the little talk last no more than two or three minutes. In it lies the key to morality and to the good progress and success of education”79.
      2. This last statement is very true and verifiable by experience. Giving the “Good Night” is very simple; nevertheless, it is not a random thought, as if one were merely filling a gap. It must be thought out, weighing what the novices need at that moment, what will do them the most good, what warning to give them, etc. It is very effective and in general should be directed toward encouraging and always instilling new desires.
      3. The doctrinal-speculative should not predominate; rather, they should be appraisals of events or circumstances that have been lived.

Among all the “Good Nights,” those of Saturday have an important place, useful for forging apostolic zeal and increasing the spirit of faith, so necessary for the apostolate; and those of Sunday, dedicated to thanksgiving for what has been received during the week.

e) Chapters

  1. Government: This is one of the elements that contribute to the development of religious life and to the perfecting of community life. Here we are dealing with the particular chapters of each house of formation. In its conduct it must be practical and agile, avoiding idle comments. It consists of three parts: a doctrinal point, the chapter of faults and a practical point.

First Point: doctrinal

  1. An exposition of the spiritual doctrine that illuminates the stage being lived; it teaches the principles necessary for religious life, with a view to practice. It is not purely practical, and in this it differs from the third point.

Second Point: Chapter of faults

  1. The accusation: There are different ways of making the accusation. In some houses it is the Superior who states the faults of the religious, and in others the brothers themselves may state the errors of the one being accused. These two ways require great humility and virtue in a heroic degree on the part of the one who receives the correction, and exquisite charity on the part of the one who corrects. It is more suited to a community that has already worked a great deal at perfection, and not to those who are at a beginning.
  2. We adopt the method of personal and free accusation, for no one is obliged to it; rather, the opportunity is given to acknowledge one’s faults and to preach humility, as well as to make amends for the offenses that may have been inflicted on one’s neighbor.
  3. Fittingness: The fruits of the chapter of faults are, besides humility of spirit and mortification of the flesh80, the restoration of relations within a community. Moreover, it serves to keep the rules and resolutions constantly in mind, not in the manner of one who emphasizes them doctrinally—as might be done through the teaching of the Master—but in the manner of those who are constantly striving to live the rules of the Institute and attain perfection.
  4. But above all, the greatest fruit concerns fraternal charity. Asking forgiveness of the one offended extinguishes any resentment that may have remained. It is the best way to avoid the touchiness proper to human weakness, which can be very present in a community.
  5. It is also a way of forming the conscience, distinguishing among the accusations those that are unfounded, the product of a misunderstanding of obligations or of scruples. That is why the accusation must be only “of exterior faults, committed in front of others, never of purely internal ones. The accusation must be simple, brief, sincere and without concealing anything, humble and without excuses, charitable and without accusing anyone or revealing the defects of others”81.
  6. Finally, it helps in the correction of specific defects, since it is also customary to suggest that the one who accuses himself make some concrete and interior resolution so as not to fall into the same fault. In some cases some punishment may be imposed publicly.

Third point: practical matters

  1. Planning: This is more varied and free, according to circumstances. It is of great benefit for the good spirit and morale of the novices. The most fitting thing is to take the opportunity to report on matters concerning the progress of the Congregation, its missions, its apostolic works and projects. Speaking much about this encourages living more united to the Religious Family. Matters proper to the life of the novitiate must also be reported.
  2. In the second place, as far as possible, plans must be made together: reforming daily life, planning apostolates, missions, trips, work, and any other project of community interest. Decisions should be taken on what it is fitting to do in common.
  3. Lastly, room is left for personal announcements, mainly from those in charge of various offices. They must be brief and concern important matters, not giving room for petty complaints or grievances that often arise from inexperience or from the defects proper to a fledgling community.

6. The novices

  1. “The novices, conscious of their own responsibility, are to collaborate actively with the Master, so as to respond faithfully to the grace of the divine vocation”82.
  2. “The members of the Institute are to collaborate for their part in the formation of the novices, by the example of their life and by prayer”83.
  3. The Religious Family: The novitiate is a community in formation. The other members of the same Religious Family help it. Example and prayer are the principal means of cooperation. They withstand time and distance. But other occasional means are not excluded. This help is already an apostolate in itself84 which, as a duty of all, must begin to be carried out with those of the household before those outside the family.
  4. Community life: But the novice, after the Master, is the first one responsible for the small community of the novitiate. The novice is a free and conscious person who wants to sanctify himself, not a mere passive subject before formative content. Without losing his individuality, he will collaborate with the Master through submission, openness, receptiveness, creativity, initiatives, incentives, demands. From the very beginning they must strive to realize the ideal of religious life: “a religious Institute is a society in which members, according to proper law, pronounce public vows, either perpetual or temporary which are to be renewed when the period of time has elapsed, and lead a life of brothers or sisters in common”85.
  5. Fraternal life in common is constitutive of religious life. It must be distinguished from mere fraternity. The latter speaks of the mutual or reciprocal help that members owe one another, insofar as they share the same supernatural family and the same ends, the same constitution and regulations, but they have no obligation to live in common. Life in common adds the sharing of the same goods, house and time, and continual community interaction.
  6. Building community: The novitiate must be made into an atmosphere of holiness, which gives room to the generosity of the strongest and supports the weakest. For this, stability is required, analogous to that which St. Benedict prescribed for his monks. Barring exceptions86, the novitiate takes place in a house erected for this purpose. Because of the advantages that appear in this Directory, efforts will be made to have the novitiate take place in a house designated solely for this purpose.
  7. The communion of life takes place principally in the liturgy and prayer. This liturgy must be stately, so that one longs eagerly to take part in it. Community mortification is another factor of unity of life. Work provides the occasion to join efforts, support one another, and dialogue.
  8. Recreation and rest in common are highly important. We call this by the name of the virtue to which they belong: eutrapelia. St. Thomas wisely says that no one has the right to be burdensome to others. It is in eutrapelia that generosity is seen and good spirit is verified; not in recreation itself, which may be the fruit of human enthusiasm, but insofar as it is supernatural joy, which can exist even in the company of trials or interior dryness.
  9. It is the obligation of the Master to promote a true life in common. Community is not sanctifying ex opere operato. Simply piling people together means nothing; being together is morally indifferent. Its goodness, or its badness, will be given to it by the end that is pursued. It is true that the essential goodness of community life comes from its being part of religious life. But this is open to further perfections, in the manner of accidental additions. Life in itself can appear monotonous and always the same, causing dullness instead of spurring on to the good by enlivening spirits.
  10. Moreover, at present it is necessary to bear in mind that “interculturality, differences in age and diversity of outlook increasingly characterize Institutes of consecrated life”87. For this very reason, the Master “must educate in community dialogue in cordiality and in the charity of Christ, teaching to welcome diversity as a richness and to integrate the various ways of seeing and feeling. Thus, the constant search for unity in charity will become a school of communion for Christian communities and a proposal of fraternal coexistence among peoples”88.
  11. In short, it is the task of the Master to guide community life. For this he must give instruction on its aspects and advantages. But the work is more practical than theoretical and, therefore, he must undertake the actions himself: accompany the work, share in sports, suggest activities during eutrapelia, games, songs, after-meal gatherings, interesting conversations, spread joy, etc.
  12. There is no fixed or obligatory measure for taking part in eutrapelia, but it is certain that the more charity one puts into it, the more of God’s goods one will receive; and it can be verified that those who are most generous in moments of joy are those who perform best in their other activities.
  13. Fleeing from community: What is indeed disastrous is to shun community life. God cannot communicate Himself to the soul that by its own choice withdraws from the course of grace He has determined. God communicates Himself only to the faithful and docile soul, the one that, obeying legitimate authority, is where it has been placed, at the proper time and in the proper occupation: Blessed are those servants whom the Lord finds watchful when He comes (Lk 12:37). Let us remember that no external occupation or work can impede divine action, since by it one pleases God: “When one prays, one is gifted by God; when one works, one gifts God”89.
  14. Fleeing from community life ordinarily comes from pride. One seeks to be “singular” in one’s actions, believing that I lose my personality by acting like everyone else. One grows vain thinking that one “understands better” than others what must be done and how the regulations should be fulfilled. Pride is the root: I am not like the rest, said the Pharisee in the temple (Lk 18:11). One thus withdraws from God’s plan. Community life demands humility. For in doing what is common, what is commanded, one does not shine; one usually goes unnoticed at first sight; one does not draw attention. If it is true humility and not faintheartedness, it is blessed by God, for God gives His grace to the humble (1 Pet 5:5).
  15. On the other hand, man does not increase in grace because he leaves no room for God. He is very full of himself, always looking at himself. He wants to act in his own way and does not let God act. According to St. John of the Cross’s comparison, we are like a willful child whom his mother would pick up and carry at a quick pace, but the child insists on walking by himself and barely advances90. To be filled with God one must empty oneself of oneself and one’s own interests; and community life and its activities distract us from ourselves, they close the selfish eyes. God communicates Himself when man is most distracted from himself. Whether in work, games, conversations, after-meal gatherings, feasts, the religious acts looking outward, as his obligations indicate, and gives way to God’s action.
  16. The principal moment: As regards the common life of a religious, Sunday is a principal feast day. Along with it, the other solemnities. These days must be very different from the rest of the week. It must be exalted by every means. Effort and time must not be spared so that this day may be the best; it must be looked forward to and then appreciated and given thanks for.

7. Conclusion of the novitiate

  1. Duration: “For validity, the novitiate must be carried out during twelve full months in the same novitiate community”91. The criteria for computing the twelve months required are those given by the Code of Canon Law92.
  2. Ways of conclusion: “A novice can freely leave the Institute; the competent authority of the Institute can dismiss him. At the end of the novitiate, if the novice is judged suitable, he is to be admitted to temporary profession; if any doubt remains about his suitability, the major Superior can extend the time of probation according to proper law, but not beyond six months”93.
  3. Free departure: The novice freely leaves the Institute whenever he wishes, and the law protects him in this decision. The Institute cannot retain him, even if his Superiors or the Master consider the decision unjustified. Although the Master may try by every means to convince him of his error, he cannot force him in the internal forum.
  4. Dismissal: Properly speaking, this is not a dismissal, but a non-admission or an interruption of the process of verifying the objectives of the novitiate, which belongs to the Provincial Superior who admitted him. Therefore no process is required. It is the freedom of the Institute to protect its own life, excluding those who could harm it. This freedom is relative and conditioned upon the existence of causes at least proportionate to the gravity of a positive dismissal. There is no obligation to make these causes known, although it is fitting for the spiritual good of the young man. But without such causes, it seems inconceivable that, once admitted, there could be an expulsion. And if causes must exist, it follows that the one dismissed cannot be left deprived of every right; at least a general recourse, in devolutivo, must be recognized for him.
  5. Extension: It is not desirable, but in some cases it can yield positive results. It is fitting when there are rational and well-founded doubts, or when the novice has given neither evident and compelling reasons for dismissal nor for admission. It is likewise the major Superior who can use this faculty, not the local Superior. It cannot be for more than six months. Even before the end of that period, the Superior can dismiss or admit him.
  6. Admission to profession: Only at the end of the novitiate and for one who is judged suitable. In practice, the indispensable reports on each of the novices must be taken into account, from the Master and the assistants, who issue them by virtue of their office, and from any others who may see fit to issue them at the request of the Superior.

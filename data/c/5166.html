Not long ago the American commentator Tucker Carlson said in his weekly column: “Is God dead? That is the famous question the magazine Times asked way back in 1966, when the magazine Times still mattered. The answer then, and still now, is no, God is not dead, but a great many of the people who believed in Him are dead. Which indicates that, not long ago, this was a Christian country. As recently as 2009, 77% of Americans said they considered themselves believing Christians. Only 10 years later, after the Obama administration, that number fell 12 points. Over the same period, the number of atheists and of people self-identified as non-religious in America jumped drastically. And that was before the covid epidemic. Politicians used the pandemic in the country to close thousands of churches and to throw Christians in prison for practicing their faith. […] But that does not mean—and this is the critical point—this does not mean that this has become a secular country. There are no secular countries, just as there are no secular people. Everyone believes in something. We are all born with the need to worship. The question is what”1. Then, the commentator goes on to argue: “America has not lost its religion. It has only replaced it. What is dying is the faith that created Western civilization, Christianity. In its place is found a new creed, which, like all religions, has its own sacraments, its own sacred texts”2.

This introductory paragraph, which Carlson uses to say that some American politicians promote the “cult of the coronavirus,” making it “the new religion”3—letting the situation of the Church in that country show through in some way—can well be applied—more or less—to the reality of the Church in not a few countries of the world, in which the situation becomes even more radical, no longer because of the ‘coronavirus’ but because of the ‘virus of worldly immanence’ that some of her members try to implant by sacrificing the prophetic spirit of the Church, making pacts or compromises with the spirit of the world in their eagerness to be ‘accepted’ by it. This is the reality we are called to live today and the battlefield on which we must fight at present.

Something very similar was denounced by Fr. Santiago Martín, FM, in a video published on September 24 of this year entitled “The ‘new Church’ has failed”4. There Fr. Martín pointed out that “a study states that only 12% of Germans consider religion to be something good for humanity. Catholics, Protestants and Jews are included in that percentage. This clearly indicates that, after everything they have been doing to adapt themselves to the world, they have failed. If only 12% consider religion to be something positive for humanity, it means that those who are promoting change—this change of the new Church—and who say that everything begins from now on and that one must be in rupture with everything that came before (the Word of God and Tradition): they have failed […].

The churches are ever emptier, and this idea that by making things easy, adapting to the world order, accepting what we are asked to accept (the whole complete package, from abortion to gender ideology), this idea that this surrender of what we are and what we believe will serve to bring people closer to the Church, that is a lie. Reality is there to prove it […].

Today it is not a question of whether we interpret the Second Vatican Council in a key of rupture or of continuity; the immense majority of the baptized simply do not care about that. Some years ago the Catholic Church was a minority; today the Catholic Church is irrelevant. Not in the whole world, of course, but, for example, in Germany. If 12% consider religion to be something positive for humanity, that means that 88% consider that religion is good for nothing. Similar cases occur in other parts of the world—although not so radically—and even in the USA, where the percentage of atheists is proportionally equal to the number of believers. Likewise in Latin America, where young people have deserted en masse.

This shows that the ‘new church’ has failed, that even with their reductions already applied, tolerated in their application, they have not managed to halt the decline in which the Church already was and which with them has become more pronounced. There is only one solution: unity, which cannot be achieved except around the Truth (in the Word of God and Tradition)”5.

As members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word and of the Church, this reality is not foreign to us. For we have been summoned to unite under the standard of the Incarnate Word for “the mission of prophetically opposing the idolatry of power, of possessions and of pleasure”6. Since it is Christ Himself who “fulfills His prophetic mission until the full manifestation of glory […] through the hierarchy, which teaches in His name and with His power”7.

Our Institute being “a clerical Institute”8 and, therefore, knowing ourselves to be sharers “in the prophetic function of Christ”9, completely dedicated “to the building up of the Church and to the salvation of the world”10, it has seemed fitting to us to devote these lines to treating, in some way, the present reality of the Church so as, from there, to try to describe what our attitude and our conduct in the face of it must be.

Likewise, it seems important to us to clarify that our purpose is not to criticize or to adopt an attitude of protest, as do some who in practice pass from sentire cum Ecclesia to agere contra Ecclesiam, living hierarchical communion dialectically, and therefore not living it, setting the “official or hierarchical Church” against the “Church of the people of God”11 and thus promoting the attitudes of dissent that do so much harm to ecclesial communion, and that disguise themselves as prophetic denunciations. We want to be prophets of light and hope, offering an explicit witness, following the exhortation St. John Paul II made to us: “We must proclaim our beliefs in the light of day and from the housetops12, without fear and without compromise”13.

1. The Church in the desert

We have entitled the present writing “The Church in the desert” because it seems to us that—following in this idea the Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen—“the Church of today is like the Church in the desert”14. By the expression ‘Church of the desert’ we refer to the ‘church of the Israelites,’ to the People of God when, after 400 years of slavery in Egypt, they began their march toward the Promised Land of Canaan.

Similarly, the contemporary Church resembles the ancient ‘church of the desert’ in three respects:

▪ Contempt for the hierarchy, the manna or the Eucharist: certainly, until the Second Vatican Council the Church was more separated from the spirit of the world than she is now. But ever since a legitimate emphasis was placed on the Church needing to be more involved in the various aspects of the life of the world and its needs, the desire for the fleshpots of Egypt15, that is, for what is worldly, began to grow to gigantic proportions. That is why some priests and religious feel ever more uncomfortable at being called ‘conservative’ (because they associate it with rigidity in its negative connotation) or at being identified with someone from the ‘top’ (because in the hierarchy they see only oppression). This is followed by a decline in the reverence due to the Eucharist, in the same way that there was a rejection of the manna among the Israelites: The Israelites sat down and wept aloud, saying: “If only we had meat to eat!… Now our throats are parched! We are deprived of everything, and our eyes see nothing but the manna!”16. Then one begins to crave the pleasures of this world. How we remember the fish we ate for nothing in Egypt, and the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions and the garlic!17. Abortion, homosexuality, the rejection of religious vows, certain esoteric practices and not a few worldly concerns that ‘belonged to Egypt’ are now accepted and even promoted and defended by some, even within the Church herself. One no longer sees a morally solid phalanx18 hurling itself against the spirit of the world. What the Church believes or the Holy Father teaches or the Word of God warns us no longer matters so much; each one’s conscience has become by itself the sole criterion of what is right and what is wrong. Here each one does whatever seems best to him19. And so we have members of the very hierarchy of the Church who dissent on crucial aspects of our faith, while others live excessively preoccupied with irrelevant or culturally fashionable topics while the Mystical Body of Christ bleeds to death. How many live busy diluting the holy doctrine of the Church, setting aside the Word of God and Tradition, alleging for this that ‘these are different times.’ There are also not a few who, in their eagerness to draw near to the world, to “get in” with people, and always under the ‘guise of good,’ do everything to do away with whatever has the slightest shadow of the traditional—with which they associate the negative notes of ‘conservative’ or ‘sectarian mentality’—: religious congregations, missions, the preaching of the last things, fidelity to the rule and to the patrimony of an Institute, etc., to name a few examples. Such people end up uncritically embracing the culture of the world, renouncing permeating it with the Gospel, and they of course expect others to do the same. It is the way of living of many Christians, present at every level of the life of the Church.

▪ The second respect in which the Church of today resembles the church that journeyed through the desert is that both show rebellion against authority. The Israelites believed that all were equal, regardless of any call received from God. Authority became repugnant to them, whether out of envy or because their ego became their god and they could not tolerate other gods above them. The People of God protested against the authority of Moses and Aaron. You have gone too far! The whole community is holy, and the Lord is in their midst. Why then do you set yourselves above the assembly of the Lord?20. The Pope, the bishops, the parish priests, the religious superiors and priests are questioned, because there is a kind of general feeling that whoever resents authority has greater holiness. The truth is that of both the old and the new People of God it could be said: not all who are descended from Israel are truly Israelites21, that is, not all in the Church are truly of the Church22. And today as yesterday there are attempts to lower the authority or credibility of superiors or of whoever has moral authority, for example, in a religious congregation, so that they will not be heard or so that they will not lead23.

▪ Lastly, Fulton Sheen points out, the Church of today is like Israel in transition. And just as Israel was between Egypt and the Promised Land, so the Church in the present desert wanders between what she is and what she will eventually be. For just as the Israelites were brought out of Egypt but were not immediately brought into Canaan, so the Church is in transition. This being in an ‘intermediate state’ does not mean that when the Church recovers from the reigning secularism and atheism she will be perfect. When Israel entered Canaan, they had to fight seven battles. Canaan was not heaven.

The Church today also wanders. Nothing seems fixed except the fact that it is God who leads His Church. What is more, just as Israel in transitu from Egypt to Canaan frequently lost heart in the face of difficulties, so the new Israel—the Church of today—tends to lose heart; that is why God from on high says to us again today: Take courage, all you people of the land!—oracle of the Lord—. To work! For I am with you—oracle of the Lord of hosts24.

Now, it seems important to us to note this observation of the Venerable American archbishop: “the Church is something that dies and comes back to life constantly”25. Because the law of the Body is the law of the Head, that is, Crucifixion and the empty tomb: Resurrection26.

2. Worldliness

There were many, and there still are now, who, given the alarming circumstances through which the Church went or is going, foretell the end of Christendom. Carlson himself said so in the commentary we quoted at the beginning. The truth is that “the Church has had four great deaths in history, one every five hundred years. The first was the fall of Rome, which disturbed St. Jerome to such a degree that it was thought to be the end of the world. St. Augustine spent eighteen years writing the City of God to explain why Rome had fallen. The second death of the Church came with the advance of the Muslims, which destroyed the Church in North Africa, and also the Eastern schism. The third death was during the time of the Reformation, when the ‘reformers’ changed dogmas, when in reality it was their moral conduct that they ought to have reformed. We are in the fourth death of the Church, where the enemy is not nomadic peoples, schismatics or heretics, but the spirit of the world that has infiltrated the Church”27.

The Venerable American archbishop goes on to say that “during cycles of 500 years the Church was attacked in different ways. In the first cycle the Church was fought with heresies centered on the historical Christ: His Person, His Nature, Intellect and Will. In the second cycle it was the Visible Head of Christ that they denied. In the third cycle it was the Church or the Mystical Body of Christ that was divided into sections or sects. In our days, the attack is secularism, and it is aimed at attacking holiness, sacrifice, self-denial and kenosis. The new enemy of the Church is ecological; it belongs to the environment in which the Church lives. The challenge that now swirls around the faithful is: Are you on board with this? [Are you with it?] – that ‘this’ always remains anonymous and undefined. For that reason it is closely related to the demonic. For God defines Himself as ‘I Am Who Am.’ The devil defines himself as ‘I am who am not’”28.

Fulton Sheen concludes by saying something we have already quoted at other times but which it seems opportune to us to recall once more: “We are living at the end of Christendom, not of Christianity. By Christendom we mean the political, economic and social order imbued with the ethics of the Gospel. We no longer live in a Christian civilization. Christendom refers only to the world and its institutions; Christianity refers to Christ and His Mystical Body in its evidently worldwide reach. The age of faith has been followed by the age of reason, which, in turn, has given way to the age of the senses. Christianity is considered out of place. […] But Christianity is not dead; on the contrary, it is very much alive. Forty or fifty years ago it was easy to be a Christian; the air we breathed, the family atmosphere in which we grew up were not foreign to the Sermon on the Mount. Now Christianity is under attack. That means these are magnificent days to be alive. Now we must stand up to be counted. It is easy to float along with the current. Dead bodies float along with the current. But only living bodies resist the current”29.

That is why the Holy Father Francis warned in one of his homilies, paraphrasing Fr. De Lubac: “the worst of the evils that can befall the Church is worldliness. And he does not exaggerate, because he then points out some evils that are terrible, and this is worse: spiritual worldliness, because it is a hermeneutic of life, it is a way of living, also a way of living Christianity. And in order to survive in the face of the preaching of the Gospel, it hates. It kills”30. And the Pope goes on to say: “Worldliness is not superficial at all! It has deep roots, deep roots. It is like a chameleon, it changes, it comes and goes according to circumstances, but the substance is the same: a proposal of life that gets in everywhere, even into the Church. Worldliness, worldly hermeneutic, makeup—everything is made up so that it is so”31.

“Worldliness is a culture; it is a culture of the ephemeral, a culture of appearance, of makeup, a culture of ‘today yes, tomorrow no; tomorrow yes and today no.’ It has superficial values. A culture that does not know fidelity, because it changes according to circumstances, it negotiates everything. This is the worldly culture, the culture of worldliness. And Jesus insists on defending us from this and prays that the Father may defend us from this culture of worldliness. It is a throwaway culture, according to convenience. It is a culture without loyalty; it has no roots. But it is a form of life, a way of living, also of many who call themselves Christians. They are Christians, but they are worldly”32.

Indeed, worldliness is “infinitely more disastrous than any other merely moral worldliness”33.

3. The Church, a prophetic community

That is why St. John Paul II firmly affirmed: “the mission of the Church is, above all, prophetic. She proclaims Christ to all nations and transmits to them the message of salvation. […] Today’s world everywhere awaits, perhaps confusedly, consecrated lives that proclaim Christ and His Gospel more by deeds than by words”34.

“Christ did not force anyone to accept His teachings. He presented them to all without exception, leaving each one free to respond to His invitation. This is the model that we, His disciples, must follow. We Christians affirm that every man and every woman has the right to hear the message of salvation that Christ has left us, and we affirm that they have the right to follow it if it convinces them. Far from feeling obliged to apologize for making the message of Christ available to all, we are convinced that we have the right and the obligation to do so”35.

When the Church loses sight of that prophetic mission, she then moves away from the ideal of Christ by ‘making a pact’ with the world. How does she do so? By diluting or cutting down the message of our Lord, by conceding things that are inadmissible, calling good or morally right something that the fashion of the age considers ‘good or right,’ remaining trapped in the slogans of ‘inclusion’ (at all costs) instead of proclaiming the Gospel truth with firm confidence, adopting an attitude that is rather a surrender at the feet of the world and its maxims. She also does so when, with a ‘self-conscious’ attitude, she asks forgiveness for concrete decisions or measures taken by the hierarchy of the Church in the past simply because they do not fit the contemporary pluralist and pseudo-philanthropic mentality, which only leads the enemies of the Church to feel confirmed in their calumnies. She does so by thinking herself ‘brave,’ not for contradicting or refuting the message of the world with the truth of Christ and His cross in the face of the corruption of political or economic power36, or for showing herself ardent in her missionary zeal and wanting to make real the desire of Christ: I have come to bring fire upon the earth, and how I wish it were already blazing!37, but because she likes to appear pacifist, humanitarian and ‘open-minded,’ negotiating everything, not without grave scandal for souls. It is worldly to love being right more than loving the truth. Those who act thus are not friends of the truth, which is Christ Himself.

How many members of the Church today—even highly committed in the Church—write books, promote ideas, and openly declare themselves in favor of issues explicitly condemned by the Magisterium of the Church, simply in order to ‘accommodate themselves’ to the way people live; how many with their gestures and even with their practices lead souls to ‘faith’ in astrology or other practices of a markedly pagan tone. How many think themselves so open-minded (broadminded) and with so much ‘freedom of spirit’ (denigrating what they, according to their own criterion, consider ‘rigid’ or ‘fanaticism’) that they identify tolerance with indifference about what is right and what is wrong, truth and error. How many speak of ‘doing away with the ‘old paradigm,’ an expression they use to refer to the eternal truth taught by the Church of all times, in order to suggest, or rather to impose with sophisms, a ‘new paradigm,’ broader, which accepts and conforms to the ‘new norms.’ How many, instead of supporting, fostering and safeguarding the religious Institutes faithful to their identity and mission within the Church, seek to dynamite precisely what defines their face in the Church, namely, their charism, their rules, their patrimony, their way of doing apostolate, their founder, etc., arguing for this that those Institutes foster in their members a ‘sectarian mentality,’ ‘self-referentiality’ or ‘single-mindedness’’ when in reality those Institutes seek only to be faithful to their mission, not to ‘dominate the space of the Church’38 nor to display a “self-referential and Promethean neo-Pelagianism, like those who ultimately trust only in their own strength and feel superior to others because they observe certain norms or because they are unshakably faithful to a certain Catholic style proper to the past”39. How many others live agitated by the cause of ecology, of environmental pollution, of equality, of the cult of health (which has nothing to do with the pastoral care of the sick as the Church understands it), of immigrants, etc.; all of them valid things, with which one must be concerned, bringing to them the spirit of the Gospel. But at the same time nothing or almost nothing is heard about missionary promotion, about vocational ministry, about the solid and consistent preaching throughout the centuries on the necessity of receiving the sacraments, about the last things, about the Cross of Christ, about the open and clear preaching of the Truth of Christ regarding so many controversial issues that cause so much confusion and havoc in souls, even in priests. Even more, there are some who invoke religion to destroy religion, since they even speak of Christ, but not of His Cross, simply because worldliness does not tolerate the scandal of the Cross.

The truth is that when souls perceive that duplicity, when they realize that ‘negotiation’ with the spirit of the world, when in their churches they no longer hear the proclamation of the immutable truths of our faith, when they hear only opinions, when instead of ‘teachers’ they find ‘commentators,’ when instead of receiving principles they find only statistics, only nature instead of nature and grace; when they are asked to sell their freedom in exchange for security (like bread that becomes a political weapon, and only those who think as they do will be able to eat); when they hear ‘brotherhood’ promoted but without mention of the ‘fatherhood’ of God, when they see high-ranking members of the Church render to Caesar even the things that are God’s, then they lose confidence in ‘Church people,’ others are scandalized, others decide no longer to believe, and, what is worse, so many others join them in embracing worldliness.

Who after that will be surprised that vocations to the priesthood are scarce, that churches are empty or almost empty, that religious houses and monasteries are closing at an incomparable speed, that souls do not come to the sacraments, that euthanasia is chosen almost ‘naturally,’ that homosexuality is for many—even Christians—a ‘way of life,’ that so many countries build models so far removed from the common good…? For “every counter-witness, every incoherence between how values or ideals are expressed and how they are in fact lived, every seeking of oneself and not of the Kingdom of God and His justice40, every falsification of the word of God41, ‘are frequently strong obstacles for those who feel the call of Christ: come, follow me…’42”43.

Truly, everywhere, people are waiting for bishops, cardinals, priests, monks and religious who are witnesses of Christ, consistent with the truth they preach. They do not go to churches to have repeated to them the same thing the newscasts say, nor to be told about some ecclesial scandal: souls thirst for Christ; they want the message of Christ to be presented to them in an accessible way, without mutilations, without diluting it, but rather in all its luminosity and transparency.

We must not be naive, but know how to discern and realize that today as yesterday there are in the Church those who behave as false prophets, even within the hierarchy.

That is why we wish to bring up here certain notes that the Spiritual Father of our Religious Family gave us for distinguishing true prophets from false ones, and in this very thing to learn what our attitude must be. He says:

– “In Israel’s contacts with the neighboring peoples there was no lack of manifestations of false prophecy, which led to the formation of groups of fanatics, who replaced the spirit coming from God with music and gesticulations and even adhered to the cult of Baal. Elijah engaged in a decisive battle against those prophets44, remaining solitary in his greatness”45. And there we have the first attitude in the face of the times we are living, if we truly want to be faithful to our vocation and, ultimately, faithful to the Incarnate Word: the firm and determined determination (that is, with conviction) not to enter into compromises with the world, not to replace the truth with fashionable opinions, not to adhere—out of fear of threats or in exchange for passing goods—to anything that compromises the integrity of our faith, of our religious and missionary commitment, of the loyalty we owe to our God. In a word, to know “at every instant the things for which one must die”46. And this out of love for men, that is, precisely out of love for the “world” of men, understood as a good reality, created by God, but which must be saved and elevated to the order of grace, given that “what is not assumed is not redeemed”47.

– “In the genuine biblical tradition the true idea of the prophet is defended and vindicated as a man of the word of God, instituted by God, like Moses and following after him48”49. If a religious, priest, bishop or cardinal does not conduct himself in accordance with his condition and with the authentic prophetic witness that is expected of all of us, and, even more, neglects to deepen the Word of God “in the Church”50, that is, with the same Spirit with which it was written51, then his apostolic labor languishes or, worse still, does harm and destroys instead of building up. Likewise, if our missionary labor within the Church does not find in the Word of God its source, its message, its criterion for judging realities, as well as the food for our own sanctification, we could not speak of evangelization of culture, for—in the best of cases—we would only be flailing at the air. “Our poor breath is fruitful and irresistible only if it is in communication with the wind of Pentecost”52, for precisely the Word of God is the strength of our Institute53.

– Another aspect of that criterion of judgment is fidelity to the doctrine given by God to Israel, in resistance to the seductions of idolatry (cf. Deut 1:2 ff.). This explains the hostility against the false prophets54. The task of the prophet, as a man of the Word of God, is to combat the “lying spirit” found in the mouth of the false prophets55, in order to protect the people from their influence. One of our documents says it very clearly: “The Christian [and even more so the religious] may pass through a thousand trials, but he must never be confused regarding the Word of God. […] The Word of God must not be silenced: the Antichrist will come in the midst of the silence of the Word of God. The devil himself arrives when the Word of God is silenced: The one who receives the Word among thorns is the man who hears the Word, but the cares of the world and the seduction of riches choke it and they cannot bear fruit56. The Word of God must not be peddled: But we are not like many who peddle the Word of God; rather, we speak with sincerity in the name of Christ, as sent by God and in the presence of God Himself57. The Word of God must not be falsified: …and we have never kept silent about anything out of shame; we have not acted with cunning or falsified the Word of God58”59.

– The prophet, a man of the word, must also be “a man of the spirit,” as Hosea already calls him: he must have the spirit of God, and not only his own spirit, if he is to speak in the name of God60. […] Speaking in the name of God requires, in the prophet, the presence of the spirit of God61. That is why St. Gregory the Great recommended: “Take care first to chastise what is your own with tears, and afterwards cry out against what must be chastised in others; and before the words of exhortation resound, shout with your works everything you are to say”62.

Moreover, fidelity to God and firm adherence to His Word also produce certain effects63 in those who receive the message, and these effects serve us to judge whether our own apostolic work or that of others is fulfilling the prophetic mission expected of us:

– incomprehension: you seek to kill me, because my word has not been received by you64; the disciples did not understand it…65;

– contradiction: some said: «He is good…», others: «no, he leads the crowd astray…»66;

– hatred: the world… hates me, because I testify against it that its works are evil67;

– contempt: all the people listened and had themselves baptized by John, but the Pharisees and doctors did not have themselves baptized by him68;

– scandal: they sought to kill him… because… he called God his Father… 69;

– repugnance: These are hard words! Who can listen to them?70.

“To be tolerated [or accepted] is sometimes a sign of weakness; to be persecuted is a compliment. The mediocre survive”71, said Fulton Sheen.

4. Pilgrimage through the desert

Therefore, this whole ecclesial situation we were mentioning, which concerns us doubly, namely, by our condition as baptized and by our belonging to the Institute—although, humanly speaking, it may overwhelm us, worry us, and even make us lose confidence in certain men of the Church—must in reality make us raise our gaze to God, who from the height of heaven governs His Church and directs history, so that we may be able to say with the psalmist: In you our fathers trusted, they trusted, and you brought them to safety; to you they cried, and they were set free; in you they trusted, and you did not disappoint them72.

This Psalm we have just quoted “presents the figure of an innocent man persecuted and surrounded by adversaries who want his death; and he has recourse to God in a sorrowful lament which, in the certainty of faith, opens mysteriously to praise. In his prayer the anguishing reality of the present and the consoling memory of the past alternate, in a suffering awareness of his own desperate situation which, nevertheless, does not want to renounce hope”73. Although the Psalm has strong Christological implications, it does not seem far-fetched to us that it can be applied to the Mystical Body of Christ that journeys as a pilgrim through the desert, and even more to our Institute itself.

Pope Benedict explained in one of his catecheses: “That God who today seems so distant to the psalmist is, nevertheless, the merciful Lord whom Israel always experienced in its history. The people to which the one praying belongs was the object of God’s love and can bear witness to His fidelity. Beginning with the patriarchs, then in Egypt and in the long pilgrimage through the desert, in the sojourn in the promised land in contact with aggressive and hostile populations, down to the darkness of exile, all of biblical history was a history of cries for help on the part of the people and of saving responses on the part of God. And the psalmist refers to the unshakable faith of his fathers, who trusted—this word is repeated three times—without ever being disappointed. Now, however, it seems that this chain of trusting invocations and divine responses has been interrupted; the situation of the psalmist seems to belie the whole history of salvation, making the present reality even more painful.

But God cannot go back on His word, and so the prayer returns to describing the sad situation of the one praying, in order to induce the Lord to have pity and intervene, as He had always done in the past. The psalmist calls himself a worm, not a man, the shame of men, despised by the people74; they mock him, they jeer at him75, and he is wounded precisely in his faith: He went to the Lord; let Him bring him to safety; let Him deliver him if He loves him so much, they say”76. Allowing for the differences, these lines inevitably bring to mind many vicissitudes in the history of our Institute. And yet God has always been present in our history with an unquestionable providence and merciful tenderness. Therefore, our Institute can well remind God together with the psalmist: You are the one who drew me from the womb; you kept me safe at my mother’s breasts; from the womb I passed into your hands77. Notwithstanding the desolation of the present, and within the framework of preparing for the celebration of the Birth of our Redeemer, we must acknowledge a divine closeness and love so radical that we exclaim, in a confession full of faith and generative of hope: from my mother’s womb you are my God78. And let us confidently implore together with the psalmist: Do not stay far off, for danger is near and no one helps me79.

It is true that many times almost the only closeness we perceive, and which can frighten us, is that of the enemies who corner us, and they are like powerful bulls, like lions that open their mouths wide to roar and devour80. “Anguish alters the perception of danger, magnifying it. The adversaries appear invincible; they have become fierce and most dangerous animals, while the psalmist is like a little worm, powerless, without any defense. But these images used in the Psalm also serve to say that when man becomes brutal and attacks his brother, something animal-like takes the lead in him; he seems to lose all human appearance; violence always has something bestial in it, and only the saving intervention of God can restore to man his humanity. Now, for the psalmist, the object of such a ferocious aggression, it seems there is no longer any salvation, and death begins to take possession of him: I am like water poured out, my bones are out of joint […] my throat is dry as a potsherd, my tongue sticks to my palate […] they divide my clothing among them, they cast lots for my tunic81”82.

United in prayer, let us once again urgently direct our petition for help: But you, Lord, do not stay far off; my strength, come running to help me […] Save me83. “This is a cry that opens the heavens,” the Pope explains, “because it proclaims a faith, a certainty that goes beyond all doubt, all darkness and all desolation. And the lament is transformed; it gives way to praise in the welcoming of salvation: You have answered me. I will tell of your fame to my brothers; in the midst of the assembly I will praise you84”85.

Let us dispose ourselves to thanksgiving, because at all times God has protected the poor and helpless, as at the end of this Psalm. “The Lord came to his aid, saved the poor man and showed him His face of mercy. Death and life intertwined in an inseparable mystery, and life has triumphed; the God of salvation showed Himself the invincible Lord […]. It is the victory of faith, which can transform death into the gift of life, the abyss of sorrow into a source of hope”86.

In this way, placing once again all our trust and our hope in God the Father, in the present moment of anguish we too can pray to Him with faith, and our cry for help, if it pleases God, will be transformed into a song of praise.

Let us be persuaded that “the Church ‘will receive her perfection only in the glory of heaven’87, when Christ returns in glory. Until that day, ‘the Church advances on her pilgrimage amid the persecutions of the world and the consolations of God’88. Here below, she knows herself to be in exile, far from the Lord89, and longs for the full coming of the Kingdom, ‘and hopes and desires with all her strength to be united with her King in glory’90. The consummation of the Church in glory, and through her that of the world, will not take place without great trials”91.

5. Mary, Mother of the Church

“Yours is my blessed Mother, to be for you a careful and loving Mother,” our Proper Law tells us in a fatherly way, quoting the Master of Ávila92. That is why we must invoke the Most Blessed Virgin at all times, so that she may shine more than ever before us in mercy, power and grace93.

St. Paul VI, desiring that the title of Mary, Mother of the Church, should acquire an ever more important place in the liturgy and in the piety of the People of God journeying through the desert, proclaimed the Most Blessed Virgin “Mother of the Church, that is, Mother of the whole People of God, both of the faithful and of the pastors, who call her loving Mother”94 in a memorable address at the close of the Second Vatican Council. On that occasion the Vicar of Christ pointed out: “The reality of the Church is not exhausted in her hierarchical structure, in her liturgy, in her sacraments, or in her juridical ordinances. Her intimate essence, the principal source of her sanctifying efficacy, must be sought in her mystical union with Christ; a union that we cannot conceive of as separated from her who is the Mother of the Incarnate Word, and whom Christ Himself willed to be so intimately united to Himself for our salvation. Thus the loving contemplation of the wonders that God has worked in His Holy Mother must be framed within the vision of the Church. And knowledge of the true Catholic doctrine on Mary will always be the key to the exact understanding of the mystery of Christ and of the Church”95.

Likewise, more recently the Holy Father has definitively included in the General Roman Calendar the memorial of the “Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of the Church”96. That is why, with confident spirit and filial love, let us raise our gaze to her, despite our unworthiness and weakness; she, who gave us in Christ the source of grace, will not fail to come to the aid of the Church and of the Institute that bears the name of the most august mystery of her Most Adorable Son.

May the Most Blessed Virgin protect the whole Church, and ourselves within the Church, from falling into appearances of religiosity and even of love for the Church which, instead of the glory of the Lord, seek human glory and personal well-being97. And Mary being at once Spouse of the Holy Spirit and Mother of the Church, may she heal the members of the Church of that “suffocating worldliness” with the pure wind of the Holy Spirit, who frees us from being centered on ourselves, hidden in a religious appearance empty of God98.

*****

In view of the luminous mystery of the Birth of the Son of God, an immense confidence in the power and the help of our Redeemer must be rekindled in us. Christmas is, then, the “day of the mercies of God”99. Indeed, “is there anything that can declare the mercy of God more unequivocally than the fact that He accepted our misery? What is more overflowing with compassion than the Word of God become so small a thing for us?”100.

That is why “the birth of the Incarnate Word urges us, among other things… to live in joy”101. A deep and spiritual joy, even in spite of the arduous struggles and trials that we do not lack, nor does God want us to lack. It is, therefore, our fervent prayer that this Christmas the Incarnate Word may permeate the souls of all the members of the Institute with the dew of joy that multiplies good works into more graces: the joy of remaining independent in the face of the maxims, mockery and persecutions of the world out of loyalty to God102, the joy of our belonging to the Church through faith and Baptism103, joy in the strength of the possumus104 and in the weakness of save us, Lord, we are sinking!105. Joyful when they throw stones at us, strike us and martyr us106 for the cause of Christ, joyful when they take us “for dull, backward and even feeble-minded”107, because then the greatest grace that God can grant to our tiny Religious Family will have fallen to our lot. Joyful in God and with Him alone108; joyful in faith and hope, even if it be in darkness109; joyful, “living here as pilgrims, poor, exiled, orphaned, dry, without a road and without anything, hoping for everything there”110.

This is the reality of the Christmas of the Lord! This is the historical event, laden with mystery, that God has entrusted to His Church. Let us proclaim it unceasingly.

To conclude, we offer here a homemade translation of a poem that Ven. Archbishop Fulton Sheen wrote to a friend three days before his death111:

Our Lord came to this earth to experience

what it feels like when we feel let down…

When we fail to get a reservation at a hotel;

when we have to bump into a number of stubborn donkeys

and a number of stupid oxen on our way through life;

when we do not receive the birthday presents we were expecting…

BUT. He also came to teach us that we would never feel let down…

If we find the Love before which all other loves fall short;

if we learn to see that, next to His Presence in the Blessed Sacrament,

our neighbor is the object most like Christ that we will ever know;

and if we learn to admit the dirtiness of the stable of our lives,

we will have found the first sign of His Presence in us…

Then we would understand that if He freed us from every disappointment;

if He gave us only pleasant and new things;

we would forget Him

and that would take from us the joy of longing for one another.

Merry Christmas!

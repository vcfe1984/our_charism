Memorial of St. John Paul II

[Exordium] Dear all in the Incarnate Word, we are gathered here in this magnificent Basilica of St. Peter, whose walls rise as silent witnesses of the innumerable demonstrations and teachings of holiness of the successor of St. Peter whom we celebrate today. I am referring to our beloved Spiritual Father, His Holiness John Paul II.

This celebration is of special importance for our Religious Family, not only because we are celebrating the “great missionary of the twentieth century,” who “knew how to write with his life a sublime epic deed” and governed the Universal Church with constant solicitude and heroic generosity, but also because “his magisterium animates the fundamental aspects of [our] charism,” making it a “fruitful source from which we quench our thirst for fidelity to the Lord.” That is why this occasion fills our soul with great joy.

The Gospel we have just heard ends with the irresistible invitation of Our Lord: Follow me! 1, after St. Peter’s threefold affirmation of love, and after the Incarnate Word had announced to him his martyrdom. This reminds us that the condition of a true apostle is the cross, because, as Christ himself teaches us: the good Shepherd gives his life for the sheep2. These words serve as the perfect frame for contemplating the life of this man -a priest- who “died as he had always lived, animated by the indomitable courage of faith, abandoning himself to God and entrusting himself to Most Holy Mary”3, giving himself to the very end.

Today the figure of St. John Paul II rises before us “with the strength of a giant”4 as a “model and powerful intercessor of the good Shepherd, and invites us to follow in his footsteps and his loving self-giving without limits”5.

And I would like to emphasize these words: “without limits,” because in them are summed up the “consistency of his faith, the radicalism of his Christian life and the desire for holiness that he continually manifested”6.

An author says that “God sends to each age the saint who excels in the virtue the world most needs”7. John Paul II was “a man of colossal virtues,” a “hero of the seven seas.” And all his biographers remind us that Karol Wojtyła was “a gift from God” in one of the darkest moments of the twentieth century. That is why today I would like us to reflect together on the marvelous invitation to heroism that his example and his words set before us.

1. Heroic forgetfulness of self

St. Thomas Aquinas points out that “virtue consists in following or imitating God”8. And that “man must raise himself beyond his natural life toward the divine life,” following the command of our Lord: Be perfect as your heavenly father is perfect. And of this St. John Paul II gave us an eloquent example from his youth.

It is well known that when he had just begun his university studies, with the Nazis in power in his native Poland promoting a regime of terror in the country, he showed heroic courage by joining the clandestine seminary run by the Cardinal of Kraków. For he was convinced that “vocation is a divine initiative,” and it was the Incarnate Word who whispered in the depths of his soul the Follow me that we have just heard in the Gospel.

When he asked to enter the seminary, the rector told him: “I accept you, but not even your mother must know that you are studying here”9. So he studied on his own from the manuals he received from his professors and, as he himself recounts, “I studied theology in secret”10 while “I worked in the factory”11, which required exquisite discipline. But far from becoming discouraged, he studied with heroic enthusiasm in the midst of work and of the dangers to which he was exposed because of it. Later he reflected on this, saying: “That was the situation then. Even so I managed to keep going”12.

That is why once this great connoisseur of the human heart told us with great ardor: “Do not be discouraged! You are not alone in walking the way [of the cross]. Jesus walks with you”13. “Do not be afraid to welcome Christ and to accept his power”14. “Always look upward: look to Christ, who is at once the author, the giver and the absolute exemplar of the priesthood. From Him you will obtain the satisfaction and the joy of following Him and serving Him in souls. Look upward –and he encouraged everyone to recall the words of St. John XXIII, who wrote in his Journal–: ‘I must always convince myself of this great truth: Jesus wants from me […] not just a mediocre virtue, but the highest: He is not content with me until I make myself, or at least strive with all my strength to make myself, a saint’15”16.

St. John Paul II was deeply persuaded that holiness “is not a kind of extraordinary life, practiced only by a few ‘geniuses’ of holiness”17; that is why he exhorted everyone to live life to the full, with heroism, with unlimited trust in the Goodness of God the Father, telling us: “We are not the sum of our weaknesses and our failures; on the contrary, we are the sum of the Father’s love for us and of our real capacity to become the image of his Son”18, that is, to become saints.

And so, on one occasion he told seminarians: “The discipline of seminary life must be valued not only as an effective defense of common life and charity, but as a necessary part of the whole formation, in order to acquire self-mastery, because it is in this way that one forges the dispositions of soul that are of the greatest use for the ordered and fruitful activity of the Church”19, that is, to die and bear fruit. “It is in the atmosphere of recollection created by seminary discipline that those attitudes so desirable in a priest develop interiorly, such as joyful obedience, generosity and self-sacrifice”20.

You see, then, how St. John Paul II understood that the capacity to give ourselves even to the point of heroism is already formed in the seminary, in the house of formation. It is in the seminary that one learns -he said- the “habit of forgetfulness of self”21, which is, the Holy Father added, “an indispensable condition for loving truly and caring only for the interests of Christ”22.

And so, even in the midst of the communist tyranny that soon replaced the Nazi regime, and already ordained a priest, he showed with new ardor his priestly mettle and courage when, in the face of harassment by the regime, of unjust criticism, of the threat of severe punishment, he carried on his priestly work with great fortitude, devoting himself to the formation of young people in the great Catholic spiritual and theological tradition. He spared no effort to bring the Gospel of Christ to all men, without claiming anything for himself. He gave us the example of an incisive, enthusiastic pastoral work; not one of waiting, but of proposing23. Convinced that “pastorally speaking there is nothing more effective than total death to one’s own self”24.

He kept very much in mind that on the day of his ordination he had prostrated himself on the ground. This “is a sign -he said- of the total gift of oneself to Christ, who, in order to fulfill his priestly mission, emptied himself of his rank and took the condition of a slave […]. And so, acting like any other man, he lowered himself even to submitting to death, and death on a cross”25. Karol Wojtyła understood his priesthood “as a self-giving marked by total commitment”26.

So much so that, already as Successor of Peter and after having suffered the assassination attempt here in St. Peter’s Square in 1981, after a long hospitalization and convalescence, he intensified his pastoral commitments with heroic generosity. Because “to become a personal witness of the Good Shepherd,” whom one must “love with all one’s soul and all one’s heart, so that this love may be the norm and the driving force of all our actions,” the Holy Father affirmed, “renunciation and mortification are indispensable”27.

With these sentiments of heroism, of love to the extreme, with full awareness of the radicality of his call to be a “servant of the power of Christ”28 -as he identified himself- beat the heart of our beloved St. John Paul II, called the Great, and Spiritual Father of our Religious Family. How much his example enlightens us!

Of this self-giving of the Holy Father to the end and without cutting corners, Pope Emeritus Benedict XVI also bore witness when, speaking of his predecessor, he said: “The Holy Father was a priest to the end, because he offered his life to God for his sheep and for the entire human family, in a daily self-giving in the service of the Church and above all in the harsh trials of the last months”29.

2. Heroic fidelity

Like all the saints, John Paul II did not admit a half-hearted following of Christ, but on the contrary invited everyone to a heroic fidelity, to the point of complete configuration with our Lord, the Good Shepherd.

“We have consecrated our life to Christ, who loved us first and who, as a good shepherd, sacrificed his own life for us.” That is why he considered it a duty of loyalty to Christ that “we too, pastors of the Church,” be “the first to commit ourselves to responding to this love by being faithful, keeping the commandments and offering our life daily for the friends of our Lord”30.

Especially to us –consecrated persons– he taught on one occasion: “To understand what it means to be faithful, we must look to Christ, the faithful Witness 31, the Son who learned obedience through what he suffered32; to Jesus who said: I do not seek my own will, but the will of him who sent me33.” And he added: “We look to Jesus, not only to see and contemplate his fidelity to the Father in spite of all difficulties34, but also to learn from Him the means he used in order to be faithful: especially prayer and abandonment to the will of God35”36. Because ‑as he himself explained with great emphasis on one of his pastoral visits-: “[It is] necessary that the heroic become normal, everyday, and that the normal, everyday, become heroic”37.

And so he encouraged everyone, saying: “Remember that in the final analysis perseverance in fidelity is a proof not of human courage and strength, but of the efficacy of the grace of Christ. Therefore, if we are to persevere, we must be men of prayer who, through the Eucharist, the Liturgy of the Hours and personal encounters with Christ, find the courage and the grace to be faithful. Let us trust, then, remembering the words of St. Paul: I can do all things in Him who strengthens me38”39.

With what force the words he spoke at the beginning of his pontificate resound now: “Do not be afraid! Open the doors to Christ!”40, inviting us all to heroism, to live with the spirit of a prince.

Our Lord in today’s Gospel says to Peter: I assure you that when you were young you dressed yourself and went wherever you wanted. But when you are old, you will stretch out your arms, and another will bind you and take you where you do not want to go.

Our beloved St. John Paul II –we all know it– never wanted to safeguard his own life, to keep it for himself; rather he gave himself without reserve, to the last moment, for Christ and for us.

Thus we saw him, during the first years of his pontificate, go to the ends of the earth, as an “expression of his constant pastoral solicitude for all the Churches”41. But “during the last years, when the Lord gradually stripped him of everything, in order to assimilate him fully to himself; and when he could no longer travel, and then not even walk, and in the end not even speak, his gesture, his proclamation was reduced to the essential: to the gift of himself to the very end. His death was the culmination of a consistent witness of faith”42. And he knew how to walk heroically “the royal way of the cross,” discovering that in suffering, too, Christ was to be found, who once more repeated to him: Follow me! And so, raising high the banner of the Cross, he gave the Church perhaps “its most fruitful stage, the one with the greatest spiritual resources and greatest evangelizing efficacy, and even more, the one with the greatest apostolic projection onto the modern or postmodern world”43.

[Peroratio] My dear brothers and sisters in the Incarnate Word: The example and magisterium of St. John Paul the Great rise before us and before those who will come after us –we who are honored to have him as Spiritual Father of our Religious Family– as a lighthouse from which shines the light that scatters the darkness, as a staff for our steps and as an invitation to heroism that compels us to follow him. Today from heaven St. John Paul II says to us all once again with great fervor -to those present here and to those in the most distant missions-: “Do not be afraid! Open, and open even wider, throw wide open the doors to Christ!”44.

St. John Paul II was undoubtedly “a hero for all ages”45, and from Paradise he continues “pushing entire generations to the heroism of following Jesus Christ”46, and us too, and those who will come; that is why the words he spoke at what was to be his last World Youth Day seem especially addressed to us: “I still identify with your expectations and your hopes. […] Although I have lived through much darkness, under harsh totalitarian regimes, I have seen enough to be unshakably convinced that no difficulty, no fear is so great that it can completely stifle the hope that springs eternal in the hearts of the young. […] In the difficult moments of the Church’s history the duty of holiness becomes even more urgent. Holiness is not a matter of age. Holiness is living in the Holy Spirit”47.

We ask this “Apostle formed by Mary”48, who now enjoys “a dignity that is in a certain way infinite”49 and “is our intercessor and protector”50, to obtain for us through his intercession from the Incarnate Word the grace that, like him, we may know how “to direct the soul to great acts in every virtue; […] so that, possessing ourselves, we may know how to give ourselves, never ceasing to aspire to a holier and more perfect life, never stopping”51, “ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God”52. And may he obtain for us from Most Holy Mary the grace to be always “ready to do great, heroic, even epic things for Christ and for his Church”53. These graces we ask of him in this Holy Mass.

«Vocation in the little ones»

Directory of Minor Seminaries, 4

The Lenten atmosphere of the present month is brightened by celebrations that turn our attention to the youngest members of our Institute: the minor seminarians. Thus, for example, in a few days, on February 3, we will celebrate the beginning of the minor seminary experience in the Institute with the entrance in 1986 of the first two minor seminarians, today Fr. Ricardo Clarey and Fr. Marcelo Cano. Likewise, on the 10th and 20th of this month we will celebrate St. José Sánchez del Río and St. Francisco Marto, patrons of our minor seminaries in the United States and Ecuador respectively.

Vocational ministry in the Institute is intrinsic to the end of the evangelization of culture and to our proper charism1. And truly, we must give thanks to God that an abundance of vocations has characterized the Congregation from its first years of life. At present, the Institute has 456 vocations in formation, of whom 99 are minor seminarian vocations!, present in the various minor seminaries of the Institute around the world2.

Now then, “we must be aware that all vocations are a free gift of God to our Institute, and we think that the reasons for the divine good pleasure are:

– The urgent need of the Church.

– Fidelity to the founding charism.

– The witness and joy of priests and seminarians.

– The preaching of the Exercises”3.

It cannot be denied that, at the ecclesial level, the question of vocations is very topical and urgent. There is great concern. And this affects us directly, because if the problem of vocations is not solved the new evangelization will be very difficult, the present missionaries will be ever more overburdened, not all mission posts can be filled as they should be, and when they are gone, who will there be to replace them?

Our Proper Law gathers numerous expressions of St. John Paul II that highlight the fact that “the problem of vocations affects the very life of the Church”4. Thus, for example, it says: “‘It must be said that the problem of priestly vocations is ‘the fundamental problem of the Church’5, the one ‘that requires the greatest attention’6; it is a ‘central problem’7, ‘of the future’8, ‘vital’9”10. And further on it says: “The question of vocations “affects the Church in one of her fundamental marks, which is her apostolicity”11.

That is why in this letter it seemed fitting to us to highlight the relevance that vocational apostolate with children and adolescents has for the pastoral work of the Church and in particular for the evangelization of culture, since it is a seedbed of priestly vocations. And we wish to do so, first, by sharing, even if briefly, what the beginnings of the Institute’s first minor seminary, the “St. John the Apostle” Seminary, were like. Then we will present the Thomistic doctrine on the vocation of children to religious life, and, finally, highlight the characteristic ‘stamp’ of our minor seminaries.

1. The beginnings

As we said at the beginning, the present Fr. Ricardo Clarey and Fr. Marcelo Cano were the first ‘minor’ vocations in the Institute. Let us recall that the Institute was founded in San Rafael on March 25, 1984, and its members lived that first year in the diocesan seminary on Tirasso Street (San Rafael, Mendoza). That seminary was begun by the Institute and was in the charge of the Institute, at the express request of Bishop León Kruk. It was the first work entrusted to us. On February 22, 1985, the feast of the Chair of St. Peter, the first Mass was celebrated in the new house that would be the seat of the Institute in San Rafael, the Finca “Our Lady of Luján” on El Chañaral Street. During those first two years there was still no minor seminary in the Institute of the Incarnate Word.

Both vocations had already been together in a diocesan minor seminary, which they had left. “On the occasion of the July 1985 winter holidays” −relates Fr. Clarey− “I visited Fr. Buela at his house at Remedios de Escalada and Nazca, in Buenos Aires. On that occasion, he was with Marcelo Morsella, they too being on holiday. I told him of my desire to go to San Rafael […] Father told me that it seemed good to him that I go to San Rafael, but that he had to speak about it with Bishop Kruk and would let me know shortly”12. Meanwhile, “in those months, I don’t remember exactly when,” the account continues, “Marcelo Cano traveled to San Rafael to get to know the Finca, and expressed the desire to be able to go to San Rafael as a minor seminarian”13. The fact is that, once the approval of the local bishop arrived, he too traveled to San Rafael.

The simplicity of the account of that journey to ‘enter’ the minor seminary that was beginning with them lets us read between the lines the trust and simplicity of those who, as St. Thomas says, give themselves to the service of God from early youth14: “I traveled to San Rafael from Buenos Aires on the ‘Moby Dick’ (this was the nickname of the Mother House’s bus) together with some seminarians (Guillermo Costantini, Gaspar Farré, etc.) and after passing through San Luis we arrived in San Rafael and then at El Nihuil, on February 3, 1986. I was 16 years old. We met up again with Marcelo Cano, and some others joined (among them the present Fr. [name omitted] Domenech and two others). In those first days at El Nihuil we saw Marcelo Morsella several times”15. Once there, two seminarians were charged with looking after them, as prefects, during the month of February. And thus, simply and plainly, the experience of the minor seminary in the Institute began 35 years ago now, by the grace of God.

In March of that year, and now back at the Finca, the minor seminarians already numbered 10, since young people from San Rafael, Buenos Aires and Entre Ríos joined the experience. All of them occupied half of the St. Paul house (the major seminarians were in the other half). Naturally all these young adolescents had to finish their secondary studies. And so, with much sacrifice, four of them began to attend the Marist School, four the Polyvalent School of Arts and two the Normal School of San Rafael. For at that time the Institute did not have a school of its own. Fr. Clarey relates: “Every morning we all had to take the bus into the city, for school. We came back at noon and Brother Payo would be waiting for us with lunch (often a combination of rice with fried eggs…). In the afternoon we had Mass in the chapel, usually celebrated by the young priest José Corbelle. Afterwards we had adoration of the Blessed Sacrament with the major seminarians. On Saturdays we helped the major seminarians in the apostolate”.

After some time of the minor and major seminarians sharing the house, the latter were redistributed among the other houses of the Finca (St. Andrew, St. Peter, St. James) and the minor seminarians kept the St. Paul house. Those who were seminarians at that time remember with longing the familiarity, the joy and the undoubtedly formative experience of the outings they made to Cerro Sosneado or to El Nihuil with their superior, the founder of the Institute.

At the end of that first year the minor seminarians Cano and Clarey had finished minor seminary and were moving on to first year of philosophy, and so they were granted to take part in the popular mission together with the major seminarians in Matará (Santiago del Estero, Argentina) “where the people still had Marcelo Morsella, who had been on mission there the previous year, very much in mind”16, Fr. Clarey recounts.

The following year, 1987, the minor seminarians moved to the parish of St. Maximilian Kolbe, and then in later months of that year to the present house of formation “St. John the Apostle” on Rawson Street in San Rafael, after the property had been purchased thanks to the generosity of Juan Demianczuk and Juan Mazzeo. All this marked the beginning and the formal establishment of the minor seminary, which took place on December 27, 1987, the memorial of St. John the Apostle.

These beginnings of the Institute’s minor seminary allow us to glimpse, in broad outline, certain features that characterize the direction and formation given in our minor seminaries today. To point out just a few:

  • The minor seminarians’ participation in daily Mass : “As in all the houses of the Institute, Holy Mass is the center of life; it is the sun that illumines the interior life, the apostolate, work and every activity”17.
  • Likewise, daily Eucharistic adoration: “It is to be done every day for the space of half an hour”18 in the minor seminaries.
  • And although it is not explicit in the testimony quoted, we know that from the beginning a great devotion to Our Lady was instilled in them; that is why our Proper Law says: “The minor seminary must be a school of deep filial love for the Most Holy Virgin Mary”19.
  • In the Institute’s minor seminary we strive to ensure that the seminarians −normally of an age to be pursuing their secondary studies”20− receive a careful intellectual and spiritual formation, which they could hardly attain in another environment21. Clearly in the beginning we did not have a school of our own; nevertheless, an effort was made to send them to study at the best schools in the area. For what was sought, and “what is sought, is sufficient instruction to make the pursuit of higher studies possible. That is why it is laid down that ‘the students will pursue their studies according to the official level of education, so that they may have the possibility of going on to higher studies or universities’”22. Our Proper Law further indicates that, insofar as possible, the vocations of the minor seminary be given a humanistic formation23, but it also provides for the fact that “if there is no center of humanistic formation or other school of our own available, ‘the students will be sent to other centers, ensuring that these correspond to the spirit of the documents issued by the Congregation for Catholic Education’24”25.
  • “Outings to the mountains, trips to places of interest or recreation, etc., are very formative experiences because they allow other sports to be practiced (such as mountaineering, fishing, etc.), other kinds of games can be played, and there is the opportunity to practice virtues such as generosity, helpfulness, good spirit, etc.”26 And among many other advantages we could mention, we must say that these outings all together with their Rector and the prefects strengthen the community life and the spiritual life of the boy27, and it is something that should be lacking in no minor seminary of the Institute.
  • The missionary stamp of our Institute is essential. That is why, from the very beginnings of the minor seminary, the aim was “to form the young people in the apostolic spirit by carrying out pastoral activities suited to their age and by taking part in missions, accompanied by the priest formators and the local pastors or chaplains”28. That first year-end mission in Santiago del Estero was the first of many carried out in accordance with what the legislation of our Proper Law establishes when it says: “They will also have the year-end mission, where they will especially help the major seminarians with the children’s apostolate”29.
  • The sacrifices of the early days of the minor seminary must not be foreign to the seminarians of today, who, according to circumstances, will also have to know how to offer them, since these are part of the Christian life. That is why our Proper Law says: “Life in community, with all its demands and rewards, is the habitual and ordinary means for the maturing of young people”30. How necessary it is to teach the vocations of these times to “offer as a mortification bearing patiently and without complaint certain annoyances, whether in living with other companions, or a little heat, or some cold”31.
  • Of no lesser importance is the freedom with which the candidates discern and decide on their vocation. “Man can turn to the good only in freedom”32, says the Magisterium. “Speaking positively, we must say that the minor seminary is, fundamentally, a place where there is an orientation. There is a priestly orientation, which, far from pressuring the seminarian, progressively shows him the beauty and greatness of consecrated life”33. Therefore, “the young man, far from being ‘imprisoned’ in the religious vocation, acquires a freedom he would not have in the world. He can see things more objectively and discern his vocation in the light of supernatural motives. The minor seminary makes it possible that ‘on completing his studies…, the candidate, having a clear awareness of the divine call, will have reached such spiritual and human maturity as to allow him to make the decision to respond to that call with sufficient responsibility and freedom’34”35.
  • Likewise, all who were minor seminarians at that time highlight the family spirit in which they lived and which permeated all activities, for everything unfolded in a climate of great trust, without the life of discipline being diminished by it. They felt responsible for the running of the seminary and lived in constant joy. All this family spirit is expressed explicitly in anumerous paragraphs of the Directory of Minor Seminaries and, to tell the truth, it permeates the spirit of all its legislation. To illustrate, we quote here no. 10 of that Directory: “It will be proper to the minor seminary to foster a warm family atmosphere. Another characteristic of this house must be youthful joy. And all this in a relationship of great trust with the Superiors. All these elements are key for right vocational discernment: ‘(discernment) requires that there truly exist in the minor seminary a familial trust with the Superiors and a fraternal friendship among the students, so that, all forming one family, they may more easily develop their own nature fittingly and adequately, in accordance with the dispositions of divine Providence’36”.
  • The help of the prefects, who “are seminarians, preferably from the Theology courses”37 and help the Rector maintain discipline, organize certain particular activities, etc., behaving as an older brother to the minor seminarians.

Many more elements could be pointed out that have been present since the beginnings of the minor seminary experience in the Institute and that leave a stamp which clearly distinguishes it, because the way of life and the manner of carrying out this apostolate is inspired by our proper charism. Certainly, with the years and the different circumstances many things have been improved, but its distinctive marks endure and have become ever more firmly rooted. Thus, what began in such a humble and small way 35 years ago has now spread to 8 more countries and has multiplied in vocations and in blessings.

Concretely, the distinctive marks of a minor seminary of the Institute are38:

– Spirituality centered on the mystery of the Incarnate Word.

– The specific end of the Institute, to inculturate the Gospel, which especially marks the style of the seminarians’ spiritual, intellectual and cultural formation.

– The missionary zeal that we try to foster in concrete apostolic labors, in the missionary intention placed in study, and in prayer and the offering of works for the missions.

– The importance given to community life and to fraternal charity.

Our minor seminaries must be houses of high Christian formation, in virtue. They are not a mere Catholic “school” or “boarding school”: Christ must be explicitly proposed as the Model. They must be given an intellectual, spiritual and cultural formation according to the charism of the Institute, with all that this implies, especially so that they establish an intimate relationship with Christ, with Our Lady, with the saints, and are formed in an ecclesial spirit. Our minor seminarians must learn from their earliest years to be apostles, to take an interest in the missions and the missionaries, giving them too −always as far as possible and according to their age− the opportunity to experience zeal for souls and to do concrete things to help them. In a special way the minor seminarian must learn to do apostolate with his own fellow seminarians, living fraternal charity with his companions: helping in service, consoling the sad, trying to lavish joy and peace on everyone, etc. There the priestly temper begins to take shape, for to be a priest is nothing other than to lay down one’s life for the sheep39, for others.

2. The Thomistic doctrine on the vocation of children to religious life

Progressively, and by the grace of God, the number of minor seminarians has been increasing in recent years. To illustrate, we cite here the figures since the last General Chapter:

The grace of having minor seminaries in the Institute has let us experience at very close range the wonderful and mysterious reality of children’s vocations, in the face of which criticisms and accusations of inauthenticity have not been lacking.

That is why we believe it may be useful to set out, at least briefly, the teaching of St. Thomas Aquinas on the matter, which, moreover, is the traditional doctrine of the Church. Not without first affirming that our position –in full harmony with both– holds that: “Aware that God’s call is gratuitous and that it depends on His most free Will, [the Institute] knows that it can be awakened at any time of life. There are adult, late vocations, but also early ones. ‘As long experience shows, the priestly vocation frequently has a first moment of manifestation in the years of pre-adolescence or in the very first years of youth… even in those who decide to enter the seminary later on, it is not rare to note the presence of God’s call in much earlier periods’40. ‘This vocation manifests itself in various periods of a person’s life and with various motivations; it manifests itself in youth, in mature age and in children, among whom it is not rare for it to appear as a kind of «seed», joined to a particular piety, an ardent love of God and neighbor and an inclination to the apostolate’41. Pope St. John Paul II affirms that Christ’s follow me ‘makes itself heard most of the time already in the period of youth, and sometimes it is perceived even in childhood’42. […]

It is also the doctrine of the saints. Thus, for example, St. John Bosco, addressing the Salesians, said: ‘But what I most recommend to you are the young boys of good disposition, lovers of the practices of piety, who give some hope of being called to the ecclesiastical state. Yes, take a lively interest in these hopes of the Church; do what is possible, and I would say the impossible, to cultivate in these tender hearts and make the precious seed of vocation germinate; direct them to some place where they can pursue their studies, and if they are poor, help them also with the means that divine Providence has placed in your hands, and that your piety and love of souls will be able to suggest to you. Fortunate are you if you manage to provide some priest to the Church in these times when sacred ministers are so scarce…’43”44.

Hence the great importance that must be given to vocational ministry with children, adolescents and young people. An importance that must be translated into pastoral zeal, into dedication, into care for and accompaniment of these vocations, into prayer for the increase of vocations to the minor seminary and into concrete works to cultivate −as Don Bosco says− the seed of vocation in children.

St. Thomas Aquinas in his work Contra Retrahentes defends every call to religious life, every vocation, even that of children, showing that vocation is something pious and noble and that, in the end, to follow one’s vocation is to try to imitate Jesus Christ. And he also makes clear that those who unceasingly oppose this call45 are chiefly the devil and carnal men:

“The devil, the enemy of human salvation, has since ancient times sought, by means of carnal men, enemies of the Cross of Christ, attached to earthly things, to hinder aspirations as pious as they are salutary”46.

The fact is that even today not a few people, even religious, discourage children and young people who aspire to religious life. They argue that they are not accustomed to the observance of the commandments, and some even think it is something irrational. Now, it is a fact, never denied, that the Church has permitted and does permit parents to offer their children at an early age47, to be educated in the observance of the counsels before they can exercise themselves in the practice of the commandments. If it were irrational the Church could not approve it; yet she not only permits it in fact, but even counsels it. The Aquinate uses as an authority a text of St. Gregory. Beyond the particular case raised, what is important and what the Angelic Doctor wishes to highlight is the lawfulness of letting children enter into regular observance. If the Church permits it and, even more, counsels it, it is something good. St. Thomas further holds that entering religious life is not only not evil in itself, but does not even have the appearance of evil48.

Moreover, basing himself on Sacred Scripture, St. Thomas affirms that children must not be prevented from coming to Christ. On the contrary, there must be a concern to draw them to Christ. Children must be won for Christ. Following a text attributed to Chrysostom49, with great common sense the Aquinate teaches that if children, the most innocent, are prevented from coming to Jesus, who then will be able to approach Jesus? On the other hand, since man approaches the Lord by the practice of the counsels, what reason is there to prevent children from approaching Christ through the observance of the counsels?

The Angelic Doctor then goes on to highlight the importance of this apostolate with children. On the one hand, it is a good thing to bring them to the Lord through the practice of the counsels, not preventing their entrance50. But furthermore, we ourselves must become like children and bring them to Christ: “The Lord, on the contrary, exhorting His disciples, men already mature, to be condescending for the benefit of children, tells them, so that they might become like children toward the children, in order to win the children: to such as these belongs the kingdom of heaven… this we must keep very much in mind, lest, presuming to possess a superior wisdom, the little ones of the Church be despised by those who boast of being great, preventing children from coming to Jesus”51.

It is interesting to note that in the doctrine of St. Thomas we find no room for doubt regarding the authenticity of children’s vocations. In children there are true vocations to religious life, as the testimony of so many religious throughout history shows. Many of them are saints of the Church: St. John XXIII, St. John Bosco, and a long etcetera.

The religious vocation, in fact, is nothing other than a purpose of giving oneself entirely to the Lord. A purpose that can only arise in the heart through an impulse of the Holy Spirit, even when it may appear as the fruit of human suggestions or exhortations. This has always been a clear and firm affirmation of St. Thomas. That is why it is fitting to reaffirm ourselves in the Thomistic doctrine that supports the apostolate of children’s vocations, since it frees us from false fears, which often cause incipient vocations to miscarry.

St. Thomas makes clear that neither does it count against children’s vocations that some, having entered when young, later abandoned religious life. Vocation, in fact, as has been said, is not a predestination, but a grace that can be lost, and is in fact lost especially, in the first place, when children who feel called are not allowed to enter, leaving them in the world surrounded by dangers that end up stifling the seed of the vocation and even of the Christian life. Vocations can also be lost if the minor seminaries do not create an environment favorable to the development and strengthening of that vocation. Indeed, there are not a few parents who, having resisted the vocation of their young children, later lament the lack of Christian life to which they come from having been left in the world.

If the holy purpose of wanting to be a religious arises in a child, we are in the presence of a true and proper vocation. If there is a “crooked” purpose, one that is not upright (as is the case of those who want to become religious for other interests, such as economic, academic ones, etc.), this cannot be called a true vocation. In such a case the Superiors will not be slow to distinguish one case from the other, and if they are zealous in their apostolate or office, they will send the uncalled back to their homes.

We must take as addressed personally to us the exhortation of our Proper Law to “know how to call, teach, direct, accompany and select vocations”52, which includes the vocations of children and adolescents. Is it not proper to us to work on the turning points of culture, among which seminary formation is listed53? Let us remember that also in the case of vocations to the minor seminary “the work must be active, constant, full of drive and vitality, committed and urged on by the charity of Christ54, and necessarily opposed to a mentality of ‘ordinary administration or bureaucratic slowness’55, which waits negligently for vocations to knock at the door. God sows the seeds of vocation by grace with full hands56”57.

3. Love creates familiarity

Finally, I would like to highlight one more aspect of our minor seminarians. We have just said that vocations can be lost if the minor seminaries do not create an environment favorable to the development and strengthening of that vocation. What exactly is this environment? Our Proper Law says it explicitly: “It will be proper to the minor seminary to foster a warm family atmosphere”58. This is a very important characteristic of our minor seminaries, which are distinguished from others precisely by “the importance given to community life and fraternal charity”59.

What does this family atmosphere consist of? This family atmosphere or spirit is nothing other than the application of the preventive system in the community of the minor seminary (and what is said here of them applies also to our schools, major seminaries, religious communities, etc.). In this family atmosphere love is the driving force of everything that is done or left undone, said or left unsaid, undertaken or set aside. Love makes the educator lovable and, for that very reason, the young person’s heart surrenders to him. In this sense it is important that the children be persuaded that the good of their souls is being sought60.

“This love leads to watchfulness: not as one who imposes and mercilessly demands discipline, but as a father who loves and therefore watches over his children so that they do not fall into dangers. So that the children always have upon them the paternal eye of the Superiors or of the prefects and assistants”61. It is necessary “to put the children in the impossibility of doing wrong”62. “That is why those who want to work in the minor seminary and want to fulfill their mission well must be ready for a life of true sacrifice”63.

This love, moreover, shows itself in gentleness: “Everything with gentleness, nothing by force; harshness spoils everything, exasperates hearts, breeds hatred”64. And this brings with it an element that we consider fundamental for the family spirit that must be lived in our minor seminaries as well as in every religious house of the Institute: familiarity. St. John Bosco used to say: “Familiarity with the young, especially during recreation. Without familiarity affection is not shown, and without this showing there can be no trust”65. “For this it is necessary, then, to be with the children: ‘Spend as much time as possible with the children. This is the great secret that will make you master of their hearts’66”67.

With a fatherly tone our Proper Law points out the profound educational value that the superiors’ practice of charity and virtue has for the minor seminarians. To them it counsels: “It must never happen that a pupil surpasses you in virtue… And what will be the key of example for us? It is the rules of the Congregation, and especially obedience… The educator must be the personification of the rule”68.

The fruit of this charity and of the atmosphere of familiarity with which life is lived in the minor seminary is youthful joy, for which the minor seminaries of the Institute must stand out.

Far be it from us to have minor seminarians who are “cold young men, ‘well-mannered’, ‘disciplined’ [outwardly], lifeless, without passions, without love, incapable of giving their lives for something, useless for creating”69. That is why our Proper Law insistently repeats that the “minor seminary must unfold in an affectionate and joyful family climate: the seminarian must learn to love his companions as true brothers and his Superiors as true fathers”70.

This family climate, rightly understood, does not dispense with the life of discipline; on the contrary, discipline is like the rail on which the life of the seminary runs. Don Bosco affirmed it, and the Directory of Minor Seminaries repeats it: “in the house discipline is everything”71; it “educates the will, strengthens it and makes it at the same time docile and ready for the good”72. “Discipline is the foundation of morality and of study”73. But we must bear in mind that discipline must not be an end in itself, but only a means to achieve order to the extent necessary74.

Let us bear in mind that what is aimed at in the minor seminary is “formation in virtue, because it is the best way to protect and discern the seeds of vocation”75. This formation in our seminaries is an integral formation, which includes spiritual and liturgical formation, formation of the intellect and of the will, and an aesthetic and artistic formation. Our Proper Law is abundant in describing the various means available to the educator to achieve this formation, some of which we have already mentioned, such as daily Mass, Eucharistic adoration, outings, humanistic formation, etc. But it also mentions other ‘minor’ means, so to speak, which also contribute to achieving this formation and which give the education imparted in our seminaries a particular stamp: the ‘Good Nights’, the monthly retreats or annual Spiritual Exercises (according to age)76, tender and constant devotion to Our Lady, the conviviums, the monthly outings, the disputatio, plays, the practice of musical instruments, sports, etc. All these activities not only enrich the seminarian’s life humanly, but help him to forge that princely spirit that must shine in all of them.

It is true that each of our minor seminaries has its particularities and consequently its challenges; nevertheless, it is to be hoped that every possible means will be employed to give the vocations God sends us the best, the most careful, the most solid and Christian formation possible.

*****

Together with these lines go our most heartfelt affection and gratitude to all the priests and the team of formators in the minor seminaries of the Institute for their commendable effort and dedication in cultivating “the vocation of the little ones”77. For we know well that the words of St. John of Avila, which the Constitutions quote when speaking of spiritual fatherhood, fit them very well: “Who will count the silence needed with children, who complain about every little thing; the watching lest envy arise from seeing another more loved than they, or seeming to be? The care of feeding them, even if the father must take the morsel from his own mouth, and even leave being among the angelic choirs to come down and give the child his little sops? One must always be even-tempered, lest the child meet with some answer less than loving. And sometimes the father’s heart is tormented with a thousand cares, and it would be a great relief to him to let loose the reins of his sadness and weep his fill, and if the little son comes, he must play with him and laugh, as if he had nothing else to do. And the temptations, drynesses, dangers, deceptions, scruples, with a thousand other misfortunes they fall into, who will count them? What vigilance to prevent them from coming upon them! What wisdom to know how to get them out once they have fallen in! What patience not to grow weary of hearing them ask once, twice and a thousand times what has already been answered, and of telling them again what they have already been told! What continual and courageous prayer is needed before God, begging for them that they may not die!”78.

That is why in this month, in which we commemorate the 35th anniversary of the beginning of the minor seminary experience in the Institute, we give thanks to God and to Most Holy Mary for all the vocations with which they have blessed us throughout all these years, for those who began and for those who followed them. Let us raise our prayers for the perseverance of all these vocations and that, if it pleases our Lord, we may multiply many other minor seminaries where priestly vocations may germinate, for the glory of God and the service of the Church.

To Most Holy Mary, through whose tender hands all graces come to us: our most humble gratitude.

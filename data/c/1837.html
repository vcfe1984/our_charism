For the Kingdom of Heaven1
Constitutions, 257

Every year we members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word make the Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius of Loyola, in which the Spanish saint “invites us to the great conquest of holiness”2. During the ‘second week’ of these Exercises we are given the opportunity to make the contemplation of the “call of the King”3, since the Christian life is not the mere adherence to a doctrine, but the following and accompanying of a Person.

There we hear, like a refrain, the question “What must I do for Christ?”4, which denotes the singularly ‘practical’ tone of the call and of the consequent following of Christ. For, as St. Ignatius rightly points out, “love ought to be put more in deeds than in words”5. Hence the importance of constantly renewing the dispositions of magnanimity and generosity in our adherence to the will of the King. This certainly includes returning often to the first fruits and freshness of that call which Christ one day made personally to each one of us, and once again resolutely pronouncing our yes to the Beloved. At the same time, it includes ‘making more room’ in our life, in our souls, in our heart, so that God may reign in us.

1. Jesus Christ is King

The Directory of Spirituality says: “The Lord is King of all humanity in the strict, literal and proper sense, since He received from the Father authority, power and the kingdom6; that is why He is King of kings and Lord of lords7. As you know, the foundation of this kingship is fourfold:

  • In the first place, it belongs to Him because He is God, the Incarnate Word, and thus St. Cyril of Alexandria says: “Christ holds dominion over all creatures, not seized by force nor taken for any other reason, but by His very essence and nature”8;
  • in the second place, it belongs to Him by virtue of the Redemption, by right of conquest, since He bought us with His blood: You were not ransomed… with perishable gold or silver, but with the precious blood of Christ9;
  • in the third place, because He is Head of the Church, by the fullness of grace: full of grace and truth10;
  • in the fourth place, by right of inheritance: whom He appointed heir of all things11.

His royal authority includes the fullness of the threefold power: legislative, judicial and executive”12.

Moreover, “the scope of His kingship is twofold, personal and social. He reigns over minds because He is the Truth, ‘and men must receive the truth from Him with obedience’13; He reigns over wills because He is Goodness, ‘in such a way that He inflames us toward the noblest things’14; and He reigns over hearts because He is Love. And He also reigns socially, since ‘there is no difference between individuals and civil society, because individuals, united in society, are no less under the power of Christ than each one of them is separately. He is the source of private and public well-being’15”16.

“Certainly His is not a temporal or earthly kingdom: my Kingdom is not of this world17; rather it is an eternal Kingdom: His Kingdom will have no end18, and it is universal: all power in heaven and on earth was given to Him19; a Kingdom of truth and life, of holiness and grace, of justice, love and peace20”21.

2. An effective kingship

Now, having freely heard the Lord’s call to follow Him wherever He goes: “whoever wishes to come with me”22, we have accepted to be with Him, because we want to be steeped in His presence and to become, little by little, “other Christs”23. That is, we religious have embraced “the form of life which Jesus, the supreme consecrated one and missionary of the Father for His Kingdom, embraced and proposed to the disciples who followed Him”24.

This implies −as we were saying earlier− that Christ must reign in our lives, and that is nothing other than “to prefer nothing to Christ”25 . For “it is clear −teaches St. John of the Cross− that the more one fixed one’s eyes on the king’s servants and paid attention to them, the less notice one took of the king and the less one esteemed him; […] for the more one sets [one’s eyes] on the servants, the more one takes away from one’s lord; and then such a one did not judge very highly of the king, since the servants seem to him to be something in the presence of the king, his lord. So it happens to the soul with regard to its God when it pays heed to the said creatures”26.

In this sense, “the religious state […] reveals in a special way the superiority of the Kingdom over all created things, and its radical demands. It also shows all men the extraordinary greatness of the power of Christ the King and the infinite efficacy of the Holy Spirit, who works wonders in His Church”27.

Many of you will know the story of St. Rafael Arnáiz Barón (1911-1938), a Trappist monk, whose monastic life was interrupted three times because of his illness (diabetes). Rafael had just asked the abbot to be allowed to return to the monastery for the third time. And this would be the last. Then one day in November 1937 he wrote a letter to Brother Tescelino −who had been his infirmarian at the Trappist monastery− telling him that Father Abbot had told him he could return to the monastery whenever he wished… but that he should think it over carefully and not be hasty, because they now had no infirmarian (since Brother Tescelino had been called up for military service). Then Rafael reflects: “Humanly speaking, it is very prudent, don’t you think? But what am I to do? Well, look, this is how I think about it; let’s see what you think.

Suppose you are at home sick, surrounded by care and attention, almost crippled, useless…, unable to fend for yourself, in a word. But one day you saw Jesus pass beneath your window. […] If you saw that Jesus was calling you and giving you a place in His retinue, and He looked at you with those divine eyes radiating love, tenderness, forgiveness, and said to you: Why do you not follow me? … What would you do? Would you perhaps answer Him… Lord, I would follow You if You gave me an infirmarian…, if You gave me the means to follow You in comfort and without danger to my health… I would follow You if I were healthy and strong enough to fend for myself…?

No, […] surely you would have said to Him: I am coming, Lord; my ailments do not matter to me, nor death, nor eating, nor sleeping … If You will have me, I am coming. If You wish, You can heal me… It does not matter to me that the road by which You lead me is difficult, steep and full of thorns. It does not matter to me if You want me to die with You on a cross […]. I am given prudent warnings… But what is all that worth beside the gaze of a God like Jesus of Galilee, who offers you a place in heaven, and an eternal love? Nothing, brother… not even suffering until the end of the world is worth giving up following Jesus”28.

Likewise, each one of us −certainly assisted by the grace of God− has answered our Lord’s call, leaving everything behind to follow Him. This is the radicalness that is required if we are to love the Incarnate Word with an undivided love. For ours has been, and must continue to be, “the choice of a greater and undivided love for Christ and His Church”29. That is what enables us “to offer a ‘privileged witness of a constant search for God and an absolute dedication to the growth of His Kingdom”30. This is something we should keep very much in mind, above all when poverty is felt more harshly, when health does not keep pace with apostolic zeal, when we are criticized in spite of our dedication, when loneliness becomes heavier, when divine help seems to have run out for us, when the temptations to make a pact with the world grow gigantic.

In other words, our consecration implies –in fact– a life of lordship.

A religious of the Incarnate Word must exercise this life of lordship:

  • Over himself: to the extent that he triumphs over sin, masters the incentives of the flesh, and governs his soul and body. The religious, to the extent that he fully submits his soul to God, arrives at a state of indifference and detachment from the things of the world, which gives him a masterful and free will, capable of dedicating himself to things without letting himself be dominated by them31.
  • Over men: to the extent that the religious gives himself generously to the service of Jesus Christ, the only King who deserves to be served, he acquires an effective, though spiritual, kingship over men, even over those who have power and authority, and even over those who abuse it. For they take upon themselves the burden of their sins and their sorrows, through a humble and serviceable love that goes as far as the sacrifice of oneself32.
  • Over the world: this happens in two ways, namely: 1) by collaborating with the world of creation through work and with the world of redemption through the apostolate. For this kingship to be effective, it will be necessary that, together with dedication to things, there be at the same time a detachment and disengagement from them; 2) by rejecting the world, whether out of loyalty to the world itself, which must be held as a means and not as an end, or out of loyalty to God, resisting the concupiscences, temptations and sins of the world; being independent in the face of the maxims, mockery and persecutions of the world, depending only on our upright conscience enlightened by faith; ready for martyrdom out of loyalty to God, which constitutes the full and total rejection of the evil world33.
  • Over the devil: our Proper Law says: “We need religious convinced not only that by the grace of God they have power to resist the devil, but also to be able to exorcise him”34.

We must be convinced that we have been “consecrated for the Kingdom” and, therefore, we are true religious insofar as we are totally consecrated to the service of God, offering ourselves to Him as a holocaust35. This is achieved through the faithful and constant practice of the religious vows. Indeed, the Magisterium of the Church teaches that “the vocation of consecrated persons to seek first the Kingdom of God is, first and foremost, a call to complete conversion, in self-renunciation, in order to live fully for the Lord”36. Therefore, it is our oblation through obedience, poverty and chastity that makes our existence “a living memorial of Jesus’ way of living and acting as the Word made flesh37, in relation to the Father and in relation to men”38, and an effective contribution to the reign of Christ.

3. I make my oblation39

Christ’s invitation “whoever wishes to come with me” brings with it “to labor with me,” and St. Ignatius puts the reason for this on the lips of Christ: “so that, following me in suffering, he may also follow me in glory”40.

And although we have already said it many times and it is known to all, we believe it is worth mentioning again that the following of Christ “is not the promise of easy successes. It promises no one a comfortable life. It is demanding. And at the same time it is a Great Promise: the promise of eternal life for man, subject to the law of death; the promise of victory, through faith […]. To find life, one must lose life; to be born, one must die; to be saved, one must take up the Cross”41.

Our consecration is synonymous with holocaust, with oblation. Said in a more Ignatian tone, this would be equivalent to saying that our consecration to the kingdom of Christ “entails the acceptance of spiritual combat”42.

That is why it seems possible to us to draw a parallel between St. Ignatius’ prayer of oblation of the kingdom and our formula of religious profession, which it is always worthwhile to explore more deeply. It is remarkable how the two correspond in a way that fills us with wonder; let us see:

Oblation of the kingdom43

Formula of consecration in the IVE44

Eternal Lord of all things

For love of the Father, first origin and supreme end of consecrated life;
of Christ, who calls us to His intimacy;
of the Holy Spirit, who disposes the soul to welcome His inspirations.

Our consecration has its origin in God’s initiative, and it is toward God Himself that we tend, for “for the glory of the Most Holy Trinity, the greater manifestation of the Incarnate Word and the honor of the Church founded by Christ”45 we have embraced this state of life which “imitates more closely … that form of life which the Son of God chose when He came into the world…”46 with the sole “end of seeking God alone”47. In this way, our religious life, insofar as it promotes a transfigured existence through the practice of the evangelical counsels, becomes a manifestation of God and a confessio Trinitatis, that is, one of the concrete traces that the Trinity leaves in history48.

“Concretely, chastity is the reflection of the infinite love that unites the three divine Persons; poverty is the expression of the total gift of self that the three divine Persons make to one another; obedience is the reflection in history of the loving correspondence proper to the three divine Persons”49. Thus, then, starting from a transfigured existence, we wish to participate in the life of the Trinity and to confess the love that saves50.

Indeed, our formula of profession51 and the very Constitutions52 that govern our particular style of sanctification and apostolate begin with a Trinitarian invocation and end with one; because the beginning and the end of our consecration is none other than “God alone”53. And just as St. Ignatius begins by saying: “Eternal Lord of all things,” our Constitutions begin by saying: “We confess that God is the Lord and Father of all things, the beginning and end of them all,” and all of them we wish “to restore, entirely, in Christ”54, since that is precisely “to prolong the Incarnation in all things”55.

That is why we understand that the practice of our religious vows is a “manifestation of the giving of oneself to God with an undivided heart56 and a reflection of the infinite love that unites us to Him”57.

This requires a “holy familiarity with the Word made flesh”58 and being formed in a profound intimacy with God59, which disposes our soul to welcome His inspirations60, “so that we may belong more and more to Christ, and therefore to His Mystical Body, since if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to Christ61”62.

In this way, our “religious consecration becomes an image of the Trinity: ‘it is a proclamation of what the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit, accomplishes with His love, His goodness and His beauty… That is why its first objective is to make visible the marvels that God works in the frail humanity of those who are called’63”64.

In other words: “We embrace this state of life in order to be able more easily to give ourselves to divine things, attaining eternal beatitude with greater security and, finally, to dedicate ourselves with greater freedom to the work of leading others to the Kingdom of heaven”65, in such a way that our consecration “must be useful to our neighbor insofar as it is a living witness of supernatural realities”66.

Formula of consecration in the IVE

I make my oblation,
with Your favor and help

I, N.N., freely make to God the oblation of my whole being […]

“Consecration is the basis of religious life. By affirming this, the Church places first the initiative of God and the transforming relationship with Him which religious life involves. Consecration is a divine action. God calls a person and sets him or her apart for a particular dedication to Himself. At the same time, He offers the grace to respond, so that consecration is expressed, on the part of the human person, in a profound and free self-surrender”67, as the Magisterium of the Church teaches.

Now, this “consecration to God of the religious life is a total consecration, which ‘requires, according to St. Gregory, consecrating to God ‘one’s whole life’”68, which can only be carried out by means of the vow, since we do not possess our whole life all at once, in one moment. By means of the vow man freely renounces ‘the very freedom to abstain from what pertains to divine service’”69.

That is why our Constitutions make explicit that our oblation consists principally in the fulfillment of the three vows of chastity, poverty and obedience, impelled by charity70. And therefore, religious profession constitutes a true holocaust of oneself, since by virtue of the vows one gives to God everything one has, reserving nothing: by the vow of chastity, the good proper to the body; by the vow of poverty, external things; and the goods of the soul by the vow of obedience71.

This consecration truly embraces one’s entire life, and that is why we say “of my whole being.” For this reason we can say that “religious life consists essentially in the three vows of poverty, chastity and obedience72, by which one gives to God external goods, the right to form a family, and freedom, which is what man holds most dear. So that absolutely nothing is left to the religious.

St. Thomas teaches that ‘those are called religious par excellence who consecrate themselves totally to the service of God, offering themselves to Him as a holocaust. […] In the holocaust the victim offered to God is immolated. Immolation implies the total destruction of the victim in honor of God. The religious immolates himself to God as a holocaust, following Christ poor, virgin and obedient unto death on a cross”73. “But rather than of a destruction, we should speak of a positive transformation. The religious gives everything in order to attain the perfect charity that will make him possess God intimately and fully”74.

So, “it is a matter of giving everything, but not as an end in itself, rather in order to be free of all the impediments that could draw one away from perfect charity75, and therefore with a view to a more intimate consecration to God and a new and special dedication to the service of God and to the saving mission of the Church”76.

What is more, our formula of profession explicitly mentions that we make the oblation of our whole being “freely”:

  • Freely we have chosen to be among the eunuchs who have made themselves so for the sake of the Kingdom of Heaven (Mt 19:12)77.
  • Freely we have chosen poverty in order to proclaim “the Absolute of God, who is our only wealth”78.
  • Freely we choose to be obedient and to submit our own will to legitimate Superiors, who take the place of God when they command something according to the Constitutions79, imitating in this Jesus Christ, who became obedient unto death, even death on a cross80.

Therefore, “the evangelical counsels of chastity, poverty and obedience, lived by Christ and embraced out of love for Him, appear as a path to the full realization of the person in opposition to dehumanization; they proclaim the freedom of the children of God and the joy of living according to the evangelical beatitudes”81. Far from being a renunciation that impoverishes, they rather enrich us because they free us “from the impediments that could draw us away from the fervor of charity and the perfection of divine worship, consecrating us more intimately to the service of God”82, so as to come to possess God intimately and fully83.

Put another way, we have chosen to belong totally to the Incarnate Word, and that makes us joyfully free. And freely we have consented to belong to Him, making the oblation of our whole being84 through the practice of the evangelical counsels according to the “evangelical path traced out in the Constitutions of the Institute of the Incarnate Word”85, because we are convinced that, as the Mystical Doctor says, “the immense goods of God do not fit nor fall except into an empty and solitary heart”86.

Our formula of profession takes on full solemnity as we present ourselves “before God our Lord and all His saints” and invoke the intercession of “Our Lady, the Twelve Apostles and the other patron saints.” And this is so because the oblation we make is at once a historical and a supernatural reality, in such a way that both the profession and the very practice of the vows become a reality that transcends the earthly plane, moving us to practice the virtues of transcendence: faith, hope and charity87.

Moreover, it gives our consecration a stamp and a luster that distinguishes us from other religious, namely, the great veneration for the Mother of the Incarnate Word and for the saints, masterpieces of God, in whom we honor God Himself88.

It seems fitting to me to affirm that, since our spirituality is anchored in the mystery of the Incarnation to the point that we can say that it “derives from the Person of the Word and of His Mother”89, Marian devotion is inherent to our consecration, and without it our consecration cannot be understood, for we say that we are “essentially missionary and Marian”90. That is why we raise our prayers to the Mother of God, that she may obtain for us the grace “to attain this disposition of supreme, total and unrestricted docility to the Holy Spirit”91 “in order to claim for Jesus Christ the lordship of all that is authentically human”92. So that our consecration may manifest “a lordship over all things, together with a most free will, ready to please God alone”93. And from the Virgin Mother we hope to obtain all the help necessary to fulfill the honorable duty of “prolonging the Incarnation in all things”94. Consequently, our consecration is framed by, impregnated with, and diffusive of devotion to Mary Most Holy, its “natural consequence being to marianize one’s whole life”95. Our Lady then becomes “the model that guides ‘all our intentions, actions and operations’96”97 and is, moreover, the end toward which our acts are directed, the object that draws our heart, and the motive of the labors we undertake98.

Thus, then, if our consecration implies the oblation of our whole being to the Incarnate Word, we wish to make that offering −“in order to serve Jesus Christ better”99− through the hands of Mary Most Holy, “so that she may dispose of everything according to her good pleasure100, certain that through Mary, Mother of the Incarnate Word, we are to go to Him, and that she is to make of us ‘great saints’101”102.

Furthermore, the veneration of the saints – as “the best members of the Mystical Body of Christ and the greatest and most complete fruit of the Incarnation and the Redemption”103−, the study of their wise doctrine, the riches with which they adorned the work of the Church, their heroic virtues and examples of life, their intercession before the throne of God “impel us to seek the city that is to come… perfect union with Christ, that is, holiness”104. “They constantly commend to us heaven, eternal life, the reward of merits, God! […] spurring whole generations on to the heroism of following Jesus Christ”105. For this reason our self-giving to Christ finds in the saints inspiration, help and wise doctrine, so that we may reach the heaven they have already reached.

And this is so, simply because “love ought to be put more in deeds than in words”106.

To “pledge all one’s strength” is to “resolve in earnest”107 to imitate Christ in His suffering, of which St. John of the Cross speaks and which our Proper Law expresses in various ways: “We want to imitate Jesus Christ as perfectly as possible”108; “firmly resolved to attain holiness… with ‘a great and very determined determination not to stop until reaching it (holiness)’”109, etc. For religious consecration in this Institute implies the closer imitation of the mystery of the Incarnate Word and of the attitudes it entails110, which brings with it the demand for conversion and holiness, for a transfigured existence, for contemplating and bearing witness to the transfigured face of Christ111. The Incarnate Word offered Himself to the Father without restriction; He made Himself an oblation as a victim, immolated Himself in expiation, gave Himself without reserve, and that is precisely what the priestly attitude of every member of our small Religious Family must be112.

Therefore, all of us members of the Institute seek to perfect ourselves “by being in Christ ‘an eternal offering to God’113, ‘a living and perfect victim for the praise of His glory’114. For that is the priestly attitude proper to the ‘third class of men’ of the Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius of Loyola115. And we wish to live permanently in that attitude, without diminutions or retractions, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without retreats or sluggishness. Both in the undertakings of the inner life and in high historical undertakings”116. Thus, then, since “in institutes dedicated to the apostolate there is no path of holiness, nor of profession of the evangelical counsels, nor of life dedicated to God and His service, that is not intrinsically linked to the service of the Church and of the world117; all our missionary and apostolic work is founded on the conviction that He must reign118.

Now, in this work of the apostolate, the first place is held by the witness of life, since “one is a missionary above all by what one is… before being one by what one says or does”119, and that is for us the “first and irreplaceable form of mission”120, so that the charity of Christ may shine forth among the faithful121. In this sense, “all the members of our Institute must not only fulfill with the greatest possible perfection the evangelical counsels and the self-giving to Jesus through Mary, but also ‘order their life according to the Proper Law of the Institute and thus strive to attain the perfection of their state’122”123. Nevertheless, not only through our consecration but also through our apostolic labor we seek to associate ourselves with the work of redemption and with the spreading of the kingdom of God124, transfiguring the world from within with the power of the Beatitudes.

Confessing that Jesus Christ is true God and true man, who in His one divine person unites both natures, we are in the world without being of the world, and we assume in Christ everything human, since “what is not assumed is not redeemed”125. Consequently, our sublime endeavor is “to ‘assume’ cultures, purifying them and elevating them starting from Christ and His Gospel, understood ‘in the Church’”126, and we wish to do this “even in the most difficult situations and under the most adverse conditions”127.

The Word assumed human nature, granting it the dignity of His divine person. In the same way, this assumption of the whole reality of men and their cultures “is only real when it truly transforms what is human in Christ, elevating it, dignifying it, perfecting it. What is left only on the human level has only apparently been assumed”128. So, in all our missionary work we are guided by the principle which says “that true inculturation is from within and consists, ultimately, in a renewal of life under the influence of grace”129.

At the same time, we are convinced that “it is the task of consecrated life to spread the Kingdom of Christ to the most distant regions, to the growing multitude of those who do not know Christ. For this reason also “it urges us to work in the most difficult places (those where no one wants to go)”130.

What is more, far from regarding pastoral work as a means of escapism, which has led some to fall into activism, for us missionary labor is a cross and, as such, unites us more closely to Christ. Indeed, the interior source of our apostolate is, and we wish it always to be, our ever deeper communion with the pastoral charity of Jesus Christ131. Contrary to what some think, we are persuaded that “mission strengthens consecrated life, gives it renewed enthusiasm and new motivations, and stimulates its fidelity”132, since the act by which one loves God and the act by which one loves one’s neighbor are specifically the same133.

“For all these reasons the members of our Religious Family must collaborate with all enthusiasm in missionary work134, making the gift of themselves to God out of love for so many men and women of our time who do not know Jesus Christ, and who seem to be pleading like that Macedonian: Come over to Macedonia and help us135”136. The zeal of our members for the establishment of the Kingdom of God and the salvation of men becomes the best proof of an authentically lived self-giving137.

These words of our consecration –so profound and so rich in content– point us to the very reason for religious profession, namely: to bring the consecration of Baptism to its fullness and to represent perpetually in the Church that form of life which the Son of God chose when He became incarnate138 .

Therefore, if earlier we said that our religious profession was an image of the Trinity, now we must say that it is at the same time Christocentric, since our vocation implies “an adherence that enables us to conform our whole existence to Christ, through the practice of the evangelical counsels as the most radical way of living the Gospel and following Christ”139. And although “this applies to every religious, ‘with much greater reason it falls to us to live this reality as religious of the Family of the Incarnate Word’140. For this reason, even though it is essential to every religious, we want it not to go unnoticed in our lives. Even more, we want this Christocentric stamp to be branded with fire upon us and upon our apostolate of evangelizing the culture. Since this reality of being other Christs is central to our spirituality”141.

That is why “we want to imitate Jesus Christ as perfectly as possible, since He teaches us: I have given you an example142, and St. Paul exhorts: Have the same sentiments as Christ Jesus had…143, so that we may be the good fragrance of Christ144, ambassadors for Christ145… of the mystery of the Gospel146, a letter of Christ147, clothed with Christ148, firmly convinced that we are predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son149, reproducing Him150, becoming like Him151, being configured to Him152, knowing that we reflect the very image153 of the Only Son of God”154. In a few words: “If we are religious, it is in order to imitate the Incarnate Word, chaste, poor, obedient and son of Mary”155.

Each one of us members of the Institute knows that we are called to be united to God by the the practice of perfect charity, and by the profession of the three vows we seek to remove at the root all the obstacles that could prevent us from reaching the perfection of charity. These obstacles, as our Proper Law clearly teaches, “can be three: the love of external goods, which is destroyed by the vow of poverty; the desire for sensible pleasures, above all carnal ones, which is destroyed by the vow of chastity; and, finally, the disorder of the human will, which is remedied by the vow of obedience”156. Consequently, if the evangelical counsels essentially constitute religious life, charity is its soul and its most intimate reason for being; it is its end.

Through the practice of evangelical poverty, we members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word seek to imitate Christ poor: “poor in His birth, poorer in His life and poorest on the cross”157, until we come to win total detachment, not only from material goods but from everything that is not God Himself, which presupposes the perfection of charity and complete and consummate holiness. As St. John of the Cross says, “to love is to strip oneself, for God, of all that is not God”158.

Through the practice of consecrated chastity we offer God the holocaust of our body and of all our natural affections, which implies a preferential choice of exclusive love for God. Our longing is to live a victorious, triumphant chastity, as a longed-for and joyful sacrifice in order to obtain intimate union with God in that spiritual and virginal marriage which characterizes consecrated life, in which the heart is not stifled, but given, made sacred159.

Furthermore, and most especially, following the example of the Incarnate Word, we members of the Institute give ourselves totally to God through the vow of obedience, by which we wish to make a gift of our will160. “Man can offer nothing better to God than the submission of his own will to another for the love of God”161, teaches St. Thomas Aquinas. Therefore, in imitation of the Incarnate Word, who became obedient unto death, even death on a cross162 –by which He redeemed and sanctified men– we seek to live obedience by cooperating with Christ in the salvation of our neighbor, that is, by participating in the Redemption of Christ.

Finally, let us say that “religious life is a following of Christ, but not in just any way, ‘but with the purpose of not turning back. That is why our Lord said: No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the Kingdom of God163. And it is with that firm purpose of following Christ that we make the vows’”164. But being aware of the sublimity of such an undertaking and of the miseries and weaknesses that afflict us, we not only ask for the intercession of Our Lady and the saints, but we ask God Himself to help us to be faithful in the work He has begun165.

In the name of the Church and of the Institute, the one who receives the profession of religious vows acknowledges the religious’ explicit desire for total conformity with Christ, the Incarnate Word166. A desire that must be made effective and in which one must persevere precisely “for a more abundant holiness of the Church”167 and the spreading of the kingdom of God.

* * * * *

The path that leads to holiness, the conquest of the kingdom, entails, then, the acceptance of spiritual combat.

“The transformation of the world and the building of the kingdom of Christ, a kingdom of justice and peace, can only be accomplished by the grace and the power of God’s love in us. Only love can transform hearts… The only violence that leads to the building of the kingdom of Christ is the sacrifice and service born of love”168.

That is why the resounding idea continues to be: to sacrifice oneself. This is how history is steered, even silently and hiddenly169.

May Mary Most Holy, Queen of Heaven, obtain for us the grace “to know how to die”170 in order to live171.

SOLEMNITY OF THE INCARNATION

The end of the Incarnation according to St. John

[Exordium] The words of the Archangel St. Gabriel to the Blessed Virgin Mary that we have just heard are striking, among so many riches, because they contain various elements that speak of the kingship of Christ (it was in fact one of the prophecies about the Messiah who was to come, that he would be King). And so we read: He will be great and will be called Son of the Most High; the Lord God will give him the throne of David his father, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and of his reign there will be no end1. Reading the Gospel of St. John, we notice that these words find a particular echo in another passage of the Gospel, which also speaks to us of Christ as King and of his reign, and it is to this passage that I want to refer in today’s homily. It is a verse known as “the end of the Incarnation” according to St. John.

Scholars say that the evangelist St. John used the Greek verb γένναω (to be born) only once in his whole work and spoke only once of the “birth” of Jesus; this is found in Jn 18:37, our verse.

Both texts (that of the Annunciation and this one of St. John) highlight, on the one hand, the transcendence of the kingdom of God, which is founded on the fact that it does not derive from a merely human initiative, but from the plan, the design and the will of God himself. And, on the other hand, they tell us plainly that Jesus Christ is the one who makes it present and brings it about in the world, through his Incarnation. However, the reign of Christ (this too the texts make very clear, and we know it very well) is not as the world imagined it back then, nor even now –we might say– although more than twenty centuries have already passed.

That is why today, when we have the happiness of gathering in this magnificent church of the abbey of Fossanova to celebrate the Solemnity of the Incarnation of the Word, an “event which –as the Constitutions say– is greater than the creation of the world and cannot be surpassed by any other”2, and which (also according to our Proper Law) is for us the sun of our faith, the starting point of our history, the pledge of our hopes, the motive that glorifies all our sorrows, what drives us in our missionary task, and the tremendous fact that links our life with immortality, I would like us to ponder a little the uniqueness of this text.

In our Constitutions we have the following sentence, which is somewhat mysterious or mystical, and which says: “The law of the Incarnation is a law of suffering”3. Perhaps in no text of the Gospel are those realities found so closely united as in this one.

The text of Jn 18:37 is a profound expression and, if you like, disconnected in itself; but read in the light of the context in which it unfolds, of the solemnity of the account and of the magnitude of what is taking place, it makes us return to it again and again.

1. The Dialogue between Jesus and Pilate

As we well know, the dialogue between Jesus and Pilate inside the Praetorium is a marvel of the fourth Gospel. It is an extremely graphic text and a remarkable literary production, so much so that it seems written as if it were the script of a theatrical scene.

It is a text that represents a singular peak in the story of the Passion. After this episode, we could say (following St. Thomas), the definitive road toward the cross will begin. Jesus will be scourged and reviled; a crown of thorns will be pressed onto his head, a red cloak and a reed scepter will even be put on him in mockery, and he will be brutally humiliated before the whole crowd in the Ecce Homo (marking in a graphic way, as it were, the contrast between the two kingdoms, that of Jesus and that of the world). From then on the physical sufferings in the Passion will go constantly in cresendo until ending on the cross, in St. John’s consummatum est (19:30).

The episode with the procurator Pontius Pilate, as we know, has two moments: one public, before the people and the Sanhedrin (probably even Caiaphas was there, as St. John tells us); and the other in private (he had Jesus called aside, in the praetorium, because he had serious doubts about the charge on which he was being condemned), and it is this one that interests us now.

– There is first a dialogue of questions: Then Pilate went back into the praetorium and called Jesus and said to him: “Are you the king of the Jews?” Jesus answered: “Do you say this on your own, or have others told you about me?” Pilate answered: “Am I a Jew? Your own people and the chief priests have handed you over to me. What have you done?” (Jn 18:33-34).

– And finally there are affirmations. Two, specifically:

  • The first of them refers to the nature of his Kingdom: My Kingdom is not of this world. If my Kingdom were of this world, my followers would have fought so that I would not be handed over to the Jews; but my Kingdom is not from here4.

This affirmation or confession of Jesus probably placed Pilate in a strange situation: the accused was claiming kingship and a kingdom (basileia). But on the other hand he was affirming that it was a kingship and a kingdom of an altogether particular kind: no one fights for this reign, it is not a violent kingdom, it has no legion at its disposal. Therefore, he would have thought, it did not represent a threat to the Roman order, and this perhaps reassured Pilate.

The kingdom of Christ is not domineering: the Son of God became incarnate and came into this world when crimes were great, and yet he told us: those who take up the sword will die by the sword5; He came into this world when men would have proclaimed him king immediately if he had improved their political or economic situation, and yet he said: My kingdom is not of this world. He came into this world, accustomed to judging temporal power by appearances, and to this world he said: Take up your cross and follow me6.

  • Then there is the second affirmation Our Lord makes, in which he highlights the relationship between the king, the kingdom and the subjects who make it up (and it is the text that interests us, Jn 18:37): Pilate therefore said to him: “So you are a king?” Jesus answered: “You say it: I am a king.” “For this I was born and for this I came into the world, to bear witness to the truth. Everyone who is of the truth hears my voice”7.

The verse is strong, for, on the one hand, it places Truth as an essential, definitive and distinctive constituent of the kingdom he came to establish in this world; and, on the other, it declares that it is the cause (or the end) of the Incarnation: for this I was born… for this I came into the world.

It even seems like an unexpected affirmation (Pilate asks him whether he is a king, and He responds with this explanation of his affirmation that he is a king). If we look closely, we have:

  1. First, a reference to the king: Yes, as you say, I am a king (obviously of the kingdom he has just described).
  2. Second, there is a reference to the kingdom: For this I was born and for this I came into the world: to bear witness to the truth (it is a kingdom established in the truth).
  3. Finally, in third place, a reference to the subjects of that king. It is an important sentence: Everyone who is of the truth hears my voice. Everyone who accepts the truth and abides in it belongs to his kingdom.

Pope Benedict XVI explains it this way: “Throughout the interrogation Pilate introduces another term coming from his world and normally linked with the word ‘kingship’: power, authority (exousía). Dominion requires power; indeed, it defines it. As we see, with these words Jesus has created an absolutely new concept of kingship and of kingdom, and he sets it before Pilate, the representative of classical power on earth.” And he asks: “What must Pilate think? What must we think of this concept of kingdom and kingship? Is it something unreal, a daydream we can do without? Or does it perhaps concern us in some way?” But there is something more, the Pope says: “Alongside this, however, with the clear delimitation of the idea of kingdom (no one fights, earthly powerlessness), Jesus has introduced a positive concept to make understandable the essence and particular character of the power of this kingship: truth. And this is what is important.”

Certainly Christ’s answer must have surprised (or unsettled) the Roman procurator.

That is why it has been said that in this verse of “the end of the Incarnation” two unquestionable realities are taught to us:

  1. It teaches us that truth is the end of the Incarnation… he came into the world to manifest it… to bear witness to it. For this I was born… for this I came into the world.
  2. It teaches us that, ultimately, the search for truth, adherence to it and preaching it are the most important things in this life: everyone who is of the truth hears my voice.

Impressive and simple at the same time: Jesus Christ is king, he came to bear witness to the truth, and whoever adheres to the truth is the one who forms part of his kingdom. For this Truth (with a capital letter) it is worth leaving behind everything that is most precious to man. If their kingdoms are different, this affirmation implies in a certain way that adherence to this truth entails ‘not getting along with the world’ and being scourged and crucified by it (as we see if we read on about what Pilate does with the Truth). “The law of the Incarnation is a law of suffering”8.

There are many who assent to these words outwardly, but few are those who are willing to suffer as much as necessary for adherence to this Truth. That is why our Proper Law, in a fatherly and farsighted way, already warned us, telling us to guard against “sacrificing the truth and one’s own conscience in an attempt to maintain a false peace, not to cross a friend, to avoid some problem or, at times, to gain advantage through silence or applause. [Because] Those who do such things resemble those Jews who believed in Him, but because of the Pharisees did not confess him, fearing to be put out of the synagogue, for they loved the glory of men more than the glory of God9”10.

Today, the day of the Incarnation of the Lord, it is fitting to remember that on the day of our religious profession we committed ourselves to the sublime office of being servants of the truth11, and to “always seek the glory of God, the ultimate end of the whole universe; in a particular way, in the search for, investigation, proclamation and celebration of the truth”12, when we solemnly said that we want “all men to discover the attraction of and the longing for divine beauty”13.

2. The perennial question

The text of the dialogue with Pilate ends in a sublime way… it lets us understand that the procurator grasped, in a certain way, the foundational character of Jesus’ affirmation; and he then asks his question not about the accusations presented, but about the foundation: What is truth? What is known as “the perennial question,” or the inevitable and inescapable question of life. A question that was addressed –no less– to the one who had just said for this I came into the world, to bear witness to the truth.

What is truth? Does truth exist? Does it make sense to speak of objective truth (based on the object… on reality)? Can one speak of absolute truth?

The outcome of the scene is incredible: Pilate does not wait, does not listen, does not seek… he simply goes outside, explains that there is nothing serious against this man, and watches as the people and their rulers reject the truth by choosing Barabbas. What does he do then? He goes back inside and orders the crucifixion of the one who had said he was the witness to the truth… of the one who had said I am the truth. Astonishing!

3. The world we live in

Let us notice that there is a very great and eloquent parallel between the attitude of Pilate and the attitude of the contemporary world, with which we must dialogue and to which we too –because we follow Jesus Christ– have the obligation to offer the witness to the truth.

There is a very great contrast between the attitude of those who profess faith in the Truth of the Incarnation and the attitude of the world before the very mystery of truth incarnate. Not for nothing had our Lord, before the affirmation to which we are referring, said that his kingdom was not of this world… that it was totally different from the kingdoms of this world.

For, like Pilate, modern man also asks the question: what is truth… but, like Pilate, with his evasive attitude he trivializes what is fundamental and foundational. Just like Pilate, man today realizes the transcendence of the question, the importance it has, but once he has formulated it, he falls into a skeptical and relativistic attitude. It seems that the basic observation is that truth does not exist… or if it exists, we do not know whether we can attain it. Both end up blinded before the Truth in person who stands in front of them; and how many today, like Pilate yesterday, order the truth to be scourged and finally crucified.

This is an image to remember (that of Pilate sending Jesus… the Truth… to be crucified); for we must be forewarned: to be witnesses to the truth implies the violent persecution of the world. That is what happened to our Lord, and the disciple is not above his master (Lk 6:40).

This is the world we face:

– Not long ago the Barna Research Group in the United States asked Americans Pilate’s same question in the following form: “Does absolute truth exist?” The result of the survey was the following: 66% of adults answered that they believe that “there is no such thing as absolute truth.” What is more, the most common statement among the answers was that “different people can define truth in conflicting ways and still be right at the same time.” They then asked the same question of younger people (between 18 and 25 years old), and the result was that 72% stated that one cannot speak of absolute truth.

– Another example: at Harvard University a series of numerous random interviews were conducted among various students with the same question, “Is it possible to speak of the existence of truth?”; more explicitly, the question was framed as follows: “Is there a truth that can remain stable across times and cultures?” The answers obtained were summarized in the following four statements, the report says: 1. “Truth is found in whatever you believe, whatever that may be”; 2. “There is no absolute truth”; 3. “In case there were an absolute truth, there is no way we could know which it is”; 4. “People who believe that absolute truth exists are dangerous people.”

It is easy to see that all these statements are illogical and contradictory; nevertheless, we know well that they are what make up the dominant way of thinking. Pope Benedict XVI has described this reality as a dictatorship, as despotic thinking, as “the dictatorship of relativism.”

That is how it is, and we see it constantly. For many thinkers (leaders, rulers) of today, truth depends on the outlook not only of a culture but of whatever one wants to impose. The dominant way of thinking does not always consider a statement true by taking care to ascertain its correspondence with reality. Adecuatio rei et intellectus: that is not the criterion. A true statement is nothing but the statement that a person, a culture, a situation or a convenience causes to be considered true. And this is the criterion that is applied.

A poet named Steve Turner wrote a parody of this modern view of reality, which he called “I believe” (Creed). And in one part, sarcastically, known as the “creed of modern man,” he says (in our rendering): “I believe that every man must find the truth,
whichever is good for him / reality will then have to adapt to it. / The universe, if necessary, will readjust itself / and history, if necessary, will be altered. / I believe there is no absolute truth / except the absolute truth that there is no absolute truth. / I believe in the rejection of creeds, / and in the flowering of individual thought.”

This poet also says that to the question “Can man live without God?” or “Can man live without an absolute truth?” the modern world answers: “we all know very well that the answer is NO… but we will keep trying anyway.”

If the existence of Truth is denied, or if it is sent to be scourged as Pilate did, everything becomes relative and everything is irrelevant; and the only thing that counts is the thinking of whoever wants to impose himself, regardless of whether it has any relation to reality or not. If there are no absolute truths, there is no right and no correct. It is as Fr. Richard John Neuhaus (the founder and editor of First Things) said: “when truth is absent, the one who has power is the only one who matters in society” (in the absence of truth, power is the only game in town).

Pope Benedict XVI affirmed it: “What is truth? Pilate was not the only one who set this question aside as insoluble and, for his purposes, impracticable. Today too it is considered bothersome, both in political struggle and in discussion about the shaping of law. But without truth, man ultimately loses the meaning of his life, leaving the field open to the strongest.”

The modern world thinks that in this way it can do “basically whatever each one feels like” and thus believe itself “free,” forgetting that the founding principle of authentic freedom is truth. The truth will make you free, says our Lord in St. John.

4. The perennial truth

Finally, the following. Before this reality… before these statistics… before the reigning relativism and skepticism… we may well ask ourselves: What is our answer? What is our attitude? What are we going to say to modern man? To young people who basically do not believe that truth can be reached?

Well then, our answer is basically what we celebrate today with so much devotion and solemnity. Our answer is: the Word became flesh… It is a clear and firm answer. We believe that in this mystery is found the answer and the solution to all the problems and questions of contemporary man and of man of all times.

The Word became flesh and came to bear witness to the truth. For this he was born and for this he came into the world. He showed us the Truth, He taught it to us and He explained it to us, and He gave us conclusive proofs of the truthfulness of his testimony. Since then there is no other possible option, there is no other answer… neither for us nor for the world… only united to Him can we find the way out and the solution, because everyone who is of the truth hears my voice.

Quid est veritas? We say it to the modern world, we say it to Pilate: Truth is God and Jesus Christ. Truth is the Incarnate Word.

  • And the Word of God became flesh, and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, full of grace and full of truth (Jn 1:14)
  • I am the way, I am the truth and I am the life (Jn 14:6)
  • I speak the truth (Jn 8:45)

That is why St. Paul’s recommendation to the first Christians was the following: if indeed you have heard him and have been taught by him, according to the truth that is in Jesus… put off the old man … and put on the new man, created according to God in the justice and holiness of truth14.

[Peroratio] During the novena that we finished praying yesterday, we went day by day meditating on and asking for the immeasurable grace of our faith in the Incarnation of the Word. For we know that in that faith is found the answer to everything, the solution to all the problems of our existence; there is found the peace and the life of the soul.

That is why, yes –let us say it once more– our answer is and will always be that of St. John’s prologue: the Word became flesh and we have seen his glory, full of grace and truth… for the law was given to us through Moses, but truth came to us through Jesus Christ. Even more:

  • truth is our end on this earth, for “we commit all our strength to inculturate the Gospel, that is, to prolong the Incarnation [of the Word] in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man”15;
  • it is our spirit, for “we consider that our spirituality must be profoundly marked by all the aspects of the mystery of the Incarnation”16;
  • it is what we set before ourselves when we say that we want “to live with all radicality the demands of the Incarnation”17;
  • because ultimately this is our mission: “the mission received from the founder and sanctioned by the Church is to bring to fullness the consequences of the Incarnation of the Word, which is the compendium and root of all goods”18.

May the Blessed Virgin always watch over and protect our faith in the fact that the Word became flesh and dwelt among us.

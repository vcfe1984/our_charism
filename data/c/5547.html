The necessary path of the metaphysical element

Cf. John Paul II, Fides et Ratio, 83

[Introduction] Without a doubt “culture is a fundamental dimension of the person and of human communities. [And] since culture is the result of the life and activity of a human group, the persons who belong to that group are formed, to a great extent, by the culture in which they live. As persons and societies change, so too does culture change with them. When culture is transformed, it likewise transforms persons and societies”1.

Each one of the religious who are here, I and each one of you, has been called by God to the sublime and, more than ever, urgent task of evangelizing the culture. For this, “it is essential to know sufficiently today’s culture in its main lines of thought and orientations”2 in order to be able to refute effectively the errors of our time3 and to make our proposal from the Gospel.

Hence the great importance for us of attending the Lectio Brevis as the inaugural act of the sustained effort we must make throughout the whole year to study, sparing neither means nor effort4 for it, given the great bearing it has on evangelization; since study is one of the principal means by which we prepare ourselves for a fruitful and effective dialogue with the men of our time, as the apostolic ministry5 entrusted to us demands.

In this identifying of the lines of thought and orientations of the humanity of our time, the Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen, in the prologue of his book “Religion Without God”, maintains that nowadays6 “there is an evident contempt for all that was best in the culture and in the tradition we inherited”. And in this same sense Gaudium et spes does not hesitate to affirm that: “the conditions of life of modern man, from the social and cultural point of view, have undergone a profound change, such that one can speak of a new age of human history”7. In which the “modern world appears at once powerful and weak, capable of the best and the worst”8.

We perceive this in innumerable symptoms: it is enough to go downtown one day and observe people’s behavior, to look even at the behavior of children and young people in our parishes, to read some political speech in the newspaper, to pay attention to the debate on laws in some countries… it is evident that our world is deteriorating in aspects that are vital.

The Archbishop emeritus of Philadelphia, Archbishop Chaput, wrote in an article something that seems to me to sum up these symptoms in some way: “we live in a disenchanted world, which means a world remade by human hands, rationalized and technocratic. It is a world without transcendence. In this world, we ‘protect’ ourselves from the inconvenience of God, from His demands and His invitations, by walling ourselves off from Him. God, for example, we do not mention in polite conversation. Religious convictions are held to be difficult or awkward at best, and dangerous at worst. What happens to human reason in the absence of any reference to God is predictable. Its horizons become low. It becomes the tool of the modern sciences. […] Man is reduced to a physical animal who solves his problems through technology, politics and economics. Culture becomes an empty shell. The spirit is stifled by the deep questions about justice, friendship, fidelity, worship, existence, God, beauty. […] Our modern universities usually avoid God as a serious subject of study. Without God, or at least without some sense of a higher order, the dignity of the person is little more than folklore, the residue of pre-Darwinian beliefs. God and the soul are in exile. And that is”, concludes the Archbishop, “because classical philosophy is in exile, to the detriment of genuine learning”9.

The genius of Fulton Sheen said something similar: “What traditional thought called transcendent (but not in Kant’s sense), our contemporaries regard as extrinsic; and the extrinsic is considered a threat to freedom of thought and to the freedom to think; as something that stands between us and ‘our power’ to procure our salvation by ourselves”10, and so one ends up adopting an attitude of complete indifference toward the truth; man is no longer interested either in what lies beyond him, or in what is above him, or in what is superior to him.

Indeed, we see ad nauseam the notorious distrust of reason and of the human capacity to grasp the truth; even the very concept of truth is called into question. There are those who say that truth is constructed culturally for the management of the masses and that so-called “absolute truths” are nothing more than the product of a culture at a given moment of time and place, and therefore subject to criticism and change11. (To affirm such a thing “is the same as saying that something is true at three in the afternoon but false at half past four, and the same as saying that two apples plus two apples are four apples on a Monday, but not on a Friday”12). At the same time, each person is urged to develop an autonomous vision of himself, different from that of others; each has his ‘own truth’. It is the culture of “It’s all about me” or “the me-culture”. This leads inevitably to a culture of simultaneous selfishness “in which everyone thinks, more or less, the same thing, which is what the media repeat daily, ad nauseam, the media being under the dictatorship of the ‘givers of meaning’”13.

According to the Scottish philosopher Alasdair MacIntayre14, our society today is regulated by what he calls the ‘morality of emotivism’15, by which man implicitly places feelings above prudent judgment (it is the “just do it” we see, for example, in Nike advertisements), ‘sincerity’ above facts, and so, in the name of being ‘authentic’ –though in a worldly and even perverse sense– man does not care about the great harm he may cause to others and even to himself; what is more, he takes pride in it16 (in fact, ‘authenticity’ in quotation marks is one of the arguments used by the agenda of gender ideology).

“Life is no longer appreciated or loved; that is why a certain culture of death advances, with its bitter fruits: abortion and euthanasia. The body and human sexuality are not rightly valued or loved; from this derives the degradation of sex, which is manifested in a wave of moral confusion, infidelity and pornographic violence”17…If one stops to think about it, modern man has manufactured a world of sex without children, a world where one can generate (and even design) a new life without there being any contact between its progenitors.

All this that we have been saying is what our Proper Law synthesizes when it says that man, “intoxicated by the prodigious conquests of an unrestrainable scientific-technical development, and fascinated above all by the most ancient and ever new temptation of wanting to become like God (cf. Gen 3:5) through the use of a freedom without limits, forgets God, considers Him without meaning for his own existence, rejects Him, and sets himself to adore the most diverse ‘idols’”18.

Faced with these symptoms, what is the diagnosis? Our beloved St. John Paul II, with clairvoyant vision, does not hesitate to affirm: “We live in a time characterized, in its own way, by the rejection of the Incarnation”19, because of ancient errors that do not die, but remain dormant for a time and then reappear in other forms20, and which we identify as the two great evils of gnosticism and of the philosophy of immanence (other great evils derive from these postulates, such as, for example, neo-Pelagianism in morals and neo-Arianism in dogmatics). Gnosticism is ancient; immanence is modern. And these are the two main points into which I am going to divide this conference.

1. Gnosticism

In the first place, gnosticism. Gnosticism is a syncretism, which is why it is often difficult to detect. Nevertheless, gnosticism claims to give a worldview of God, of man and of the world. It is “the knowledge of what we are and of what we have become; of the place from which we come and of that into which we have fallen; of the goal toward which we hasten and from which we have been rescued; of the nature of our birth and of our rebirth”21. It is not a particular religion, but rather takes elements from different religions to form a kind of mosaic. What is more, it avoids taking sides or opposing any religion and culture.

A first misunderstanding that should be dispelled: gnosticism is not the same as gnosis. We are speaking in the context of biblical revelation, Mosaic and especially Christian. There exists a Christian gnosis clearly different from gnosticism, even Christian gnosticism.

When one speaks of Christian gnosis, one refers to that knowledge of God and of His mysteries which the ancient authors describe as the higher ideal of the perfect Christian and which they distinguish from simple faith. Christianity –and the Jewish religion that preceded it– is a knowledge; the knowledge of a secret or revelation, which is God’s design to save man. In a particular way, this Christian gnosis would be fully and systematically elaborated, in large part against the gnostic heresy, by Clement of Alexandria and Origen. The Origenist tradition remains alive in Gregory of Nyssa and Evagrius Ponticus.

But there is always a grave danger. That danger is gnosticism, which John Paul the Great defined as “that attitude of the spirit which, in the name of a profound knowledge of God, ends up distorting His Word, replacing it with words that are merely human”22. That is why we can say that gnosticism is a corruption of the Christian gnosis we were speaking of a moment ago. Now notice that the same Pope warned us: “Gnosis has never disappeared from the sphere of Christianity, but has always lived alongside it, sometimes in the form of philosophical currents, more often in religious or para-religious modes, with a decided, though at times undeclared divergence from what is essentially Christian”23. “Are we not seeing today the strange resurgence of gnosis and esoteric beliefs together with ancient pantheism and old paganism? Men have abandoned the fountains of living water, as the Prophet already said, and have gone to empty cisterns”24. And even before him, the Holy Pope Paul VI denounced: “We do not accept the attitude of those who seem to ignore the living tradition of the Church, even the Gospel itself, spiritual realities, the divinity of Christ, His Resurrection or the Eucharist, practically emptying them of content and thus creating a new gnosis”25. These are –says John Paul II– those who with “bold denials that touch the very heart of the Catholic faith, such as, for example, the lack or at least the uncertainty, regarding Christ, of His clear identity as Son of God, and many other vital points of the Creed”26.

They are those who make a concoction of the natural and the supernatural, confuse psychology with spirituality27, “want to accommodate the Church to the world”, etc. “Hence progressivism, [for example] being a gnosis, is compelled to fall into religious syncretism or irenicism or false ecumenism, in which all doctrinal distinctions disappear, even revealed ones”28. “It is a kind of mixture with which no Christian understanding should clothe itself”29, says our Directory of Spirituality.

The decisive point is the following: Why is legitimate gnosis so easily transformed into gnosticism?30 It is because of human pride, the same pride that brought evil into the world in Genesis and that makes reason grow proud and believe itself self-sufficient, and therefore resist the authentic truth of God and His decisive redemptive work. For we observe, admirably, in the great thinkers who study these subjects, that the decisive and determining point is found before the mystery of the Cross of Christ; on its acceptance or rejection everything is at stake, for the truth is at stake. Let us see it.

God reveals Himself, reveals His intimate mysteries, and also reveals many things that, because of the weakness of the human intellect, are mysterious to man. Man responds by accepting, assimilating, knowing divine revelation. It is the response of faith. “Faith alone makes it possible to penetrate the mystery, favoring its coherent understanding”31, says Fides et Ratio.

We are beings made for the Truth. And that is why we are beings unlimited in our desires. However, we experience many limitations, and one of them is the limit of our reason because of “its finiteness before the infinite mystery of God”32. That is why, after experiencing the limit of our understanding, it is necessary to accept that we need help from on high. Accepting that help demands “the humility of reason before the mystery”33, against which man can certainly rebel. Here gnosticism and the resistance of reason reappear.

The Hebrew people, for example, witnessed innumerable divine interventions in their favor. In many of them God made it very clear that it was His arm and not weapons that gave the victory, a victory for which the people had to give thanks in humility.

But many of them fell into the temptation of gnosticism, also called Kabbalah: an attempt to dominate and instrumentalize the divine powers in a magical way.

Something very similar happens in modern times. To illustrate it, Fulton Sheen uses a passage from the Prophet Isaiah, who, referring to the ancient idols, said: The carpenter takes measurements with the line, marks out the shape with the stylus, works it with the chisel and draws it with the compass; he gives it the figure of a man and the beauty of a human being, that it may dwell in his house. Indeed, he cut down some cedar, or took a holm oak and an oak which he had let grow among the trees of the forest, or planted a fir which the rain then made grow. Man uses them to make fire, takes them to warm himself and also kindles them to bake bread. But, besides, he makes a god of them and prostrates himself before it; he makes an idol and worships it34. The modern idolater –says Fulton Sheen– does not go to the forest, but enters the laboratory, and there, with the help of scientific concepts, he fashions the kind of God he will afterwards worship. This is the fatality of a philosophy that no longer speaks of God, but of the idea of God”. […] “If God were only an idea, there would be very little difference between the ancient idolatry and the new, for the ancient idols were the work of the hands of men and the modern idols are created by their intelligence”35. So far Fulton Sheen.

This temptation to the “kabbalah” or to the “idolatry” of gnosticism is not absent in many who claim to be Christians, as we said before. In fact, Paul VI himself goes so far as to say: “We are in a gnostic age. And this is the battle: against the cerebrations of gnosis”36.

A few years ago we celebrated the 20th anniversary of the magnificent Encyclical Fides et Ratio (which is the true Magna Carta on this subject), where we read a phrase that can give us the answer to the question of why one falls into this: “the Cross can give reason the ultimate answer”37 that man seeks. Nevertheless, the Cross is precisely and at the same time the central point that challenges all philosophy [and that presents the great challenge for our understanding]. At this point every attempt to reduce the saving plan of the Father to pure human logic is doomed to failure. For what God wishes to accomplish, the mere wisdom of the wise man is no longer possible; rather, a decisive step is required to welcome a radical newness: God chose rather what is foolish in the world to confound the wise […]. What is low and despised in the world God has chosen; what is not, to reduce to nothing what is38. And this is for many men, for many Christians –even today– a scandal (though they do not admit it conceptually).

The wisdom of man refuses to see in his own weakness the precondition of his strength; in the face of which St. Paul does not hesitate to affirm: the preaching of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing; but to those who are being saved –to us– it is the power of God. (…) It pleased God to save those who believe through the foolishness of preaching. Thus, while Jews demand signs and Greeks seek wisdom, we preach Christ crucified, a scandal to Jews, foolishness to Gentiles39. Man cannot manage to understand how death can be a source of life and love, but to reveal the mystery of His design of salvation God has chosen precisely what reason considers folly and scandal.

In imitation of the Incarnate Word, reason too must pass through this difficult self-emptying of the cross, a self-emptying which nevertheless does not destroy it but is able to strengthen it: its knowledge is transformed into “affective” knowledge, in which God comes even to be “experienced” more than merely known. It is the “Crede, ut intelligas” of St. Augustine, who knew gnosticism from within but knew how to make use of humility, this being the vehicle that freed him from gnosticism; and it was then that his reason, thus humbled, began to be raised up to become the Eagle of Hippo and the beacon of the Christian West.

That is why, if we make an in-depth analysis, we see that it is in the truth of the preaching of, and authentic penetration into, the mystery of the Cross that contemporary problems are finally centered. For from its rejection or acceptance follows either the rise or the collapse of contemporary gnosticism (as it was with that of all times).

That is why the words of John Paul the Great in Fides et Ratio are particularly illuminating –and with this I wish to finish this first point–: “The wisdom of the Cross, then, surpasses every cultural limit that one might wish to impose on it and obliges one to open oneself to the universality of the truth of which it is the bearer. What a great challenge is presented to our reason, and what profit it obtains if it does not give up! Philosophy, which by itself is capable of recognizing man’s ceaseless self-transcendence toward the truth, can, aided by faith, open itself to welcome in the folly of the Cross the authentic critique of those who believe they possess the truth, imprisoning it in the recesses of their system. The relationship between faith and philosophy finds in the preaching of Christ crucified and risen the reef on which it can be shipwrecked, but beyond which it can flow into the boundless ocean of truth. Here the boundary between reason and faith becomes evident, but the space in which both can meet also becomes clear”40.

2. Immanence

Let us now say something about the philosophy of immanence. Our Directory of Evangelization of Culture affirms: Man is essentially a religious being, for which reason the transcendent dimension constitutes the heart of culture. ‘Every culture is an effort of reflection on the mystery of the world and in particular of man: it is a way of expressing the transcendent dimension of human life. The heart of every culture is constituted by its approach to the greatest of mysteries: the mystery of God’41. The great cultural challenge of today lies in the sphere of man’s openness to truth and to the good, ultimately to transcendence and to the mystery of God. [Since] the “ontological” dependence of man in relation to God is the ultimate foundation of the full development of human freedom and life, as well as of respect for the dignity and life of every human being. That is why the central place in every culture is occupied by the attitude that man takes before the mystery of God42”43.

However, we are not afraid to affirm that the most impressive cultural phenomenon of this age is precisely atheism, especially practical atheism. Gaudim et spes, in fact, says that “it is one of the gravest phenomena of our time”44. This practical atheism is the one about which John Paul II warned us in his apostolic exhortation Ecclesia in Europa when he said: today “European culture gives the impression of a ‘silent apostasy’ on the part of self-sufficient man, who lives as if God did not exist”45. And we have seen it reflected to some extent also in the symptoms we mentioned at the beginning, in all cultures, not only in Europe.

This atheism is the logical consequence of the principle of immanence, hence its gravity, since the principle of immanence, by remaining in mental being, does not reach extra-mental being, and cannot, therefore, validly ascend to the Principle of all beings, to the Supreme Being. That is why Pope Pius X calls the philosophy of immanence: “An infinite heap of sophisms, with which all religion is cracked and destroyed!”46

So, if earlier we said that gnosticism is a syncretism, now we must say that the philosophy of immanence presents itself as radically concentrated in a single principle: intra-mental being, the cogito.

Unlike ‘classical naturalist atheism’, in which there was as it were a lowering and a materialization of man by placing him on the same ontological level as matter, modern atheism implies a divinization of man, that is, the elevation of man to the cogito, that is, the reduction of the actuation of being to the actuation of the cogito. That is why Fabro can add that “this positivity of the new atheism is expressed in the ambitious epithet of ‘humanism’ which the atheists of the modern age claim, especially from Feuerbach on”. The latter goes so far as to affirm, for example: “God is nothing but humanity”47, and that is why one even speaks of the “drama of atheist humanism”48.

That is to say: modern thought sets up thought, the cogito, as a creative source. Hence, God is not necessary. That is: “The man who ‘makes’ God, in his head, then denies Him”. Thought suffices to create the world. If the reality of the world is not independent of and prior to thought, there is no need either to seek the explanation of that worldly reality and of thought. Consequently, God does not exist. The principle of immanence implies atheism.

Its three principal historical moments can be identified in the philosophies of Descartes, Spinoza and Hegel.

a) Descartes introduces the principle of immanence as the first principle of philosophy: “Cogito ergo sum”. God is reduced to a mere content of human consciousness. God is no longer Creator, nor does He give Himself in the mystery of the Incarnation and the Redemption; He is only an idea freely elaborated by human thought. Consequently, the foundations of the “philosophy of evil” crumble. For evil, in its realist sense, can exist only in relation to the good and, in particular, to God, the supreme Good […]. But if man by himself, without God, can decide what is good and what is evil, he can also decide, for example, that a certain group of human beings be annihilated49. Think of the communist regimes, think of abortion, and of all the other forms of infringing the law of God that emanate from this conception.

b) Spinoza introduces metaphysical immanence. For him, God is identified with the world of created things. The creature being divine, man too is divine, and he remains united to the divinity. The immanence of God in man is changed into the immanence of man in God.

c) Hegel constructs a philosophy that becomes, as it were, the most complete form of modern thought, the theoretical and most systematic expression of the principle of immanence. He goes so far as to affirm: “Without the world, God is not God”.

This is what our Proper Law –quoting Cornelio Fabro four times in a row– defines as contemporary nihilism-atheism50, which is radical atheism as the denial of the transcendent, of the existence of ‘a metaphysical world that transcends the sensible world’51, causing “the human spirit, closed in on itself, no longer to open itself to the real –to the act of being– and, therefore, to God, and so it becomes incapable of expressing the truth and of grounding freedom”52 .

Unfortunately, says a contemporary author, “it is now noticeable that the fascination with Hegel is gaining ground not only in the Protestant camp, but also in the Catholic camp”53. That is why I would like to mention at least two examples that show the application of these principles in the Catholic milieu:

The first example is “the dialectic that has also entered the Catholic world regarding the historical Christ and the Christ of faith. In the end it turns out to be something Hegelian, because for that whole line of thought the real historical Christ is of no interest; what matters is the idea one has of Christ, which is eternal, which is present in our soul beyond its historical exemplifications”54. John Paul the Great in Redemptoris Missio says, word for word: “It is contrary to the Christian faith to introduce any separation between the Word and Jesus Christ. St. John clearly affirms that the Word, who was in the beginning with God, is the same who became flesh55. Jesus is the Incarnate Word, a single and inseparable person: one cannot separate Jesus from Christ, nor speak of a ‘Jesus of history’ who would be different from the ‘Christ of faith’. The Church knows and confesses Jesus as the Christ, the Son of the living God56. Christ is none other than Jesus of Nazareth, and He is the Word of God made man for the salvation of all”57. Crystal clear.

The second example corresponds to progressivism, which considers that reality has a one-dimensional character, that is, it pushes toward a monism; and full progressivism is an absolute monism, which in its rejection of transcendence affirms absolute immanence and which entails a false concept of God58.

Cornelio Fabro very aptly warns: “The God of theological progressivism is the reflected image of progressivist confusion: a God of softness and weakness, a God of unconcern and sufficiency. No trace of the God who is a consuming fire, before whom the heavens are not pure, who did not spare His only Son the ‘chalice’ of the Passion”59.

As it seems to us, there are two implications that necessarily enter into progressivism, since it is a one-dimensional and immanentist conception of reality in which the natural, the profane, the worldly, the rational, the temporal, the secular are exalted… at the expense of the supernatural, the sacred, the interior life, faith, the eternal, the religious, God (it is the denial of the supernatural and the exaltation of the natural):

  1. On the one hand, the deification of man, not by grace or by the merits of Christ or by the work of the Church, but by himself, by his own power, without subordinating himself to anything or anyone. Christ, Son of God consubstantial with the Father and Savior of the world, is superfluous; the ‘institutional Church’, as distinct from the world –‘In her alone is salvation’– is superfluous; sanctifying grace –according to Trent the ‘única formalis causa’ (Dz. 799) of justification– is superfluous, and the sacraments are superfluous. Nothing is needed that comes from above and from outside: Man redeems himself.
  1. On the other hand, progressivist monism implies an immanent character. Since Church and world are one and the same, there is no “outside” the Church; everything is “inside”. Hence progressivists do not feel compelled to leave the Church. What is more, knowing the formative power of the Church over minds, they seek not to be singled out by the hierarchy as enemies of the Church. So as not to be thrown out, they are always ready to camouflage themselves.

3. Remedy

We said at the beginning that “culture is the activity that man carries out in order to perfect himself”60. But it is evident from all that has been said that not everything man does truly contributes to his perfection, and therefore not all his activities are truly “culture”. That is why not every philosophical reflection grounds a true human culture, since there are many currents of thought very widespread today –such as these two we have seen today– whose errors lead to the distortion of any culture that rests on them61.

Both gnosticism and the philosophy of immanence, and so many other currents of thought, show the need to ground culture in a true metaphysics, which can be none other than the metaphysics of being. For it is the metaphysics of being, and only the metaphysics of being, that can restore to man the true place he occupies in society and give the true foundations on which man must build his culture62.

And this is so because it is the philosophy of being that allows full and comprehensive openness to all of reality, overcoming every limit and making it possible to reach Him who perfects all things63.

The philosophies we have described, and all those not grounded in the act of being, are far from satisfying man’s deepest aspirations. They are far from providing man with “an absolute capable of giving an answer and meaning to all his searching. Something that is ultimate and the foundation of all else. (…) Hypotheses may be fascinating, but they do not satisfy”64. For there are many systems that speak of God, that affirm the existence of God, but it is not the God we men need and whom the Christian faith proclaims65.

Archbishop Chaput, in the same article I mentioned earlier, recounts how “he sometimes hears parishioners complain about some priests because of the content of their homilies. They say they are glad to be exhorted to generosity and kindness, but they hunger to have the substance of the faith, its mysteries and its doctrines, presented to them in a way accessible to them. These homilies are not easy to make –the Archbishop comments–. But they are impossible to make if we do not have a credible theology, one that has been informed by the strong philosophical tradition that is at the heart of the Church and is the patrimony of the Church”66; he is clearly referring to the perennial philosophy of the Aquinate.

For it is “from a philosophy of being that man can find his true foundation, which is being; he can [moreover] find his ultimate end, which is Being by Essence, and he can also find his ground, which is freedom. In this way he will be able to discover the true cultural values. For ultimately all cultural values –of science, of art, of laws and customs, etc.– as objective expressions of beauty, truth and goodness, are grounded in the act of being67.

We, the members of this beloved Religious Family of the Incarnate Word, will be able, on the pastoral level, to offer a positive and effective response to the great challenges and even dramas of ‘postmodern’ man, principally starting from the metaphysical level, through the philosophy of being68.

* * *

[Conclusion] That is why –and with this I am now finishing– we are asked “not to settle for a superficial knowledge of philosophy and theology, incapable of understanding in all its depth the drama of contemporary atheism and therefore incapable of remedying it”69. It is incumbent upon us, because of the mission we have received, to have a strong cultural formation that takes a stand before modern culture70. A “metaphysics with grip” is needed, one that bites into reality.

Pay attention to what John Paul II says: “If I insist so much on the metaphysical element, it is because I am convinced that it is the necessary path for overcoming the situation of crisis that affects large sectors of philosophy today and thus for correcting certain erroneous forms of behavior widespread in our society”. A verbatim quotation from the Directory of Major Seminaries, 315. For a philosophy in which the truth of Christ shines forth represents an effective contribution to the true and at the same time worldwide ethics that humanity needs today.

The present age has an urgent need of Jesus Christ. The needs of the new evangelization are enormous, and we cannot evade the challenging call Christ makes to us to go into all the world proclaiming the Gospel.

Let us always remember it: Our pastoral work must tirelessly propose Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word, the fullness of all authentically human life and culture71. “A task that belongs not only to specialists but to all”72, and for which there is need –I repeat– of a solid philosophical formation, since in pastoral life we must all face the demands of the contemporary world and examine the causes of certain behaviors in order to give them an adequate response.

“The basis for building a true civilization is to collaborate with all our strength so that ‘an authentic sense of man may prevail in the world, not enclosed in a narrow anthropocentrism, but open to God’”73. This basis is the Rock74 itself, that is, the very mystery of Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word. And He is the model from which our spirituality and our pastoral work derive at their root75.

That is why our task will be, today and always, to bear witness that the Word became flesh.

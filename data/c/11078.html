(astutia, sollertia, calliditas)

St. Thomas studies craftiness as a vice opposed to prudence, but one that has a certain likeness to it. The crafty man is the prudent one, the one who thinks, the politician, the “clever” one. The crafty man is intelligent.

But although he is intelligent, he is not of upright intelligence. He uses his intelligence to achieve his objective, whatever the means used.

For St. Paul, craftiness is opposed to the clear manifestation of the truth. Crafty is the apostle who does not preach the truth, who falsifies the word of God: “Therefore, invested with this ministry of the mercy of God, we do not lose heart, of which we have been the object, we do not lose heart, nor with craftiness nor falsifying the word of God, we manifest the truth…” (2 Cor 4:1-3).

In his commentary on the text, St. Thomas teaches us that craftiness goes against rectitude of intention. For there can be an outwardly evil work.

“In the intention evil is avoided if the intention is upright, and for this reason he says NOT WALKING IN CRAFTINESS, that is, with craftiness, simulation, hypocrisy, like those who act falsely, aiming at one thing outwardly while inwardly they seek another” (In II Cor 4:3).

Being a vice of the intention, it has a particular seriousness: it is hidden and interior. And therefore more difficult to see and to correct.

But it is not only hidden. There are other hidden vices: pride, despair, for example. Rather, its very essence is to hide, not to manifest. Hence St. Thomas relates it to simulation, hypocrisy, falsehood. It is the duplicity of which Scripture speaks: “I hate duplicity and I love your law.” (Ps 119:113)

There are people who have a great capacity for concealment. It is typical of traitors. Let us think of Judas: how he must have hidden his plan from Christ and the Apostles. Even his very way of betraying him is hidden: “with a kiss…”. The gesture of love hides the inner contempt.

In the Old Testament we find the case of the high priest Onias, murdered by the crafty Andronicus: “Andronicus came to Onias and, trusting in craftiness, clasping his hand and giving him his right hand with an oath, persuaded Onias, although the latter was not without suspicions, to come out of his refuge, and immediately put him to death, with no regard whatsoever for justice” (2 Mac 4:34).

Among us, a bad intention can be hidden beneath a pious gesture, a kindly face, a smile. Also beneath tears, which make the adversary lower his guard and feel sorry. That is why the Martín Fierro said: “I believe neither in a dog's limp nor in a woman's tears.”

It is best to concede nothing to the crafty man, for if a partial truth is conceded to him, he will easily use it to argue and to deduce from it, with great intelligence, erroneous, bad or less good conclusions. One must stand up to him, as St. Ignatius teaches with the example of the bad woman: “… it is proper to a woman, when she quarrels with a man, to lose heart and take flight when the man shows her a firm face; and, on the contrary, if the man begins to flee, losing heart, the anger, vengeance and ferocity of the woman grow very great and without measure…” (S.E. 325).

Craftiness is opposed to simplicity. Simple is the one who is one, not two: the one outside and the one inside.

The enemies of the Faith, says St. Paul, use craftiness: “…so that we may no longer be children, tossed about and carried along by every wind of doctrine through the deceitful game of men, who, to seduce, craftily employ the devices of error; but rather, embracing the truth, may we grow in all things in charity…” (Eph 4:14).

It is proper to the crafty man to “play” with people. He is “agile”: he advances or retreats, he laughs or cries, he gets angry or asks for forgiveness, he acts the friend or acts offended, he pressures or feigns docility… all of this is a deceitful game to get people to think well of him, to tell him what he wants to hear, to give him that permission he is asking for.

“Seducing” is proper to him (and in this woman is a specialist). Seduce comes from se-ducere: that is, to lead the other along with oneself; it is the art of bringing the other to my will, employing “the devices of error.”

And how many devices it is possible to use. We can say with St. Louis Marie: “I would never finish if I wanted to describe here the twists and turns of nature…” (Letter to the Friends of the Cross, n. 48).

But in the same text St. Paul gives us the means to resist the crafty: “Embracing the truth.” Note the force of the expression, for a timid and superficial adherence is not enough when strong winds blow hard.

Firm adherence to dogma. But not only to dogma. Firm adherence to spiritual truths, to the doctrine of the saints. Nor only that. Firm adherence to the concrete command of the superior.

Summing up a little, we can say that two acts belong to the crafty man: scheming and deceiving.

Scheming (excogitare) is the act by which he thinks, imagines, remembers what means to use to achieve his objective. Here the cogitative power, experience of people, intuition play a large part.

In this sense, not everyone who wants to be crafty can be.

He isn't even up to that, and so he “trips himself up,” “digs his own grave,” acts rashly. “There are fools who do not even know how to do evil,” says Scripture (Eccl 19:20).

Deceiving is the exterior act. It is the execution of what was schemed. In this too, not everyone who wants to can.

In the execution, the first means is the word, “for it holds the principal place among the signs by which man signifies something to another.” Hence guile is principally attributed to speech. However, one can also deceive by deeds.

Hence the care we must have in our words. It is very common to distinguish between “what I said” and “what I meant to say.” This shows the little value we give to words. We empty them of rational content in order to fill them with affective content.

May we be able to say: “Righteous are all the words of my mouth; there is nothing crafty or crooked in them” (Prov 8:8).

Craftiness is also exercised through deeds: “There is one who goes about bent over and in mourning, but inside he is full of deceit. He keeps his head down and pretends to be deaf, but when you least expect it he is upon you” (Eccl 19:23-24).

Craftiness is what is proper to the devil. That is why he is represented by the serpent, “the craftiest of all the animals” (Gen 3:1), for, like the serpent, the crafty man advances silently, knows how to hide and conceal himself among the foliage, knows how to wait for the opportune moment, attacks suddenly, is hard to catch, and is flexible and slippery.

In this regard, St. Paul wrote to the Corinthians: “But I fear that, just as the serpent deceived Eve with its craftiness, your minds may be corrupted, turning away from sincerity toward Christ” (2 Cor 11:3).

Craftiness, among us, can be exercised between superiors and subjects.

Cassian says that the ABC of monks was to give an account of every evil thought and temptation to their superiors.

St. Ignatius says something similar: “it is greatly fitting that subjects make themselves fully known to their superiors” (Const. 34,4).

This openness of conscience is what is most opposed to craftiness, for through it our interior is revealed, our intentions, good or bad.

In religious life, craftiness is the means to achieve two opposite things at once: to obey the superior and to do my own will. And how do I do it? Simply by making the superior command what I want to do, so that he comes over to my will. And this is not obedience, though it may seem to be.

Superiors too can act with craftiness, with a certain duplicity. With subjects we must be as transparent as we expect them to be with us.

But this does not mean that the superior may not keep things, reasons, to himself, when it is for the good of the community.

Let us proceed, then, in our life, as Christ himself did, “always embracing the truth.”

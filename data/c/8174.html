The non-negotiable elements

Good afternoon.

When I was asked to give this talk to the seminarians, I thought of giving a talk I already had prepared on a light topic, but it seemed to me that by giving that talk I would waste the great opportunity to speak to you about what has us here united as brothers and sheltered under one same roof, which is nothing other than our beloved Institute of the Incarnate Word.

[Introduction]

Some years ago now, in 1987, St. John Paul II met, as every year, with the International Union of Superiors General in Rome, and there, before an audience packed with superiors general, he told them with all the characteristic force with which he spoke when he wanted to emphasize something: “each institute must take care to maintain its own ‘physiognomy,’ the specific character of its own reason for being, which has exercised an attraction, which has given rise to vocations, to particular attitudes, giving a public witness worthy of esteem”1.

“You are daughters of your founders —the Holy Father went on to say—reflecting in present-day reality the characteristic features of their particular spiritual physiognomy. You are daughters of your congregations, which have begotten you to religious life and daily sustain you in your personal sanctification”2. And what the Holy Father said to those religious women seems to me to apply very well to us: we are sons of our founder, we are sons of our congregation. It is our task to make that physiognomy of the Institute alive in present-day reality.

This implies that each one of us must take care to keep alive that physiognomy which God has willed and wills for our Institute. What is that specific character, the Institute’s reason for being? Without any doubt, the charism of the Institute—which, by the way, everyone should know by heart and which you can find in numbers 30 and 31 of the Constitutions. But it is not only the charism; it is also those 11 non-negotiable elements attached to the charism that we have just seen in the video, and which are in one way or another unfolded in the sound traditions we have in our Institute; it is also the spirit of the congregation, which is like the air one breathes, which is not learned by reading a manual but is drunk in from our mother the Congregation, like the child who is nourished at his mother’s breast; it is, in short, the very rich patrimony with which God has been pleased to enrich our Institute.

I say this because “at times one finds today—and here I quote John Paul the Great again—a prejudice according to which the ‘differences’ that characterize and distinguish religious institutes from one another ought to be eliminated. […] It is naive and presumptuous to think or believe, in the end, that each institute must be the same as the others, practicing a general love of God and neighbor. Whoever thought this way would forget an essential aspect of the Mystical Body: the heterogeneity of its constitution, the pluralism of models in which the vitality of the spirit that animates it is manifested, the transcendent human and divine perfection of Christ, its Head, which can only be imitated according to the innumerable resources of the soul animated by grace.” So far John Paul II.

That is why in this talk we are going to look at the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism as an expression of that particular physiognomy of the Institute in present-day reality. Because at stake in this is our fidelity to God, to His Church, and therefore the endurance of the Institute in the future and, above all, our own sanctification.

Notice what the Directory of Consecrated Life says: “Religious life is a following of Christ in order to attain the perfection of charity. But such a thing is to take place only within the framework of one’s own Institute: [And it then quotes a document of the CICLSAL]: ‘The growing configuration to Christ is carried out in conformity with the charism and norms of the Institute to which the religious belongs. Each Institute has its own spirit, character, purpose and tradition, and it is by conforming himself to them that the religious grows in his union with Christ’3”4. So each one of us must be the incarnation of that charism in which God has willed us to be sanctified. Therefore, we are not only depositaries of this whole patrimony of the Institute but those responsible for it. I tell you again: this is not something that concerns only the priests, only those in perpetual vows, only the superiors, but all the members.

So here comes an explicit clarification, because there has been no lack of those who say that “we dictate what the subjects (or what everyone) in the congregation must think”5, as if we were a sect and manipulated information in favor of our interests; or, as some say, that we are fanatics, that we isolate ourselves from the rest of the Church, that we are closed, archaic… Watch out for that! Here we are simply affirming and reaffirming our identity, without watering it down, without trimming it. What is wrong with that? Since when is it wrong to call things by their name? Tirelessly the saints, the Magisterium of the Church and the pontiffs have always insisted that religious must be faithful to the Gospel and to the spirit of their founders, “without allowing ideological and instrumentalizing motivations to replace their identity or to inspire their way of acting, which must always be that of men of the Church”6, because to act otherwise would be to be “tributaries.” Do you follow?

[The non-negotiable element]Let us look, then, first of all at the non-negotiable element, which is precisely the charism of the Institute. “By the proper charism of the Institute, all its members must work, in utmost docility to the Holy Spirit and within the imprint of Mary, in order to make all that is authentically human the domain of Jesus Christ, even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions. That is, it is the grace of knowing how to act, in concrete, to prolong Christ in families, in education, in the media, in men of thought and in every other legitimate manifestation of human life. It is the gift of making each man ‘like a new Incarnation of the Word,’ being essentially missionary and Marian”7.

Therefore, and as all of you already know, “what is proper to us lies in focusing on the mystery of the Incarnation: just as the Word, in assuming human nature, united Himself in a certain way with every man, so too we want to act in our life and in our apostolates, in such a way that no work of apostolate is foreign to us, precisely because nothing authentically human is foreign to us”8.

Now, the reality is that today “what scandalizes about Jesus is His nature as God incarnate. And as with Him, they also ‘lay traps for us in life’; what scandalizes about the Church is the mystery of the incarnation of the Word. Today too we often hear it said: ‘But you Christians, be a little more normal, like other people, sensible; don’t be so rigid.’ Behind all this, in reality, is the request not to proclaim that ‘God became man,’ because ‘the incarnation of the Word is the scandal’”9. “The Son of God came and became flesh, and when that is what we preach, persecutions come, the cross comes,” said Pope Francis himself.

For many, even religious, “Christ is nothing more than a human person in whom the presence of God was found in a more particular and evident way, but they deny the hypostatic union of the human and divine natures in the Second Person of the Son, a union that is by assumption. Consequently, Christ is not the Savior of the human race, but simply an example or motivation”10. This is serious: if Christ is not God, then all the sacraments fall to the ground, the Church herself, her saving mission and her task of baptizing all nations. If man does not need the sacraments to be saved, then the Church becomes no more than a simple humanitarian organization and not the ark of salvation, being completely secularized. And if this is so: what are conversions for, what are vocations for; “why leave what I have and what I do for something I don’t know is better? Why mortify myself and crucify myself to the world if the one who demands it of me is a mere man?”11.

And unbelievable as it may seem, this is held, spread and defended by not a few men religious and priests and women religious. Many of those who say “it is the hour of the laity” in order to justify their inability to attract new candidates to a religious life with a particular charism12 often—though not always—nest in the thought that “Jesus is simply a good friend,” not the Redeemer who suffered on a cross and rose again. And that is why, instead of being concerned and occupied first of all with the ‘beyond,’ with the transcendent, with the glory of God and the eternal salvation of man, fatally their first and essential concern is the ‘here below’: temporal problems, political, economic, social liberation, ecology. There are others who replace Christ with the idea of “the values of the kingdom, which in reality has no precise content and becomes a hope without God, an empty hope”13.

That is why clinging to our proper charism, raising high the banner of the Incarnation, being passionate about “‘assuming’ cultures, purifying and elevating them starting from Christ and His Gospel, understood ‘in the Church’14”15 is nothing other than defending the deposit of our faith and “the mystery that is greater than the creation of the world and that cannot be surpassed by any other”16, which is the mystery of the Incarnation. It is in some way also defending the missionary cause, defending religious life, and spurring many others to welcome into their hearts the charism of vocation17 so as to give themselves generously to Jesus Christ, the Son of God. It is in some way doing vocational ministry—not because we proselytize, but because we are telling the world that it is worth giving everything for Jesus Christ, concretely through the witness of life we give. That is why it is not surprising that, sadly, congregations that in another time were ‘great producers’ of vocations, like the Jesuits, for example, who in 1965 numbered more than 36,000, have today lost 59% of their members and only 14,839 remain. Something similar happens with the Salesians and with the Franciscans: the former have lost 38% of their members from 1965 until now and the latter 55%; the Dominicans and the Augustinians 46%, and so we could go on. We, by the grace of God, and though very modestly compared with these giant congregations, have been increasing our vocations little by little, and today we are 928 members. And do you know how many priests we have given the Church in just these last 6 years? 106 priests. That is an enormous grace. Think that there are dioceses, there are congregations that have not had an ordination in years. And we have had 106! (and only 1 of those has left the Institute, but he is still a priest elsewhere).

Now, as a corollary of this, and so that it may be seen even more how defending the charism is defending religious life itself, we must say that “‘if we are religious, it is in order to imitate the Incarnate Word, chaste, poor, obedient and son of Mary.’ This is part of our charism”18. Our Proper Law says it very clearly. And although it would seem obvious and not to need clarification that, if one embraces religious life, one is going to live poorly, chastely and obediently, and in our case as sons of Mary, unfortunately not all religious understand this.

Do you see? To affirm and defend that “if we are religious, it is in order to imitate the Incarnate Word, chaste, poor, obedient and son of Mary” is nowadays something countercultural. And some even seize on this to say that we—as a sect that idolizes its founder—exercise “[abusive] control over the affective relationships and sexual life” of our members. It’s crazy! But that’s how it is; recently it was published that our members “are separated from their families, are not allowed to have contact with other people, are sometimes made to go hungry to keep them weak. Eventually they disappear, through transit houses, to destinations such as Italy, among others”19. These people have understood nothing! It is ridiculous to say such a thing: I don’t know if there is another Institute more oriented toward families than we are; nothing is further from us than making our members go hungry! And if we had more, we would give them more… Making them disappear because they go to Italy to study? … oh well, they say what they want.

The point is that one must realize that remaining firm in the charism is not just reciting it from memory. It must be lived conscientiously, with an eye to eternity, in the reality of the world in which we are given to live, with complete radicality, without shrinking back, and therefore one must be ready to suffer the counterattack. Only thus shall we be what we promised to be on the day of our vows: “a concrete imprint that the Trinity leaves in history”20.

Let us now move on to those non-negotiable elements attached to the charism, which are not simply decorative or accessory. “These are elements that bear supernatural fruit, that go against the current—for which reason they often provoke rejection—and that have allowed us to present a living Christianity which stands in contrast to the world, and they have been a source of vocations”21. They are so non-negotiable that doing without them would mean renouncing the mission that has been entrusted to us, deforming our identity (our physiognomy, as JPII said), and would very possibly mean that we are submitting to the spirit of the world, thus betraying the precious friendship to which Christ has called us.

[Serious spirituality] Our spirituality, as could not be otherwise, is deeply marked by all the aspects of the mystery of the Incarnation22. Therefore, it is a spirituality that impels us to transcend the sensible and disposes us to total detachment, seeking in everything and through everything the glory of God23. So then, in accordance with the charism with which God has blessed us, and given the immense spiritual needs of humanity in today’s world, we are convinced that with an ever more deeply rooted grounding in the principles of our spirituality, and by being creative when it comes to spreading it, we members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word can render the particular service that the Church asks and expects of us.

Note that the evangelization of culture demands of us a spirituality with particular nuances; that is why our Proper Law says that it “asks for a new way of approaching cultures, attitudes and behaviors so as to dialogue in depth with cultural environments and to make their encounter with the message of Christ fruitful”24. This demands of us a faith enlightened by continual reflection that confronts itself with the sources of the Church’s message, and a constant spiritual discernment sought in prayer. Therefore, following the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church, each one of us must be convinced that “evangelizing consists principally in bringing the grace of God to all men, making of them a new humanity, that is, new men created according to God in true justice and holiness25”26.

Tomorrow you are going to be missionaries in different parts of the world; so, the first thing is to pray, the second to bring the grace of God to men. And how are you going to do that? By preaching the Spiritual Exercises according to the genuine spirit of St. Ignatius of Loyola, by preaching popular missions in which Eucharistic devotion and the sacrament of reconciliation, together with devotion to Mary Most Holy, are the pillars on which the evangelization of a people is raised and preserved; and of course, by proclaiming the Word that leads men to conversion to God through “full and sincere adherence to Christ and His Gospel through faith”27.

This is what we have learned, this is what has been bequeathed to us, and no one here is surprised to hear a sermon preached on the Last Things, for example, or if purifications are spoken of, or if the faithful are exhorted to penance, or if the examples of purity of the saints are put before the faithful from the time they are children… However, this is not the reality in all parishes, not even of this diocese, much less of the rest of the world.

Some time ago Archbishop Aguer published an article in which he said: “those who are outside [the faith] are not called to conversion, nor are the faithful who need and desire to grow in the Faith instructed. Fundamental themes of the Creed and of Catholic catechesis have disappeared from ordinary preaching: God in His Unity and Trinity; Jesus Christ, true God and true man; the Redemption; sin and grace; the Commandments (the sixth, especially, is a thing of the past); Hope in eternal life; the devil and his wiles; the danger and threat of eternal damnation; and, in general, the content happily expressed in the Catechism of the Catholic Church”28.

And don’t think this happens on Mars. To look no further, a few years ago, in 2019, the superior general of the Jesuits, Fr. Arturo Sosa, was asked, “does the devil exist?” to which he answered: “Symbols are part of reality, and the devil exists as a symbolic reality, not as a personal reality. […] We have made symbolic figures, like the devil, to express evil”29. Another example—I don’t know if you know Fr. Damián María Montes, a Spanish TikTok influencer priest, who said: “The word punishment seems to me very strong when applied to the God of love and mercy in whom I believe, and therefore homosexuality should not be punished”30. St. Thomas Aquinas thought very differently, who wrote: “One can think of God in Himself… or in His effects, such as punishing”31. Now think how many souls such men lead into error…

That is why it is vital for us to strengthen ourselves in our spirituality, which is solid because it is anchored in the solid doctrine taught down the centuries by the Church; which is serious, because it gives primacy to the life of prayer; because, on the basis of the living and vigorous faith it seeks to infuse in us, it makes us capable of judging everything from the standpoint of transcendence, and gives us that providential view of all of life32 (which is another of the non-negotiable elements); because it invites us ourselves to conversion, to love of the cross, and in short, to everything you already know.

[Providential view of all of life]if we did not have that providential view of life which teaches us that God disposes everything, absolutely everything, for our good, we would long ago have closed the doors of our houses and devoted ourselves to something else. And yet, in spite of all the challenges, all the criticisms, all the threats, all the decrees, we have not stopped going out on mission, we have not stopped trying to be better, we have not stopped sending our men to study… and since “the cross makes fruitful whatever it touches”33, by the grace of God and in spite of the fact that we are unprofitable servants34, God has made our apostolates bear fruit incredibly. And today the Institute is present in 45 countries, and the number of monasteries has tripled in these last 6 years; for the first time, for example, the number of priests with an academic degree exceeds those without an academic degree; the number of religious brothers has also increased a great deal (before there were 15 brothers, now there are 48), and so on with many things…

Are you beginning to see how defending the non-negotiables is defending not only the physiognomy of the Institute in the Church but also the robust spirituality that the Incarnate Word taught us?

[To bite into reality]Closely united to what we have been saying is another non-negotiable element attached to the charism, which is “to bite into reality,” by which we mean the effective impact of our apostolic work on the culture we seek to evangelize. This aspect is without doubt one of the essential components of our evangelizing task and what gives our priestly ministry a distinctive note. This is so true that, in the midst of all the attacks the Institute suffers, even those who persecute us cannot but confess that our missionaries do an unequaled work and that they are in places where no one wants to go: Papua, Gaza…

This ‘biting into reality,’ in the first place, is born of and nourished by the right consideration of the mystery of the Incarnate Word and fidelity to it, without which all our pastoral work would unfailingly end in utter failure. Far from us be “embracing present-day culture while renouncing impregnating it with the Gospel”35. If we do not preach Christ with complete radicality, without trimming the truth, we are wasting our time.

To do this—and of this we are persuaded, and experience has shown it to us—familiarity with the Incarnate Word is necessary, nourished and increased in the life of prayer. For it is that familiarity with Christ, with His criteria, with His Truth, that gives us “that Christian common sense”36, that special ability to interpret the signs of the times free from every worldly pretension. That is what gives us the possibility of knowing how to esteem and value the various paths by which God seeks to communicate with men and, in short, of inserting ourselves effectively where we are working apostolically, because it will always be true that “true inculturation is from within, through a renewal of life under the influence of grace”37. If we preach popular missions, if we organize a camp, if we have youth groups, if we teach in a seminary… all this is so that there may be more souls in grace and more grace in each soul.

In the second place, what helps us to bite into reality, not to beat the air38, is Thomistic metaphysics; that is, this is what allows us to make an effective contribution so that the faith may become incarnate in the life and culture of men39. And there comes another non-negotiable element, which is the firm intention of following St. Thomas Aquinas.

[Clear intention of following St. Thomas Aquinas] One must strive to learn to think reality—from St. Thomas himself, entering into dialogue and debate with contemporary problems and thinkers—in order to make it known to others in an always creative way, without this meaning entering into “compromises with the spirit of the world”40. And this task becomes ever more imperative in this time, given the progressivism that is ravaging the Church “through lack of criticism and discernment in the face of modern philosophies and the assimilation of the principle of immanence”41.

This, which we take for granted, is a grace of God for us. How many congregations—as one author would say—are “steeped in the modernism, ecologism and sugary sentimentalism that clearly predominate in the Church today”42. “Relativism has been imposed almost officially; one can no longer expect a clear definition of what must be believed, and of the errors we must guard against”43, a bishop emeritus of Argentina affirmed recently.

[Eucharistic devotion, or the worthy celebration of Holy Mass]Linked to everything we have been saying—that is, because our specific end is to work to transfigure cultures in Christ44 and for Christ45, and because of the most beautiful Christocentric and Eucharistic spirituality that identifies us—we naturally defend an authentic liturgical education leading to a “full, conscious and active”46 participation in the Eucharist. There, then, comes that non-negotiable element which we call “the worthy celebration of Holy Mass,” which is nothing other than a style of celebrating Mass according to our charism47, “in which the Word becomes incarnate and in which He appears—sacramentally—Incarnate, in which the principal presence and action of the principal Priest—Christ Himself—is always highlighted, and in which it is perceived that the essential attitude of the secondary priest is the attitude of prayer—proper to one who knows himself to be a mere instrument, and a deficient instrument, subordinate to the principal cause and subject to its ends—and in which all the visible elements contribute to the splendid knowledge of the Invisible”48.

If in the Eucharist Christ is truly, really and substantially present, as our faith teaches us; if the Eucharist is not only the source of charity but, in some way, the goal of all our apostolate; if the Holy Eucharist is the “consummation of the spiritual life and the end of all the sacraments”49, and therefore all the members of the Institute are “ready to go to any part of the earth where the preaching of the Gospel and the celebration of the Eucharist are necessary”50, then necessarily the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass must be celebrated as our Proper Law says: “worthily, soberly, solemnly, with liturgical rhythm, etc. They must not be trivial or superficially rowdy liturgical celebrations, not secularized or desacralized, not vulgar or puerile, not showy or stiff51.

How many times has that style of celebrating Mass not been a reason for attack on the part of some sectors of the Church and of society? This is because progressivism “is compelled to fall into religious syncretism or irenicism or false ecumenism, in which all doctrinal distinctions disappear, even revealed ones”52. Regarding which, our Proper Law says that “it is a kind of mixture with which no Christian understanding should clothe itself”53.

[Fidelity to the Magisterium of the Church]Consequently—and as could not be otherwise—everything we do in our Institute, from the celebration of Mass, classes in the seminaries, the apostolate in the homes, parish ministry… in short, whatever you like, we do all of it “in accordance with the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church”54. And from here flows this non-negotiable element attached to the charism, which is “docility to the living Magisterium of the Church of all times”55. For we seek in the treasury of the Magisterium of the Church the solidity, the purity and the proximate norm of faith that the sublime task of evangelizing requires.

[Apostolic creativity] United to this is apostolic creativity. Because “healthy creativity is an essential element of the living Tradition of the Church”56. And therefore one must not be afraid of unprecedented apostolates, nor reject any apostolate a priori. Now, for example, this past year 3 rounds of online Exercises were held, and more than 10,000 people took part; at another time that would have been unthinkable, and yet today it bears a great deal of fruit.

[Emblematic missions]So, as you will realize, Christ’s call remains current: Put out into the deep57, and that must be taken as addressed personally to us, because, as the Magisterium of the Church itself points out to us, “there are millions of men and women who still do not know Christ, the Redeemer of man”58. Hence it is proper to us to go on mission to what we call emblematic missions. Many of our missionaries are in those emblematic missions, and you must prepare yourselves to be in an emblematic mission one day too.

Believe me that, if we were not in those places, there would be no missionaries there. If the missionary drive, the generosity, had not been bequeathed to us and instilled in us, if we did not defend our missionary identity in the Church, how many souls would be left without priests, without the Eucharist, without the forgiveness of their sins, without baptism…

Again: defending the charism, the non-negotiables, is not only defending the Institute and what we are; it is defending and advocating for the missionary cause in the Church.

[Works of mercy]Now, for example, we have been asked in another diocese of Tanzania to take charge of a home for the disabled, the elderly and orphaned children. Everything super precarious! But Jesus Christ is in the needy59, and since our calling is “to make all that is authentically human the domain of Jesus Christ”60, works of charity become for each of the members of the Institute a most apt and effective means of evangelization, and that is why they are another of the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism. And that is why, within the great variety of apostolates our Institute can embrace, we reserve “a preferential place for charitable work, since it is an essential component of the evangelizing mission of the Church and an indispensable element for the evangelization of culture”61. And so today we have 9 homes in various parts of the world.

[Spirit of joy] This is perhaps getting too long, but I did not want to fail to mention this non-negotiable, which is the spirit of joy with which all of us members must live. Our Proper Law says it again and again in several of its documents. And it is something we can all practice and that ought to characterize us: living in “contagious joy”62. That joy which is “a fruit of the Holy Spirit and an effect of charity”63 and which is born of considering that “God is infinite joy”64.

It is the simple joy of everyday things, without great display, which brings mutual sincerity and trust, the capacity for dialogue, sincere adherence to community discipline65.

This is almost mathematical: the more faithful one is to the spirit of the Institute, to the charism, the more united one is to the will of the Incarnate Word, the better one lives community life (if the other members are along the same lines, of course), and the better the witness one gives to those outside the community; and this becomes a source of vocations, and the family expands, and the possibilities for mission increase—do you understand me? It is joy, generosity in the apostolate, prayer and sacrifice that attract others to become part of our Family.

And what I say for each one is asked also of communities. Notice that our Proper Law tells us that “our communities must show how the love of Christ drives away even the shadow of sadness”66. Indeed, our Proper Law paternally commands us to “foster, even outwardly, an atmosphere of joy”67. And it emphasizes: “It is very important to cultivate this joy in the religious community: overwork can extinguish it, exaggerated zeal for some causes can make it forgotten, continual questioning about one’s own identity and one’s own future can overshadow it…”68. Therefore one must “know how to rejoice together, allow oneself personal and community moments of relaxation, distance oneself from time to time from one’s own work, rejoice in the joys of one’s brother, pay solicitous attention to the needs of the brothers and sisters, give oneself generously to apostolic work, face situations with mercy, go out to meet the future with the hope of always and everywhere finding the Lord: all this nourishes serenity, peace and joy, and becomes a strength for apostolic action”69. You can read it yourselves; it is in the Directory of Fraternal Life.

[Marian devotion] Finally, and as could not be otherwise, this devotion of ours to Our Lady, that Marian imprint which permeates our whole life and is part of our essence as religious of the Incarnate Word. Without the Virgin Mary we cannot fully accomplish our mission in the Church. That is why we must be “apostles of Mary”70; this means that we must “invoke her, greet her, think of her, speak of her, honor her, glorify her, commend ourselves to her, rejoice and suffer with her, work, pray and rest with her”71, and this, while it has always been necessary, is in our times, I would say, indispensable. We must not tire of instilling in others true devotion to Our Lady, a devotion that must be well formed, solidly educated, and we ourselves, each one, must stamp on our soul a great and tender devotion to Our Lady.

[Conclusion]I believe that these few cases we have cited here as counterexamples to the non-negotiables highlight even more the importance of each one being the body and blood of the precious charism God has called us to live. On our fidelity, on our commitment, on our union as “one single whole, [as] a compact block”72 the future of the Institute depends to a great extent. “If we lose this battle, we will lose the war”73.

That is why I wanted to end with the words of a holy founder, Don Orione, so that they may remain as a message: “Ah, my beloved, if we love God and the Church, if we love our soul and the good and the future of our Congregation, let us take care, in ourselves, above all, of the observance of the rules, and let us hold to the rule in everything! Let us keep our hand firm on the plow, let us be faithful and firm in our holy resolutions and vows, let us be persevering and go forward, living the true spirit and the life of the Congregation, as fervent religious, as true sons of Divine Providence—pure, humble, poor, simple, charitable”74.

May our Queen, Our Lady of Luján, today and always guard and defend our Institute and each one of you, and those who will come after us. Thank you very much. Pray much for the Institute.

Conference for the Seminarians of the “St. Vitalian, Pope” Seminary

April 20, 2018

[Introduction] I thank the Father Rector for the invitation and all of you for allowing me to spend this day with you, which is truly very consoling for me.

At Holy Mass a moment ago we were saying how preparation for the priesthood necessarily implies a configuration to Christ the Priest, which is brought about through the practice and purification of the theological virtues. All of which implies “living according to an infused reality.” For once the glorious day arrives when you are ordained priests, from then on you will be “representatives of Christ the head, charged by Christ himself with continuing his presence and saving work in the Church and in the world with his threefold office of sanctifying, teaching and shepherding”1.

That is why the Second Vatican Council, in its decree Optatam Totius, says that the preparation of seminarians “must aim at the formation of true shepherds of souls, after the example of our Lord Jesus Christ, priest, teacher and shepherd”2.

So, if you will allow me, I would like to make here a concrete application of the theological virtues in relation to the threefold priestly function of sanctifying, teaching and shepherding. For the office of sanctifying oneself is directly related to the theological virtue of charity, the office of teaching to that of faith, and likewise the office of shepherding to the virtue of hope. And in this way to highlight even more the importance of the exercise of our theological life in the soul in correspondence with our priestly vocation.

1. Sanctifying – The theological virtue of Charity

Our Constitutions point out as our specific end the attainment of the perfection of charity3. And if this applies to any religious of the Institute, whether lay brother, monk or novice, all the more does it apply to those who are preparing for the august priestly ministry. Because, once again, do not the Constitutions also tell us that “the priest is the man of charity”4?

The Magisterium of the Church affirms: “‘The new evangelization needs new evangelizers, and these are the priests who commit themselves to living their priesthood as a specific path toward holiness’5. But already now it is of fundamental importance that each one discover every day the absolute necessity of his personal holiness.

Therefore, it is imperative that “the spiritual formation of the future priest include the practice of charity, which is the bond of perfection”6. “In particular a preferential love for the poor, in whom Christ is present in a special way, and a merciful love full of compassion for sinners”7. You already have this; it is a matter, then, of knowing how to make the most of that opportunity –with all its crosses, difficulties, and also great spiritual joys– to sanctify oneself and imitate Christ. Because if we do all that we do but do not have charity, what good is it to us?

Look, this is so important that St. Gregory warns: “he who does not have charity toward another must absolutely not exercise the ministry of preaching”8.

A charity that should lead us to be able to say together with St. Paul: Although we could have imposed our authority as apostles of Christ, we were gentle among you, like a mother who tenderly cares for her children9; the love of a father ready to give his life for his children: So, loving you, we wanted to share with you not only the Gospel of God but even our very selves, because you had become very dear to us10”11. “Such is the love that the Lord expects of every preacher of the Gospel, of every builder of the Church,” the Directory of Missions Ad Gentes very aptly points out12.

We do not perform a job like civil servants, we do not climb the ranks; we are called to serve like Christ himself, who became a servant. That is why ours “is not command, nor honors, but total self-giving to the service of God and to pastoral ministry”13. We must be driven by love14, by the theological virtue of charity, which goes beyond natural aims and self-satisfaction; simply because supernatural love is sacrificial, as Ven. Archbishop Fulton Sheen used to say. It is, then, a love that knows how to rise above one’s own tastes, that knows how “to renounce oneself for the good of others”15, that knows how to conform one’s own will to the Will of God and knows how to see in those things which seem bad and adverse to our nature and which give no pleasure, what is best for the soul, because God so orders it, who wills our good and only our good, and because it recognizes that it is always more profitable to go on annihilating oneself16, as St. John of the Cross says. But above all, this supernatural love does not go about seeking consolation or pleasure, either in God or in anything else; rather, all its care is directed to seeing how it can give God some pleasure and serve him in something17.

A sign of this love will be to dedicate oneself without reserve and without looking back to the proclamation of Jesus Christ18. Without high-handedness, without arrogance, without trampling over others, but rather kindly, and always with courage, with enthusiasm and very great fatherly patience; all of it wrapped in serene joy.

Blessed Paolo Manna told his religious, and today I want to tell you here: “what most often opens the way to the faith is not the eloquence and erudition of the Missionary but his charity. A Missionary may be as learned as you like; if he is harsh, cold, dry and reserved, if he does not deign to deal with children, with savages, he will not do much good. The pariah, the poor Chinese, and whatever souls stand before us are better attracted by goodness than by the prestige of authority and of preaching. Even though conversions take place for one of these reasons, it is afterward always the personal goodness of the Missionary that wins the heart and attaches the convert to the faith and to Jesus Christ, whose superhuman image he sees in the missionary”19.

We have to understand that only the charity of Christ will save the world. It is not our programs, it is not our methods, it is not our wisdom; it is only the charity of Christ that will save the world.

In a thousand ways our Proper Law exemplifies and specifies this practice of charity –which, if you agree, it would be good, very good!, to devote yourselves to reading carefully.

Today I would simply like to emphasize two of them: the celebration of the Eucharist (where precisely the source of this supernatural charity we have been speaking of is found) and our charity particularly toward those whom –generally speaking– we will always have at our side: our brother priests.

Regarding the Eucharist: always remember that the principal reason for the ordained priesthood is the celebration of the Eucharist. The priest is in a certain sense “by it and for it”20. If the Eucharist does not become the center and root of your lives, you will never be able to fulfill yourselves fully as priests, and you will go about seeking loves and consolations in empty and cracked cisterns21. No work we carry out as priests is as important as the Mass. Indeed, the celebration of the Eucharist is the best way of serving our brothers and sisters in the world, because it is the source and center of the dynamism of their lives.

How crucial it is, therefore, for our happiness and for a fruitful ministry, that we cultivate a deep love for the Eucharist. Now, during your time in the seminary, through the theological study of the nature of the eucharistic mystery and a good knowledge of the liturgical norms, so as to prepare yourselves well to promote a full, conscious and active participation in the liturgy. Our Proper Law itself says it several times: “the celebration of the Eucharist is of essential importance in the spiritual formation of seminarians”22; and it must be “the center of the life of the Seminary”23, because that is the fountain from which we drink the charity of the Incarnate Word, which is what gives us strength for daily self-giving, now and later in priestly life.

Bear in mind also that the “worthy celebration of Holy Mass”24 is one of the non-negotiable elements attached to our charism. Therefore, you must prepare yourselves as men who are going to be masters of the ars celebrandi, because it is there that one learns to love God more, offering oneself unblemished to God25.

Notice that all “the other sacraments, as well as all ecclesiastical ministries and works of the apostolate, are closely bound up with the Holy Eucharist and are directed toward it. For in the Most Holy Eucharist is contained the whole spiritual good of the Church, namely, Christ himself”26. The Eucharist is like the source and summit of all evangelization, and from it ardent love for souls is nourished. In the Eucharist one understands that every participation in the priesthood of Christ has a universal dimension, and our readiness to go and proclaim him to all nations27 keeps growing wider.

That is why today I also want to make a request of you: “May love for Christ present in the Eucharist grow, thanks to our apostolic work.”

Regarding our charity toward our brother priests: our Proper Law commands this of us when it says that among priests there must be an intimate fraternity, which “must be shown in spontaneous and willing mutual help, spiritual as well as material, pastoral as well as personal…”28. “Our brothers in the presbyterate must also be a privileged object of the priest’s pastoral charity. To help them materially and spiritually, to make confession and spiritual direction delicately easy for them, to make the path of service pleasant for them, to be close to them in every need, to accompany them with fraternal solicitude in any difficulty, in old age, in sickness…”.

2. Teaching – The theological virtue of Faith

The Directory of Major Seminaries says it clearly: “Before the mystery of the ministerial priesthood, intimately united to the mystery of Christ and of the Church, there remains only the response of faith. Without it nothing is understood”29. “Only the man of faith can understand the reality of the priesthood and what pertains to the priesthood”30.

Hence the mystery of the Catholic priesthood can only be discovered with a deeply contemplative attitude, with a contemplation made life, and with the study of theology made contemplation. That is why it is so important that you acquire a great supernatural sense of your existence, that you nourish this faith, strengthen it every day and defend it.

You must be “professionals of the faith; the specialists of God”31, as John Paul the Great called priests. As future priests you must know that you have been called to be teachers of the faith, and therefore you must be able, already now, to give an account of the faith that you will preach and teach. This highlights the importance of study, aimed not only at the acquisition of knowledge, but as a complementary part of our vocation.

Faith must become the “new criterion for judging and evaluating people and things, events and problems”32. Therefore, one must not be content with a number of ideas pinned loosely in one’s head, but must have well-founded truths, by which one lives and for which one is capable of giving one’s life if necessary; one must be well and solidly formed in right thinking. In this one must demand the utmost of oneself; one cannot improvise, one cannot neglect intellectual formation. To do so would be to be negligent in pastoral work and to betray the commitment made to Christ. Because without the discipline and habit of study, the future priest will not be able to be the wise man according to the Gospel who, in season and out of season, exhorts with the word of God, convinces with the truth and frees from error. Souls hope to have solid certainties, words that do not pass away, absolute values. God is the only certainty; He is the Absolute who has manifested himself in the Incarnate Word.

Now pay attention to this: our Proper Law points out that you must be formed in such a way that you “are forewarned especially against the errors of ‘theological progressivism,’ which seeks a false understanding of the faith through a lack of criticism and discernment in the face of modern philosophies and through the assimilation of the principle of immanence”33.

Hence it is imperative that you, our seminarians, make the most of the richness of the formation offered to you, which allows you “to know the mystery of Christ, while at the same time preparing you so that, God willing, you may be able to proclaim the Gospel”34 with complete fidelity. St. John Bosco used to say: “after piety, what must be most recommended is study”35, because every hour of study has repercussions tomorrow in the apostolate. Here we see, then, that dedication to study must always be undertaken with a pastoral perspective, which can never be dissociated from the problems of today’s world. The Magisterium of the Church and our Proper Law itself repeat it to us over and over: it is indispensable for evangelizing the culture36 that “the priest be sensitive to all that happens around him, to the cultural movements of the age, to the currents of thought. Only thus will the problems that concern man be illuminated by Christian revelation, bringing the truth that comes from Jesus Christ”37. This is what we mean, in a few words, by “biting into reality.”

To evangelize means, in fact, to proclaim and spread, by all the honest and suitable means available, the contents of the revealed truths (Trinitarian and Christological faith; the meaning of the dogma of creation; the eschatological truths; the doctrine on the Church, on man; the teaching of faith on the sacraments and the other means of salvation; etc.). And it also means, at the same time, teaching how to translate those truths into concrete life, into witness and missionary commitment38. We must, then, undertake with all enthusiasm and all solicitude the wonderful “work of ‘intellectual charity’ through a permanent and patient catechesis on the fundamental truths of Catholic faith and morals and their influence on the spiritual life”39, in imitation of Christ the Teacher.

St. Gregory affirmed that the priest is especially appointed by God to teach the right way to him who has gone astray; and that is why St. Leo adds that the priest who does not point out to the faithful their straying shows that he himself has gone astray”. From which it follows that the office of teaching, the task of preaching, is unavoidable for the priest. And if this is true for every priest, it is even more so for us as members of the Institute of the Incarnate Word, who are commanded “to be impatient to preach the Word in every form”40. “Faith comes from the message, and the message consists in speaking of Christ, says St. Paul. So you must be fervent and diligent in acquiring a profound knowledge of the Word of God, as it is lived and proclaimed in the Church. And if you want your preaching tomorrow to bear fruit in the lives of those whom you will serve, you must cultivate in mind and heart a real interior adherence to the Magisterium of the Church”41.

Notice that without faith we do not even set out on mission; without faith our strength fails and we want to skimp on our self-giving, or one may even wish to abandon the mission post; without faith we can fall into the temptation of carrying out our offices or the mission itself as earthly business is carried out, that is, with exclusively human outlooks and methods: relying too much on earthly means and on our own skill and energy, setting aside trust in Divine Providence.

On the other hand, he who has faith not only commits himself to going to the most difficult assignments42 but does not complain about doing mission in the most difficult situations and under the most adverse conditions43. What is more, he remains faithful even “without being able to have a tabernacle and an image of Our Lady,” or having to spend some time alone, and even so he has apostolic initiatives! For that, my dear seminarians, faith is needed!

So, if you want to live and remain at the height of your vocation, you must constantly nourish yourselves with this spirit of faith, being enlightened and set on fire by meditation on Scripture and by praying well. We are instruments of God, and from Him we must receive, through continual prayer and the reception of the sacraments, the grace we need for our ministry, without which we can do nothing with regard to the eternal salvation of our souls and of those entrusted to us.

Consequently, “an aspect, certainly not secondary, of the priest’s mission is that of being teachers of prayer”44. From this it follows that an intellectual knowledge of Christ alone is not enough: one must learn from Him! One must bear witness to the faith we profess! Hence the task of becoming familiar with his Divine Person and his plan of salvation is unavoidable; one must be steeped in the very sentiments of Christ, and in some way evangelize oneself by acquiring his criteria of judgment. Jesus Christ must be the center of our thoughts, the theme of our words, and everyone must recognize that He is the reason for our life. And if the Incarnate Word is the object of our love, every initiative must draw its vigor from Him. Each one of us must embrace the ideal of the Incarnate Word as life itself, in such a way that if we were to fall dead, we would die embracing Him. That is why these years of doctrinal preparation must also find nourishment in a solid spiritual life45.

Blessed Paolo Manna has some masterly lines on this, which, if you will allow me, I would like to share with you today. He says: “The missionary is par excellence a man of faith: he is born of faith, he lives by faith, for it he works gladly, suffers gladly, suffers and dies. The Missionary who is not this is, at most, an apprentice of the apostolate”46. And he goes on to say: “the fervor of a Missionary’s life, his controlled, wise, industrious, tireless activity, his unalterable joy and his perseverance in work, even amid privations, calamities and difficulties, are always the result of a life of faith. If faith grows dim, zeal too diminishes in intensity; then, even in the strongest, weariness and depression appear, and one may come even to despair and the loss of one’s vocation. If the Missionary lives by faith, then he is great, he is sublime, he is divine; the Church and souls can hope for everything from him; no labor, no difficulty frightens him, no heroism is beyond his strength; if the spirit of faith in him is languid and weak, he will bustle about, he will nevertheless work, but his toils will profit him little or nothing, and the poor success of his half-hearted works will increase his distrust and depression”47.

I told you so in a circular letter: “Without faith, no one sacrifices himself willingly in the missions. It is through faith, through deep convictions and great love for the Incarnate Word, that the heroic deeds of the Cross are then carried out” 48.

3. Shepherding – The theological virtue of hope

There is a phrase in our Proper Law that I particularly love, because, speaking of us as missionaries, it says: “People are needed who will present the fatherly face of God and the motherly face of the Church, who will stake their lives so that others may have life and hope”49. This phrase condenses, so to speak, what we have been talking about until now, but it also adds that note of eternity: hope. So characteristic of our Lord’s style of shepherding… instilling hope, encouraging, raising people above natural outlooks.

We as missionary priests are sent into a world anguished and oppressed by thousands of problems, but right there, in those circumstances which so often pull toward pessimism, we must give our lives to proclaim the ‘Good News’ and bear witness with our own lives that we have found true hope in Christ50. That is why everything we do, everything we suffer, must be wrapped in that special grace which is the victorious hope that Christ won for us.

An author, in one of his definitions of the priesthood, says that “the priest is a beacon of hope in the midst of so much darkness and storm”51.

You here now in the Seminary are preparing yourselves “specifically to communicate the charity of Christ, the Good Shepherd”52, “who watches over, shepherds and evangelizes; who knows how to suffer in silence and knows how to give his life for his sheep”53. That is the goal of the whole formative process with a view to priestly ordination: to form shepherds of souls54, full communion with the pastoral charity of Jesus Christ55. Or as our Constitutions magnificently say: “to form for the Catholic Church priests according to the heart of Christ”56.

This tells us plainly that “pastoral work cannot be reduced, even in the best of cases, to a competent science or a truly technical practice.” “To be formed for the priesthood is to learn primarily from the pastoral charity of Christ; it is to prepare oneself, out of love for Christ, to shepherd his flock”57. And that is why “the virtues related to others” must be required of you, that is, that ‘they not be arrogant or quarrelsome, but affable, hospitable, sincere in their words and in their heart, prudent and discreet, generous and available for service, capable of personally offering and of fostering in everyone loyal and fraternal relationships, ready to understand, forgive and console”58.

That on the one hand. But on the other hand you must also “take on your responsibilities in a conscious and mature way, form the interior habit of evaluating problems and establishing priorities and the means of solving them, always grounded in clear motivations of faith and according to the theological requirements of pastoral work itself”59. How consoling it is to see a religious who lives moved by pastoral charity as the total gift of himself for the salvation of others60, who has apostolic zeal, who manages things in such a way that no one leaves him without being persuaded that he truly seeks the supernatural good of his soul, who is lavish in pastoral initiatives, and who, amid difficulties, knows how to maintain serene trust in God, which undoubtedly springs from the hope that beats in his soul!

At this point I would like to cite the example of Fr. Ciszek –an American Jesuit priest– because it seems to me that his experience sums up in some way all that I would like to tell you on this point, especially when hope is tested. As a seminarian, Fr. Ciszek volunteered to go to the Russian missions after Pope Pius XI wrote a special letter to the Jesuits telling them that he had opened a seminary to prepare young priests for the mission in Russia. After he was ordained they did not send him to Russia, but to Poland. He was there when the Germans invaded the country in 1939 and during the deportation that followed. Fr. Ciszek, passing himself off as a laborer, went with the refugees in the hope of being able to serve them spiritually. But soon the Soviet secret police discovered him and sent him to prison. He spent 5 years imprisoned in Lubyanka; afterward he was sentenced to 15 years of forced labor in the prison camps of Siberia. They put him to work in outdoor construction in extreme arctic cold, and he also worked in coal mines, poorly clothed, poorly fed and housed in miserable conditions in barracks fenced with barbed wire. He himself bears witness to how men died, especially those who abandoned hope. When the 15 years were over, he went on living in small Siberian towns, because he could not leave Siberia nor even go to the main cities of Russia. So he worked as a mechanic and at other trades of that kind, until the American government exchanged him –in 1963– for two Russian spies.

Regarding his experience in Lubyanka, and after recounting the inhuman situation in which he lived and the injustice of having been imprisoned without reason, and detailing how in prison he was despised precisely for being a priest, he writes –pay attention–: “No situation is without value or meaning in divine providence. It is a very human temptation to feel frustrated by circumstances, to feel overwhelmed and helpless before the established order, whether it be a prison, or the Soviet system as a whole, or this whole oppressive and rotten world! In the worst circumstances imaginable, man is still man, endowed with a free will, and God is always ready to help him with his grace. What is more, God expects him to act in those circumstances, in that situation, as He wants him to act. Because those situations, those people, those places and those things too are what God wills for him at that moment. It may not be in his power to change the ‘system,’ just as it was not in mine to change the conditions of the prison, but that is no excuse for failing to act. Many men feel frustrated, or discouraged, or even defeated when they find themselves facing a situation or an evil against which they can do little. Poverty, social injustice, hatred and resentment, war, corruption and the oppressive bureaucracy of institutions (and each of us could add something to the list): all of it can generate a bitter frustration and, at times, a feeling of utter hopelessness. But God does not expect any man to change the world by himself, to put an end to all evils or cure all diseases. What He does expect of him is that he act as He wants him to in the circumstances arranged by his will and his providence. To act in this way he will not lack the help of divine grace.

The feeling of hopelessness that we all experience in circumstances like these is really born of our tendency to bring too much of our self into the picture. […] We tend to concentrate on ourselves, to think about what we can or cannot do, and we forget God, his will and his providence. God, however, never forgets the importance of each one, his dignity and his worth, and the role He asks us to play in the work of providence. To God, every individual is equally important at every moment. We do matter to Him. But He also expects each of us to accept, as coming from his hands, the daily situations He sends us, and to act as He wants us to act, with the grace He grants us for it”61.

Consider that the man who writes this was deprived of all external support: of his family, of his fellow Jesuits, of the visible Church… not even his basic needs were minimally met. That is the hope we must have, an invincible hope, which neither the most adverse situations nor the most lacerating trials can succeed in bending. “In life there are no difficulties, there are only circumstances. God guides everything, and He guides everything well. There is nothing to do but abandon oneself, and serve at every moment as far as possible”62. That is the hope we must have!

Trust in Jesus even in your discouragements, even when body or spirit are reduced to helplessness and refuse to cooperate. Keep your trust fixed on God, for He looks upon you with ineffable love. It is true that at times God is pleased to multiply difficulties; He halts us and nails us down in helplessness. One would like to, but one cannot. But when one takes a step and prays, God comes and gives wings to keep moving forward63. So: take heart! Jesus does not deceive us. He is God-with-us, the faithful love that does not abandon us and that knows how to transform our nights into dawns of hope. Always trust in Him and distrust yourselves; a grace I wish you now and always.

Much more could be said on this point, but it would make this even longer.

[Conclusion] Simply, as a final thought, I want to leave you a piece of advice from the book “Sacerdotes para siempre”.

“One must begin by purifying oneself before purifying others; one must be instructed in order to be able to instruct; one must become light in order to illuminate, draw near to God in order to draw others near to Him, become holy in order to sanctify”64. This takes concrete form in the pursuit of a deep unity of life, which leads the priest –the seminarian, we could say here– to strive to be, to live and to serve as another Christ in all the circumstances of life. The faithful of the parish, or those who take part in the various pastoral activities, see –they observe!– and hear –they listen!– not only when the Word of God is preached, but also when the various liturgical acts are celebrated, in particular Holy Mass; when they are received in the parish office, where they hope to be attended to with cordiality and kindness;65 when they see the priest eating or resting, and are edified by his example of sobriety and temperance; when they go to look for him at his house, and rejoice at the priestly simplicity and poverty in which he lives;66 when they see him neatly wearing his own habit, when they talk with him, even about unimportant things, and feel comforted on finding his supernatural outlook, his tact and the human refinement with which he treats even the humblest people, with authentic priestly nobility. ‘The grace and charity of the altar thus spread to the ambo, to the confessional, to the parish archive, to the school, to youth activities, to homes and to the streets, to hospitals, to means of transport and to the media of social communication, wherever the priest has the opportunity to carry out his task as shepherd: in every case it is his Mass that extends itself, it is his spiritual union with Christ, Priest and Victim, that leads him to be –as St. Ignatius of Antioch said– the wheat of God, that he may be found the pure bread of Christ,67 for the good of his brothers68.

It only remains for me to encourage you in this race to reach Christ. Realize that He has already reached you first. Let yourselves be formed by Him. Love without reserve the Church, our beloved Institute and our Most Holy Mother. May She, the Mother of Christ the Priest, educate you with her maternal love, so that in you may be formed the true image of her Son. To the most pure Virgin I commend each one of you very specially, individually, with all your needs, with all your longings. May Our Lady always keep you. I wish you this with all my affection.

Thank you very much for your attention. May God bless you and make you holy missionary priests. Truly, thank you.

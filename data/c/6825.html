[Introduction]

“We must carry the Incarnate Word firmly planted in our heart, in our soul and in our intellect. We must be intimate with the Crucified. Without this we will have doctors, theologians and philosophers who overwhelm with their learning, but missionaries will disappear. That is why, paraphrasing St. Peter Julian Eymard, we could say: ‘I would not understand a religious of the Incarnate Word wanting to excel in any science other than the Incarnate Word. Without this, we are not in the fullness of our grace’”1.

St. Ignatius proposes to us, as we were saying this morning, to assimilate the doctrine of the Incarnate Word, to imitate his examples and to be united with his Person. Because ours is not merely adherence to a doctrine, but intimate communion with his adorable Person in order to follow him wherever he goes; that is why he said to us in the previous meditation: “whoever wishes to come with me”2.

Therefore, “the essence of prayer during this week must be to try to know Jesus Christ intimately and to love him with all the strength of our soul, all of this accompanied by a serious reflection on ourselves in order to make our own what we contemplate in Him”3.

Note that we say “interior knowledge,” which means penetrating, as far as the grace of God allows us, to the very depths of his thoughts, affections and sentiments. St. John of the Cross affirmed in this regard: “however many mysteries and wonders the holy doctors have discovered and holy souls have understood in this state of life, the greater part still remained for them to say, and even to understand; and so there is much to delve into in Christ: for he is like an abundant mine with many recesses of treasures, of which, however deep they dig, they never find the end or limit; rather, in each recess they go on finding new veins of new riches here and there”4.

So, having made this observation, let us now contemplate the august mystery of the Incarnation of the Word, the supreme work of God. Which, in the words of the Mystical Doctor, is an even greater work than creation, since what God “most displayed himself in and most attended to were the [works] of the Incarnation of the Word and the mysteries of the Christian faith, in comparison with which all the others were done as if in passing, in haste”5.

This first contemplation is found in numbers [101-109.262] of the book of the Exercises. In addition to the preparatory prayer it has three preludes, three points and a colloquy.

The preparatory prayer is the usual one:

[46] Prayer. The preparatory prayer is to ask grace of God our Lord, that all my intentions, actions and operations may be ordered purely to the service and praise of his divine majesty.

[102] 1st prelude. The first prelude is to call to mind the history of the thing I am to contemplate; which here is how the three divine persons looked upon the whole plain or circuit of all the world, full of men, and how, seeing that all were going down to hell, it is determined in their eternity that the second person should become man, to save the human race, and so, the fullness of time having come, they send the angel St. Gabriel to our Lady, no. [262].

[103] 2nd prelude. The 2nd: composition, seeing the place: here it will be to see the great capacity and circuit of the world, in which there are so many and such diverse peoples; likewise, afterwards, in particular the house and rooms of our Lady, in the city of Nazareth, in the province of Galilee.

[104] 3rd prelude. The 3rd: to ask for what I want: here it will be to ask for interior knowledge of the Lord, who for me has become man, that I may love him more and follow him.

And this asking for what I want declares in turn the purpose of the meditation: to internalize, to assimilate, to adhere with all the strength of body and soul to the Most Sweet Person of the Incarnate Word.

1. Kenosis

[106] 1st point. The first point is to see the persons, the ones and the others; and first those on the face of the earth, in such diversity, both in dress and in behavior: some white and others black, some at peace and others at war, some weeping and others laughing, some healthy, others sick, some being born and others dying, etc. 2nd: to see and consider the three divine persons, as on the royal seat or throne of their divine majesty, how they look upon the whole face and circuit of the earth and all the peoples in such blindness, and how they die and go down to hell. 3rd: to see our Lady and the angel who greets her, and to reflect in order to draw profit from such a sight.

[107] 2nd point. The 2nd: to hear what the persons on the face of the earth are saying, that is, how they speak with one another, how they swear and blaspheme, etc.; likewise what the divine persons say, that is: “Let us work the redemption of the human race,” etc.; and then what the angel and our Lady say; and afterwards to reflect, in order to draw profit from their words.

In the Directory of Spirituality we read: “We could say that our spirituality must be that of the hymn of the Kenosis”6, referring to the text of Philippians 2:6-7: He, though he was of divine condition, did not regard this equality with God as something to be jealously guarded: on the contrary, he emptied himself, taking the condition of a servant and becoming like men. This emptying of himself is what in theology is called “Kenosis.”

And it is even more challenging to read elsewhere in our Proper Law that what is properly ours is “to imitate Christ in the self-emptying of his incarnation”7. That is why our point of focus here will be to try to understand this text of the Kenosis in relation to our being priest-victims, called as we are to replicate in our lives the example of the Incarnate Word and to prolong the Incarnation8.

That is why I would like to dwell on that point mentioned by St. Ignatius, “to see and consider the three divine persons, as on the royal seat or throne of their divine majesty, how they look upon the whole face and circuit of the earth and all the peoples in such blindness, and how they die and go down to hell,” for this is what moves them to decree the Incarnation, saying: “Let us work the redemption of the human race.”

Some use the example of toads to explain the self-emptying of the Word in the Incarnation. Fulton Sheen uses that of dogs. The idea is the same. I am going to use Fulton Sheen's, above all because it seems to me more challenging, seeing as we do in today's society the ‘cult and veneration’ that some people have toward canines.

Sheen says9: suppose that we are tired of seeing how dogs are mistreated, that we have seen how dogs refuse to obey their owners, how they tore a piece out of children's trousers, etc. Then you, out of love for dogs, decide to give up your body—without losing your intellect and will—in order to assume, with your human soul, a canine body. It is as if we said: you placed in the body of a dog in order to be an example to dogs. The humiliation is twofold.

First, by assuming the body of a dog, you have decided to confine yourselves to the limits of a canine body. You could speak, but you only bark. You could reason and count the stars, but you are guided only by instinct and have your snout fixed on the ground.

The second humiliation will be that you will have to spend the rest of your life with dogs, knowing that you are a million times better than dogs. You will always have to run with them. The futility of your life among dogs will be incredibly degrading. And in the end, they will turn against you and tear you to pieces.

And one would think that the humility of such a man is very great, for his spirit of poverty, of sacrifice, of service, of love for dogs, by the fact of having become canine.

“However, that self-emptying would be within the order of creatures, within finitude; it would be the passage from a superior creature to an inferior creature. This is not, even remotely, the case of the Incarnation, in which the self-emptying is that of an infinite God who assumes a finite nature. This is abysmal. It is an infinite emptying of self and an infinite self-emptying. We are not in the same order as in the hypothesis we were speaking of; rather, in this reality, there is a lowering from the order of the Creator to the order of the creature. Therefore, abysmal too is the example of the virtues that Jesus Christ teaches us by his Incarnation: abysmal is his humility, his poverty, his obedience, his sacrifice, his patience, his sorrow, his service and his love for man. It makes one shudder to think of it! From transcendent to immanent, from powerful to weak, from majestic to humble, from immortal to mortal, from infinite to finite, from most pure Spirit to material flesh, from eternal to temporal, from impassible to passible, from immense to small, from unlimited to limited, from all-powerful to servant, from rich to poor, from Lord to slave, from King to subject, ‘from eternal life to temporal death’10. And all this, without ceasing to be God! It is an abysmal self-emptying!”11.

Nevertheless, this example of dogs (or of toads, if you prefer) helps us at least minimally to understand what it means that God became a servant and lived with servant (slave) men. But what is more important, it helps us to perceive, at least from afar, the magnificence and infinite mercy of God, who did not return to Heaven until he had made his proclamation to men of freedom from the slavery of sin.

Now, notice that we have been called to be other Christs12, to imitate—as we were saying earlier—Christ in the self-emptying of his incarnation”13. Therefore, it is fitting to ask: What are the implications of the Kenosis of the Son of God for us who are called to be priest-victims?

  • As priest, it will imply emptying oneself in order to be filled with holiness;
  • As victim, it will imply that one empties oneself in the service of others.

This Kenosis in a priest of the Incarnate Word is manifested specifically in two resolutions:

  • Humility: because the measure of the increase of the Spirit of Christ in the soul is in direct relation to the decrease of self-love, of one's own judgment, of attachments, etc.14
  • The other is emptying oneself, which also comes in direct proportion to compassion toward others. The less emphasis there is on the ego, the more care, the more attention there is toward one's neighbor.

This is expressed very well in these words: “The priest, like Christ … is a man who possesses nothing, because he possesses the One who is everything and is possessed by the One who emptied himself, who made himself nothing”15.

The more the religious, the priest, empties himself of his own plans in order to carry out God's plan, of his comfort in order to sacrifice himself for Christ, when he empties himself of that excessive desire to be appreciated, to be praised, to be taken into account, to be consulted, to be preferred to others, the less he is bound hand and foot by anxiety. As the humiliation of Christ becomes his model, then even the very sufferings he endures become part of the interior joy and of the service he offers outwardly.

2. Look at Him also made man16

[108] 3rd point. The 3rd: afterwards to look at what the persons on the face of the earth are doing, such as wounding, killing, going to hell, etc.; likewise what the divine persons are doing, that is, working the most holy incarnation, etc.; and likewise what the angel and our Lady are doing, that is, the angel carrying out his office of envoy, and our Lady humbling herself and giving thanks to the divine majesty; and afterwards to reflect in order to draw some profit from each of these things.

Our Constitutions have in number 7 this marvelous paragraph: “We want to be founded on Jesus Christ, who has come in the flesh (1 Jn 4:2), and on Christ alone, and Christ always, and Christ in everything, and Christ in everyone, and Christ All.” These very brief lines of our Proper Law, which refer so explicitly to the mystery of the Incarnation, have an immense depth and would suffice to fill hours of contemplation with great fruit for our spiritual and priestly life.

St. John of the Cross has a chapter in his book of the Ascent in which he speaks precisely of Christ as the All of the heavenly Father.

Thus the saint says that “in giving us, as he did, his Son, who is his one Word—for he has no other—[the Father] spoke everything to us together and at once in this one Word, and he has nothing more to say”17.

“What God formerly spoke to our fathers through the prophets in many ways and in many manners, now at last, in these days, he has spoken to us in the Son all at once. By which the Apostle gives us to understand that God has become as it were mute and has nothing more to say, because what he spoke before in parts to the prophets he has now spoken in the whole, giving us the All, who is his Son”18.

Christ is the definitive Word, the only and the best Word of the Father. A present, a gift that has been given to us. This being so, says the Mystical Doctor, “anyone who would now wish to question God, or desire some vision or revelation, would not only be acting foolishly, but would be offending God, by not fixing his eyes entirely on Christ, without wanting any other thing or novelty”19. By which he is already telling us that precisely what we must do is fix our eyes firmly on the Incarnate Word and realize the Treasure that we have in Him.

Then he places the following on the lips of the Heavenly Father: “If I have already spoken all things to you in my Word, who is my Son, and I have no other, what can I now answer or reveal to you that would be more than this? Fix your eyes on him alone, because in him I have spoken and revealed everything to you, and you will find in him even more than you ask for and desire”20. Here the saint is speaking specifically of those who ask for revelations and locutions, but it can well be applied to all the other created things that we ask for daily: more pleasant circumstances for the apostolate, better health, a better relationship with the bishop, more financial means, less spiritual dryness and more consolations, a good office, less criticism from people and more acceptance… each one can make his own list. Whatever the case, he answers us all: “he is my whole locution and answer, and he is my whole vision and my whole revelation. Which I have already spoken, answered, manifested and revealed to you, giving him to you as brother, companion and master, ransom and reward”21. I cannot now, but how salutary it is for the soul to think of the Incarnate Word as our brother, as our companion, as our master, as our ransom and as our reward… And the Heavenly Father goes on to say: “If [still] you would have me answer you some word of consolation, look at my Son, subject to me and bound by my love, and afflicted, and you will see how many he answers you. […] look at him also made man, and you will find in that more than you think […] [Because] in Christ dwells bodily all the fullness of the divinity”22.

Look at Him also made man, look at him emptied of himself, look at him suffering the same sorrows, and even greater ones, than those you are going through, look at him forgotten, look at his Heart saturated with ingratitude, look at him with his arms outstretched to embrace you… look at Him also made man, because in Him dwells the fullness of the divinity, that is, all rest, all goodness, all sweetness, all and the only thing that gives true satisfaction to the soul, even if everything else is lacking.

I repeat here once again the instruction St. Ignatius gives us: to look at what the divine persons do in working the Incarnation.

3. Continuation of the Incarnation

“The life and ministry of the priest are a continuation of the life and action of Christ himself. This is our identity, our true dignity, the source of our joy, the certainty of our life”23.

Each one of us, by being a member of the Institute and by being a priest, is called to “prolong the Incarnation.” That is our specific end24, our apostolate25, our charism26, our vocation.

For this we must first prolong the Incarnation in ourselves. The Virgin Mother gave God a human nature through which he taught, governed and sanctified. Each time we genuinely repeat “Behold the slave of the Lord, be it done to me according to your word”27, we give Christ our human nature so that He may analogously use our priesthood to continue his teaching, his governing, his sanctifying work.

The mission of Christ is the model of the mission of each of the priests of the Institute, according to what our Constitutions say: “We want to dedicate ourselves to the works of the apostolate, imitating Christ”28.

Therefore, each one must preach Christ, and the whole Christ, “without succumbing to the danger of attenuating or diminishing the truth”29. People are languishing to hear about Christ; they do not want to hear about strikes, about soccer matches, still less about political parties, nor about the priest's personal opinions; they want to hear about Christ and what the Church teaches, they want to learn the concrete application of those teachings to their lives.

On the other hand, the priest is also called to exercise the authority of Christ so that people are not confused. Many today leave the Church not so much because of the scandal of the Cross but because of the disparity of teachings and the mixing of religion with ideologies30.

Finally, as continuers of the priesthood of the Incarnate Word we must also be victims. “If we do not learn to be victims with the Victim, all our sufferings are useless”31. Let us fix firmly in our mind and soul that the ministerial priesthood is a sacrificial priesthood.

And in the same way that the Incarnate Word immolated himself for the salvation of souls, in the same way we must offer ourselves with Him as victims.

[Peroratio]

All that we are now going to contemplate must also move us to see the immeasurable richness of our charism, of our spirituality, precisely because it is of the Incarnate Word!

Other congregations will shine for their renown, for their great influence, for the magnificence of their works. We want only—and this has always been our declared intention—to stand out by imitating the Incarnate Word in his kenosis32, by being configured to his style33, by passing through this world doing good34 and bearing witness to the Truth35. That is why our passion must be “to ‘assume’ cultures, purify them and elevate them starting from Christ and his Gospel, understood ‘in the Church’36”37. This is and has always been our intention, our objective. Even when they contradict us or persecute us, even in the midst of the harshest poverty and in spite of our great miseries. Because our faith and our trust are fixed on Him who said: Everyone who acknowledges me before men, I also will acknowledge before my heavenly Father38; yes, in the world you will have tribulations, but do not be afraid: I have overcome the world39 I will be with you always, until the end of the world40.

The “right faith in the Incarnate Word”41 must grow in us. Without it, as we said at the beginning, we are not in the fullness of our grace. Because “the Incarnation is the first and fundamental mystery of Jesus Christ, which illuminates the whole path that, as religious, we want to travel in the Church.” That is why only from that “milestone”42 must we “launch out boldly to restore all things in Christ”43.

[Colloquies]

As a final colloquy St. Ignatius suggests:

[109] Colloquy. Finally, a colloquy is to be made, thinking what I ought to say to the three divine persons, or to the eternal Word incarnate, or to our Mother and Lady, asking according to what I feel within myself, so as better to follow and imitate our Lord, thus newly incarnate, saying an Our Father.

[Examination of the meditation]

As we always say, once the hour of contemplation is over, the examination should be made.

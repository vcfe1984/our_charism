We want to be ‘as another humanity [of the Word]’1

Good Night talk of October 5, 2017
at the Major Seminary in Argentina

Dear Fathers, Brothers, dearest seminarians:

Our Constitutions, in their general principles, already state in number 7 that we “want to be ‘as another humanity [of the Word],’ and we want to be chalices full of Christ that pour out his superabundance upon others, showing with our lives that Christ lives.”

This wanting to be ‘as another humanity of the Word’ carries within itself an implicit call to the practice of apparently opposite virtues; for example, our Proper Law lists: justice and love, firmness and gentleness, fortitude and meekness, a holy anger and patience, purity and at the same time great affection, magnanimity and humility, prudence and courage, joy and penance1, “poverty and generous self-giving”2, etc.

In this good night talk it seemed to me that it might be profitable for everyone to dwell on two of them: magnanimity and generosity –not as abstract virtues or virtues isolated within our religious life– but in relation to the missionary zeal that must characterize every religious of the Incarnate Word throughout his whole life. Because without them, I find it very difficult for one not to shy away from the missionary adventure, as we once promised.

Already in Christ’s very missionary mandate to his apostles, Go and teach all nations (Mt 28:19), throughout the whole world (Mk 16:15), there is a certain reference to these two virtues:

  • go and teach, with all the effort that this implies, with all the renunciation that it entails –leaving one’s family, one’s own country, one’s own culture, one’s language…– with all the preparation it requires, and this without time limits, with generous self-giving day after day. In the words of our Directory of Spirituality, with a self-giving “without diminutions or retractions, without reservations or conditions, without subterfuges or delays, without retreats or sluggishness”3. In a word: with generosity.
  • Also, on the other hand, Christ tells them to go and teach all nations and throughout the whole world, without exclusivisms and without exclusions4, without geographical limits, warning them against the temptation of “falling into particularisms, reductionisms, partialities or one-sidedness that would undermine catholicity”5. Rather to all men and everywhere, like this: with great spirit, with magnanimity. As the Incarnate Word Himself did and commanded us to do!: I have given you an example, so that you may do as I have done to you6, says our Lord.

That is why our Proper Law emphatically invites us to “commit our whole life to manifesting Christ to the world, so that he may be acknowledged not only by the poor but also by the rich, not only by the ignorant but also by the wise, not only by those of our homeland but also by those of other peoples”7. And this, even though at times we may have to sow in tears8.

Our dear Institute has been formed “to inculturate the Gospel in the diversity of all cultures, to prolong the Incarnation of the Word ‘in every man, in the whole man and in all the manifestations of man,’ assuming all that is authentically human, and so to carry out with greater perfection the service of God and of men,” as our formula of profession explicitly says9.

This is the ideal that must beat in the heart of each one of our religious. We must not settle for less!

To be a religious of the Incarnate Word “is an invitation to accomplish great works, extraordinary undertakings”10; “it is to take seriously, thoroughly, the demands of the Gospel: go, sell all that you have… (Mt 19:21)”11, and to do so with the “zeal of the saints and martyrs, who gave everything for God”12.

To be a religious of the Incarnate Word is –as our Proper Law says– “to dispose oneself to die, like the grain of wheat, in order to see Christ in all things”13. That is why being a religious of the Incarnate Word brings with it the missionary vocation –whether lived in the cloister or in mission lands– and therefore one must be ready to go to any point on earth where the preaching of the Gospel and the celebration of the Eucharist are needed. “Therefore, we make our own the ardent words of St. Luigi Orione –the Directory of Spirituality goes on to say–: ‘Whoever does not want to be an apostle, let him leave the Congregation: today, whoever is not an apostle of Jesus Christ and of the Church is an apostate’”14. Simply because the glory of God and the salvation of souls are at stake.

And for that we must begin right now! Not wait for the one next to us to begin. Not wait until we make perpetual vows, but now! Each one has to strive to attain a “martyrial disposition.” Each one has to begin to live and vibrate in this spirit from the moment he sets foot in our houses. Because that must be the spirit breathed in each one of them. Each one has to raise high the standard of the Incarnate Word and be ready to do “heroic, even epic things for Christ and his Church”15. Beginning right now:

As regards study, not being content with a number of ideas held together with pins, but rather having a firm knowledge of philosophy and theology that makes you capable of understanding in all its depth the drama of contemporary atheism and therefore capable of offering effective solutions16.

As regards the spiritual life, being ready to pass through the active and passive purifications of sense and of spirit17. Being men of a manly discipline18. And as St. John of the Cross says: “to want this Christ to cost us something”19.

In pastoral matters: “in the likeness of the Incarnate and crucified Word we must have a ‘thirst for souls’”20. And spend and be spent, and want to persevere in this way to the end, as we have always been taught so much.

And in everything, to move with an intrepid faith, a faith that spares no effort to bring the Gospel of Jesus Christ to as many people as it can. Without asking for anything more. That is incisive, enthusiastic pastoral work; that is ‘to bite into reality.’

There is a whole world to be remade in Christ. Two thirds of the world have not yet heard of Christ, and there are others who know Him so little!

That is why that paragraph of our Constitutions where we read the charism is so beautiful, so inspiring; it says: “By the proper charism of the Institute, all its members must work, in supreme docility to the Holy Spirit and within the imprint of Mary, in order to make all that is authentically human the domain of Jesus Christ, even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”21.

To call oneself a ‘religious of the Incarnate Word’ and to “back down” (to retreat) in the face of difficulties, trials, obstacles; to abandon what one has undertaken because of the ‘contradictions of the good’; to be afraid to take on apostolates because of the greatness of the work or out of fear of sacrifice, and so many other human limits that we sometimes set for ourselves, is to go back on one’s word; it is a contradiction. Because, as we have just said: God thought of us so that we all might work even in the most difficult situations and in the most adverse conditions”22. There, in those situations, is where the priestly mettle23 that must be forged right now is shown.

Difficulties, the cross, the cruelest persecutions, loneliness… are part of the program. None of that can diminish or dim the missionary impulse that must be ever more alive in our souls. Because to be a missionary is precisely to be totally ready “to let oneself be sacrificed joyfully in order to make Jesus Christ known, loved and served among the peoples of the unbelievers”24, said that great formator of missionaries, Blessed Paolo Manna.

From our being religious and from our very charism ‘of the Incarnate Word’ follows the incompatibility of mediocrity with our spirit. “The mediocre man shelters under a false balance, considers himself ‘middle of the road’; all magnanimity seems to him pride, all heroism seems to him extremism, all generosity seems to him exaggeration; he makes everything mediocre: whether retreats, camps, popular missions, catechesis, his own spiritual life, etc.”25

Pettiness has nothing to do with our spirit: like that of the one who sows so stingily, “doing as little as possible with the excuse of not falling into activism, or because the times are bad, or because the family does not form as it used to, or because of the unhealthy action of the media…, and who only knows how to complain: ‘Nothing can be done here’”26. Or like that of the “localist, who is concerned only with the interests of his parish, who lives wrapped up in his little work, as if the Church were exhausted in his parish, or in his city, province or country”27.

If a seminarian, if a priest, if a brother or novice ‘settles in,’ that is, builds his nest in things that are not God (his comfort, how well he does in this or that office, the experience accumulated in this or that apostolate, all the people he moves because ‘he is in charge,’ and so many other things we could mention), that religious stops vibrating for the ideal of dying in order to see Christ in all things: such a one seeks himself. If a religious does not fervently seek to advance in the spiritual life, if he settles for the least instead of the most, he is a religious –if the name even fits him– “who shies away from the missionary adventure.” That is, he is not moved by the same criteria as Christ -whose concern is to save men-; he does not go in search of the sheep; it seems to him that the minimum he does is enough; he perhaps hides behind his age, the lack of time or of means… and so he ends up neglecting the souls entrusted to him, imposing ridiculous bureaucratic obstacles on them, or blaming how bad the times are, etc.…

Dear all: to be a missionary religious of the Incarnate Word “means all that is most noble, most perfect and heroic in the imitation of Our Lord; consequently, everything that opposes this ideal does harm and wounds the spirit”28.

That is why we must not only live the life of Christ ourselves, seeking God in all things, but also spread the life of Christ in others29.

This is, and this has always been, the spirit of our dear Institute: all generosity, zeal and charity. That is why may God preserve us from this spirit being lowered or diminished through our laziness or pettiness. May nothing in us obscure, even in the slightest, the brilliant and wholly evangelical ideal of the missionary life, as the Institute understands and carries it out, and which, together with our own personal sanctification, constitutes our whole purpose.

To conclude, I would like to read you a good night talk that St. Luigi Orione gave to his sons and which I believe applies very well to our case. It is entitled: “The dynamite of Charity,” and it goes like this:

“There are the Benedictine religious, who have their purpose; the Franciscans with theirs, the Dominicans; there are the Jesuits with their very special purpose. And we too have our exclusive purpose (…) Our nature, a characteristic that must distinguish us from all the other Congregations.

And if you ask me what that characteristic is that must distinguish us from all the other Congregations, I tell you that it is THE DYNAMITE OF CHARITY. In charity toward the humblest and most abandoned of our brothers we must be dynamic and not marmots. (…)

Our Congregation must be dynamic, and not necessarily numerous. I never dreamed of a numerous Congregation. When St. Aloysius Gonzaga learned that the Jesuits had reached 20,000, he began to weep (…) Quantity is not what matters. It is not, it is not at all. During the First World War, when almost all of ours were under arms, we did not close a single house. We multiplied ourselves by seven and the Lord assisted us spiritually and materially. We were like four poor walnuts in a drawer…

When there are many, little or nothing gets done: little or nothing. When there are many, time is wasted, there is criticism, grumbling, and one always ends up doing something unbecoming to the religious spirit. (…)

Do not drag along, or go in tow. Now in Italy they have imposed the Roman step: and our step, what will it be? Everyone must get it well into his head that we will march at an apostolic pace. Not only a Christian pace, but an apostolic pace. Whoever does not feel the force of charity, its flaming force, the force of apostolicity, should have stayed at home, in his village, not with us. Perhaps he could be a holy Trappist… But whoever remains here must be a true agitator of charity.

Whoever does not have this strong will to go all the way, in love of God and of neighbor, let him leave: we can be good friends, but there is no need for us to be many. Few! Few! So that it may not have to be said: «Multiplicasti gentem et non magnificasti laetitiam» (You multiplied the nation and did not increase the joy – according to the Vulgate, Is 9:3).

(…) Ours must not be a congregation of slackers, or worse still, of effeminate men; it must be a manly and strong congregation, not an invertebrate one; to such a point that, if one day a persecution were to break out (…), a bloody persecution, our Congregation should fall entire, like the Theban legion, and die as martyrs. That is how the seed of Christians is multiplied: «sanguis martyrum semen est christianorum». Exactly so!

Our Congregation has to be prepared for the hardest trials; in defense of the faith and of the Church, of the Pope, and also of the Fatherland; because we must never separate the sacred loves of faith and fatherland.

But if we do not form ourselves, if Jesus Christ is not in us, if our breast is not on fire with love of God, we will never be prepared for such a great undertaking”30.

To be a religious of the Incarnate Word is “to immolate each day our whole person and all our activity to honor his Blood with our blood”31, as St. Gregory Nazianzen said. Only thus can we be the chalices that pour out his superabundance upon others.

With the hymn to Our Lady of Sorrows we ask her to preserve in us, and to make grow mightily in our souls with waves of charity, apostolic fervor and the joy of evangelizing32.

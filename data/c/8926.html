Liturgical prayer

We now pass to the liturgical prayer of a priest of the Incarnate Word.

Our friend Fulton Sheen writes with great realism in his autobiography: “Few priests like vocal prayer. That is a fact. This is not because priests are not men of prayer. It is because their prayers are sighs, aspirations and inspirations. […] They have few petitions. It is rare that they make a novena for something they need; they ask the people to make it. […] And few want to admit that they are bored with something they are supposed to like. The breviary belongs to that category. Priests are expected to rave with love for the breviary, but many of us are like those people who, to keep up appearances, go to an opera but neither enjoy it nor understand it. […] Perhaps the breviary was supposed to be difficult for any average priest. Could it not be like the wrestling between Jacob and God? If we see it from this point of view, it may still be a struggle, but it would fall into the category of prolonged and unceasing intercession. Because we would be praying it as our Lord prayed in the garden of olives, like the friend who kept knocking at the door in the night asking for a piece of bread, like the widow who did not cease her pleas before the judge. Importuning is not relaxing in a daydream, but sustained work”1.

The following is prescribed for us:

“One must also give all the importance it has to the pious and devout praying of the Liturgy of the Hours, formerly known as the Divine Office or Breviary. To this end it will help, among other things, to study Laudis Canticum2. Also its importance as preparation for and prolongation of Holy Mass3.

Ordinarily the major hours of the Office should be prayed in community”4.

It is important to highlight the matter of pious and devout praying, which is in no way hurried or unaware, like one who, instead of reading, ‘scans’ the page and does not think about what he is saying. It must be a moment of true prayer, of asking God, of praising him with the very words with which he wants us to ask him and praise him. They are words of the Holy Spirit! In such a way that, together with Eucharistic adoration, the praying of the Liturgy of the Hours may help to cultivate and maintain throughout the whole day the spirit of prayer we spoke of at the beginning. The praying of the breviary must truly be “a source of piety and nourishment for personal prayer”5.

“The Liturgy of the Hours is the sanctification of the day,” says St. Paul VI's Laudis Canticum. That is why it is important that, before or after Mass, it be the first thing we do, that we make sure of it.

Blessed Joseph Allamano told his own: “Let us observe the indications of the Church regarding the time for praying the Liturgy of the Hours. Having a lot of work must not lead us to postpone it. Prayed in good time, it is a sweet burden. As for the place, if possible, pray it in the church, which is the house destined for prayer. The Liturgy of the Hours is the most excellent prayer after the Mass. May praising God be one of our principal occupations, as it will be for all eternity!6

That must be a practical resolution we must make. To set ourselves a schedule for praying the hours and keep it strictly. I also think it helps to have a ‘plan B’ in case of travel, or of some particular circumstance that affects the original plan. But it should always be one of the first things we do in the day.

Under another heading we must also say that “the Liturgy of the Hours, like the other liturgical actions, is not a private action, but belongs to the whole body of the Church, manifests it and has an effect upon it”7. So that each time we take up the breviary to pray we take with us the billions of unbelievers there are in this world, the persecuted churches, the most distant missions, the thousands of Catholics in the world who do not regularly have a priest in their villages, the thousands of priests in the world who carry out their ministry “undercovered”, etc. When St. John of the Cross was imprisoned in Toledo, in that tiny cell with a minimal little window through which very little light came in, they say that in order to pray his breviary he would climb up on a little stool to be able to see. How many priests like that there must be in the world…

We advance in holiness with the breviary. Sometimes we expect too much from the breviary, but once we realize that we are not parrots repeating words, but that our prayer is the same song the angels sing in heaven before the throne of God in the name of the whole Mystical Body of Christ and of the world, then it becomes easier. Above all for those who pray in a language other than their mother tongue, one must know that perhaps we do not understand everything we are saying, but God does understand it.

A moment ago we were saying that the words of the psalms are the Holy Spirit's. Well then, it is the same Holy Spirit who can make our recitation of the Liturgy of the Hours something fruitful.

Some practical recommendations for praying the breviary8:

  1. Pray it, as far as possible, before the Blessed Sacrament. A practice which, moreover, gains us a plenary indulgence.
  2. Notice that most of the psalms confront us with two figures: the Sufferer and the King. That helps us interpret the psalms better.
  3. Have recourse to the Holy Spirit during the recitation of the breviary. Not necessarily with a formal prayer, but as a gesture asking for help to do it well, with understanding, unhurriedly.
  4. Offer certain hours of the liturgy for certain intentions.
  5. Realize, if we have not done so yet, that one cannot properly pray the breviary with the computer in front of one and the email inbox open, or with music on, and reading WhatsApp messages at the same time.

Finally, let us say that the breviary is not a yoke and a burden; rather it is an obligation, but an obligation of love. If the priest is selfish, the praying of the liturgy becomes only an obligation; if the priest is a person aware that it is the prayer of the Church, that obligation brings love contained within it; if the priest is a victim, love turns that obligation into such ardor that he feels no obligation at all.

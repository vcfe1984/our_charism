The religious has two mothers: one in the flesh, the other in the spirit; one on earth, and one in heaven.

Much more is known about the first; much more has been written about the second.

Our Lady, Mirror of holiness

The life of Our Lady must always be for us a mirror in which to look at ourselves, in such a way that her actions too may be our example; the acts and deeds of Our Lady must help us to make our life an image of her life; it is she whom we must imitate.

Just as Mary’s spiritual motherhood was not a privilege separate from her humanity, neither will it be a privilege of the religious to dispense with zeal for souls. Mary, going in haste over the hill country at the Visitation, revealed how she, the handmaid of the Lord, became the handmaid of Elizabeth. She is now the example for the religious that Christ within him must bring about dedication to souls and to all those who need us. Like Our Lady, we must know how to hurry; nothing demands such speed as the need of others. Just as Mary’s visit sanctified souls, so too through our prayers we must sanctify all those who

need it .

The religious feels a deep love for Mary, not only in his best moments, but in his failures; he trusts in her intercession to combat his weaknesses. So he always turns to her with special attention, knowing that the child who falls most often will be the one who gets the most kisses from his mother.

In the loss and finding of Jesus in the Temple we must take an example of knowing how to rise up truly from our weaknesses; she too lost Christ. Mary and the weak religious suffer together, but in different ways. She felt the darkness of losing God when the Child Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem without her knowing it. It was at this moment that Mary became the Refuge of Sinners. She understood what sin was, since, being a creature, she experientially lost the Creator. She lost the Son only in the mystical darkness of the soul, whereas the religious who falls feels the moral blackness of an ungrateful heart; but Mary found the Child.

At the feast of the Wedding at Cana, Mary teaches the religious how much he belongs to the Church, and how little to himself. At this time during the feast she is called “Mother of Jesus.” At the end, however, she becomes “woman.” What happened here is like what happened when Christ was lost

for three days. Mary had said then, “your father and I”, and our Lord had immediately reminded her of His heavenly Father, thinking of the mystery of the Annunciation and the fact that Joseph

was only His putative father.

From that moment Joseph disappears from Sacred Scripture: nothing more is heard of him. At Cana the Mother of Jesus asks her Son for a manifestation of His messianic power and of His Divinity: Our Lord tells her that the moment He works a miracle and begins His public life, He is going toward His hour, toward the cross. Once the water is turned into wine before the divine gaze, she becomes woman. Just as Joseph disappears in the Temple, so Mary as the mother of Jesus now disappears in Sacred Scripture to become the mother of all those whom He will redeem.

She never speaks again in Sacred Scripture; she has uttered her last words, with a beautiful farewell: Whatever He tells you, do it.

Universal Mother

She is the universal mother, the woman with more seed than the sands of the sea.

In the Passion Our Lady teaches us religious compassion: the saints who were least indulgent with themselves are those who were most indulgent with others. That is why the religious who leads an easy life, without mortifications, cannot speak the language of those who suffer. So to speak, the one who stands upright above need cannot draw near to console; and if he does so, it is out of condescension and not out of compassion. The good religious, on the contrary, sees Mary in the dust of human lives; she lives among the miseries of men, bodily and spiritual. She is, in a most particular way, Mother of Sinners; the Immaculate is with the stained: the Sinless One with the sinner. In the Passion Our Lady feels neither resentment nor bitterness, but only pity, pity that they do not see or do not know how to love that Love which they are sending to death.

A man can become so obsessed with his sin that he refuses to ask God for His forgiveness, but he cannot turn away and fail to invoke the intercession of the Mother of God.

In the mystery of Our Lady at the foot of the cross, the religious must know how to find strength to overcome all crosses. If the good and Holy Mother Mary, who never deserved to feel evil, could nevertheless, in the special providence of her Son, have a cross, then how can we, who do not deserve to be placed at her level, expect to escape encountering the cross? That is why, in the face of our tribulations, we must desire that there never be a complaint against God for sending us a cross, and that we have enough wisdom to see that Mary is there making it lighter, sweeter, making it her own.

Mary, as Universal Mother, gives us a true sense of the Church; every misfortune, every wound in the world, is ours as religious. As long as there is an innocent religious in jail, I am in prison. As long as a missionary is without a roof over his head, I am homeless. That is compassion. The religious will never sit back and watch the world’s enmity against our Lord, knowing that Mary’s cooperation was so real and active that she stood at the foot of the cross. In every depiction of the Crucifixion, Magdalene is prostrate, almost always at the feet of Our Lord, but Mary is standing.

The religious, who by his juridical state of perfection occupies a prominent place in the Church, must also stand out for his deep and heartfelt devotion to Mary. He must carry her not only in the depths of his heart, but also on his lips, so that he may always appear before the faithful as an ardent promoter of the cult of and devotion to Mary.

May our lips continually repeat these words: “Jesus” and “Mary.” And, as St. Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort said, may Mary be the mold in which we are sculpted.

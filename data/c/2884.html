Directory of Consecrated Life, 392

“Our world is growing old with so much seriousness… the priest today has the mission of keeping the universe transparent and so preserving humor”1, wrote the Venerable Archbishop Fulton Sheen.

In a few days we are going to celebrate, together with Christians throughout the whole world, the mystery of the Resurrection of our Lord, a mystery from which springs “an element that must be essential in our spirituality”2: joy.

What joy are we speaking of?

We are not referring here to “the consumerist and individualistic joy so present in some cultural experiences today. For consumerism only bloats the heart; it can offer occasional and passing pleasures, but not joy”3. Our Proper Law tells us that “our joy must be spiritual and supernatural”4. That is why we would like to devote this space to developing, at least in its most outstanding features, what it means that our joy must be spiritual and supernatural.

May these pages be a heartfelt tribute to so many of our religious who in many places, and carrying out very varied offices, live in constant joy, keeping alive the memory of the benefits of our Lord, and who in spite of their weariness, limitations, trials and failures live joyfully because they are close to Christ and useful to the Church. But most especially may these pages go to all those who, because of the fruitlessness of their apostolic labors, the various spiritual torments and dense solitudes, are wavering, discouraged and ready to surrender to sadness. May God be pleased to make use of these lines to console them and so that serene joy may return to their souls.

1. Joy

Joy can be natural or spiritual. Natural joy is, for example, that of the young religious whose soul has not yet experienced the trials of missionary disillusionment; or the joy of health, when all food tastes good; it is the joy of success after a battle won, or it is the joy of affection when one feels loved, and “feeling loved always produces consolation and joy”5. All these ‘natural’ joys are intensified and raised onto a more lasting and deeper foundation by spiritual joys.

In general, we could say that “spiritual joy is serenity of temperament amid the changing situations of life, like that of a mountain when a storm sweeps over it. That is why a man who does not have his soul rooted in the Divine blows every problem out of proportion. He cannot apply his full potential to anything because he is worried about many things”6. More than a passing attitude, spiritual joy is rather a habit, and being more permanent, it makes difficult things easier to bear.

In other words, this spiritual joy to which we are referring can be experienced both in prosperity and in adversity. In prosperity it consists not in the goods that are enjoyed, but in the goods that are hoped for; not in the pleasures that are experienced but in the promise of those in which we believe even without having seen them. Goods may abound, but those we hope for are of the kind that neither moth nor rust destroys, nor thieves can seize. It is the joy experienced even in adversity in knowing that the Divine Master died on the cross as the condition for his Resurrection7.

That is why our Proper Law says that “the splendid fact that Christ is risen must lead us to live… with immense joy”8. And from this follows one of the characteristic features of ‘our joy.’ The joy of the missionary, of the religious, of the monk of the Incarnate Word is intimately related to the Paschal Mystery of Christ, that is, to our Redemption. Our joy is spiritual precisely because “it is born of the contemplation of the mysteries of the Life of Christ, in particular of his Incarnation and his Resurrection”9. Let us therefore acknowledge that spiritual joy finds its source, is nourished and is preserved in prayer, in familiarity with the Incarnate Word10.

  • Contemplation of the Incarnation

“By the Incarnation of the Word the immortality of happiness is made credible”11, says St. Augustine. Jesus by his birth brought a “new vision” to the world and to men. Only if we are humble can we contemplate in the Child lying in the manger, in the Word made flesh, divine Omnipotence, infinite Joy12. But, as we say, for this one must be humble, become little and enter into the divine game of not taking oneself seriously, of not thinking oneself too central, so as to be able to become by grace what He is by nature13. Consequently, one must rise to the joyful forgetfulness of self14.

“The birth of the Incarnate Word urges us, among other things, to live in joy, the fruit of the Holy Spirit and a consequence of the Incarnation, as the angel announced to the shepherds: I bring you good news, a great joy that is for all the people (Lk 2:10); it is the joy of the Virgin Mary, ‘cause of our joy’”15.

The Directory of Contemplative Life develops this point very beautifully, saying: “In the Incarnation of the Word one can already perceive a certain loving and eternal ‘game’ that only God could carry out:

– man had aspired to become like God16 through sin; through the Incarnation God becomes man17 to save him and raise him, by grace, to his divine level.

– Man sinned by claiming ‘to be an adult,’ knowing good and evil; God, the Eternal Wisdom, in becoming incarnate ‘becomes a babbling child’ so that man may attain his maturity in Christ18.

– Man, wanting to run the adventure of his autonomy, asked God for his share of the inheritance19, and ended up feeding pigs20; God, being rich, became poor to enrich him with his poverty21”22.

That is why our joy −as far as we come to understand it− also has to do with becoming like children (which −let us clarify− has nothing to do with the infantilism that makes people who have reached biological maturity display psychological attitudes proper to childhood). Spiritual childhood restores joy to our lives because it makes us humble and, for that very reason, makes us recognize that we need Redemption.

God laughs at those who think themselves ‘wise’ because they flaunt some academic title, or at those who think themselves ‘something’ because they hold some important post, or at those who think they ‘need nothing’ because they have a few things. The Incarnation of the Word teaches us to live according to criteria very different from these, and particularly us, who make religious profession and want to confess with courage and fortitude our faith in Christ. Because, in the end, living joyfully is a question of faith.

“If a priest loses faith, he loses joy. To the extent that his faith increases, his joy increases, to the point of the folly of the cross”23. Let no one deceive himself –says the apostle– If anyone among you thinks he is wise in this age, let him become a fool so as to become wise. For the wisdom of this world is foolishness to God24. That is the logic of the Incarnation, which our Proper Law describes very eloquently: “The folly of the cross consists in living the beatitudes. Blessed are the fools for Christ! They will be carried from here to there, they will be laughed at and taken for clumsy, backward and even feeble-minded: theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven. Blessed are the fools for Christ! For they have emptied themselves and stand before God in all their guilelessness. Blessed are these fools for Christ! No wisdom of the world will ever be able to deceive them. It is the folly of love without limits or measure. It is to bless those who curse us25, it is not to repay evil for evil (Rom 12:17). When the world says of us: Look at the fools! stones are thrown at them and they kiss the hand that throws them; people laugh at them and mock them, and they laugh too, like children who do not understand; they are struck, persecuted and martyred, but they give thanks to God who found them worthy. When the world says that, it is a sign that we are on the right path”26.

The mystery of the Incarnation puts us in contact with Christ emptied, with Christ the Victim, whom we must imitate. But just as Christ knew how to bear every pain, every humiliation, and even death for love of us, so we too must know how to see in those same things the opportunity to return to Christ love for love. “If we do not learn to be victims with the Victim, all our sufferings are useless”27. But suffering is overcome by loving and by finding in it a new way of serving others. And that service is transformed into spiritual joy, which allows us, in spite of the trials and obstacles of this life, not to lose heart and always to keep going forward. Ours is to be priests always victims, but in spirit always victorious. Let us be imitators of Jesus, who, in place of the joy that was set before him, endured the cross, disregarding its shame28. It is faith that gives us the courage to overcome defeats, which in the end, in the light of the Incarnation and Resurrection of Christ, are only apparent defeats. For our victory over the world is precisely our faith in the Son of God who, incarnate, has come into the world29.

When Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word, passed by, all the people rejoiced30. In the same way we, missionaries of the Incarnate Word who make religious profession so that our life may be a living memorial of the way of existing and acting of Jesus, the Word made flesh31, must strive to radiate the joy of Christ32. Ours is not just any joy; it is spiritual and it is supernatural, and for that very reason it cannot be contained; that is why our motto is “to live in crystal-clear and contagious joy”33.

It is true that “there are hard moments, times of the cross, but nothing can destroy supernatural joy, which ‘adapts and changes, but it always endures, even as a flicker of light born of our personal certainty that, when everything is said and done, we are infinitely loved’34. It is an inner security, a hope-filled serenity that offers a spiritual satisfaction incomprehensible by worldly standards”35.

The kenosis of the Lord in his Incarnation and in his Passion makes us see the mysterious relationship between renunciation and inner joy, between sacrifice and breadth of heart, between discipline and spiritual freedom36. Let us not be pessimists: Let us rejoice even in our sufferings37!

To help us in this, and almost in passing, our Proper Law gives us the following provision: “It is fitting that the priest […] desire and rejoice to visit and adore Christ sacramentally present in the Eucharist”38.

  • Contemplation of the Resurrection

“There is no greater reason for joy –says our Proper Law– than the Resurrection of the Lord, because his triumph is our triumph, his victory is our victory. The Paschal Mystery is the manifestation par excellence of God’s love for men: he loved them to the end39. That is why in this mystery we must rejoice, and our joy must be permanent”40.

Now, for our joy to be permanent, besides keeping our gaze firmly fixed on the Incarnate Word, we must have a good sense of humor, so outstanding, for example, in St. Thomas More, who said: “Happy are those who know how to laugh at themselves, for they will never stop being amused,” or in St. Philip Neri, whom our Proper Law quotes with the following phrase: “‘melancholy is the eighth capital sin’41”42, or in the Blessed martyr Miguel Agustín Pro, in whom “neither sufferings, nor serious illnesses, nor exhausting ministerial activity, often carried out in painful and dangerous circumstances, could stifle the radiant and communicative joy that was born of his love for Christ and that no one could take from him”43, and he ended his life as he had always lived it: generously, with joy, giving everything and keeping nothing for himself.

“The priest cannot take this world too seriously. We are pilgrims, not tourists. The Kingdom of God is not the Secular City”44, said Fulton Sheen. That is why, aware that we are pilgrims, we must have our hearts set on God and learn to have a providential view of life, knowing how to look upon everything as coming from Him who does not forget even a little sparrow… and has counted even the hairs of our head45. And “in saying all things it means all events, prosperous or adverse, what concerns the good of the soul, the goods of fortune, reputation, all the conditions of human life, all the interior states through which we pass, even faults and sins themselves”46.

For this reason, if our joy is to be permanent, it cannot be worldly. “Worldliness is always sad, because if we lose the ball of this world, we feel miserable. The holier we are, the less sad we are. And if nowadays there are sad priests, it is not because ‘the world is full of problems’ –when has it ever been without problems?–, it is because the priest has lost the transparency of faith”47, in the sense that he has lost that capacity to see the providence of God in the events of this world and of his own life in particular. That is why we say that our joy is supernatural.

Now, it is true that “in every community there are various categories of souls. Some live joyfully and radiate around them the holy joy they enjoy. Not the merely sensible joy, which may depend on temperament, but the interior joy that is a prelude to eternal happiness. Others do not enjoy it; within they are troubled, restless, unhappy. Why this difference? Because, in the first case, whoever seeks God in all things always finds Him, in every place, and with God acquires the supreme good and the happiness that is not lost. In the second case, on the other hand, the soul clings to creatures, feeds self-love, and in seeking itself heads toward emptiness, and this cannot content it. It will give itself entirely to exterior occupation, but that distraction will be illusory and insufficient; it will find distraction, but not consolation, and will feel its life to be heavy and hard to bear”48.

How much we must guard ourselves against falling into the temptation of being the uptight priest: “all seriousness, always distant, inaccessible, tense, who does not know how to laugh, to whom every healthy recreation seems frivolity, for whom an ill-sounding word is a grave sin, for whom eutrapelia seems not to be a virtue, who for looking at the tree forgets the forest, and tends to make storms in a glass of water”49. Or into the temptation of being a bad-tempered priest: “always impatient, sullen, who is approachable only when he is in a good mood, and becomes sterile, sees everything as black, is not a celibate but an old bachelor, and in fact does not know that a milligram of grace weighs more than all the sins of the world, even these raised to the nth power”50. “Serene joy is the outward expression of a balanced spiritual interior life”51.

Quite the contrary, we must try each day to have a positive and hopeful spirit and, without losing realism, know how to take things with a certain sense of humor, being grateful and not overly complicated. This attitude, this supernatural view of things, this detachment from earthly things is, according to Fulton Sheen, the vestibule of mysticism52.

As priests we must learn to spiritualize, sacramentalize and ennoble everything in this world and make it into prayer, that is, raise everything to God and unite ourselves voluntarily with Him in everything, through the active exercise of faith, hope and charity. Note that no occupation is so lowly that it cannot be spiritualized, no suffering so hard that it cannot be ennobled. We must develop in ourselves that capacity which does not let the many occasions that daily life gives us slip by without making them into prayer or drawing from them a divine lesson. That is why, with a certain humor, our Proper Law places us before this situation: “it urges us to work in the most difficult places (those where no one wants to go) and when it is no longer possible to continue there, after praying about it for a night before Jesus in the Blessed Sacrament, to ask the bishop to send us to a worse place”53. So that even in such a situation, we may know where to find calm, recover the supernatural sense of things and respond with even greater self-giving, without losing heart.

The Passion, Death and Resurrection of our Lord raise us above our precariousness and our situations, so often miserable, to the supernatural plane. Fulton Sheen said: “If an act of contrition had to be translated into a facial expression, it would be a smile… If one does not know that one is wounded, one can never rejoice at being healed”54.

It is in this sense that our Proper Law exhorts us “to rediscover […] the beauty and joy of the sacrament of Penance”55 as an occasion to experience “the consoling joy of forgiveness”56. What joy is more crystal-clear than that of a clean conscience!

What is more, we can say that the Resurrection of Christ is a cause of rejoicing not only for the Lord but also for the redeemed, since it allows us to make our own the words of the psalm: Then our mouth was filled with laughter and our lips with shouts of joy57.

One day St. John Paul II, speaking to priests and religious in Copenhagen, told them (and today he wants to repeat it to us): “God has not called us only to a life of sacrifice and renunciation, but also to a life of intimate joy and fulfillment”58. That too is important to keep in mind. For at times the project of sanctification or ‘evangelical perfection’ is identified one-sidedly or exclusively with self-denial and the cross, renunciation and purification. There is no doubt: following Christ requires all that as the first condition of the disciple, and it is also important to accept it and embrace it, for these are basic guidelines of the one Gospel. However, unfortunately we sometimes reduce holiness to that alone, when in reality the active and passive nights serve to dispose us so that the divine action may reach its fullness: union with God, infinite Joy. And that must not be forgotten.

2. Distinctive characteristics

Let us now single out some distinctive features of the joy that must characterize us, and our specific way of living it and manifesting it in the Institute.

▪ Our very consecration as religious must be a source of joy; that, in the first place. For the profession of our vows proclaims “the joy of living according to the evangelical beatitudes”59. And for those of us who are already priests, “our [priestly] identity, our true dignity, must be especially the source of our joy and the certainty of our life”60.

▪ Lived in community: Consequently, this spiritual and supernatural joy of which we have been speaking must also be lived and manifested in community. “Fraternal love multiplies our capacity for joy, since it makes us capable of rejoicing in the good of others: Rejoice with those who rejoice61”62. For joy is “a fruit of the Holy Spirit and an effect of charity, and therefore we must try, by every means, that ‘no one be disturbed or saddened in the house of God.’ For this it is absolutely indispensable to live fraternal charity”63. Which is a rule for superiors and subjects alike. How many religious live discouraged or sad because of disagreements among themselves!

We must be careful about this, because a fraternity without joy is a fraternity that is dying out, and very soon its members will be tempted to look elsewhere for what they cannot find at home…64. On the contrary, joy in religious arouses an enormous attraction toward religious life; it is a source of new vocations and a support for perseverance65.

Our Proper Law is clear: “Every community of our little Religious Family must live that spirit of healthy recreation and contagious joy, which are founded on the fulfillment of religious duties and on always considering that ‘God is the first served’66”67. Now, “only he truly rejoices who rejoices in love: ‘where charity rejoices, there is festivity’68. And this because every feast implies an existential richness that in turn is born of a renunciation, for there can be no time for a feast unless something is renounced. And this renunciation implies love, for there is no renunciation where there is no love69”70. And from this follows the following practical note for living joy as a religious of the Incarnate Word. Namely:

▪ “To live in a climate of festive joy”71: This is one of the characteristics of our joy, closely related to the previous note. For “our communities must show how the love of Christ drives away even the shadow of sadness”72. Indeed, our Proper Law, in a fatherly way, commands us “to foster even outwardly an atmosphere of joy”73. And it emphasizes: “It is very important to cultivate this joy in the religious community: excessive work can extinguish it, exaggerated zeal for some causes can make one forget it, constantly questioning one’s own identity and one’s own future can overshadow it…”74. Therefore, one must “know how to rejoice together, allow oneself personal and communal moments of relaxation, step back from one’s own work from time to time, rejoice in the joys of one’s brother, pay solicitous attention to the needs of brothers and sisters, give oneself generously to apostolic work, face situations with mercy, go out to meet the future with the hope of always and everywhere finding the Lord: all this nourishes serenity, peace and joy, and becomes strength for apostolic action. The path is difficult, but possible in Christ: Joyful in hope, strong in tribulation, persevering in prayer75”76.

We must always be clear that our way of living in community implies living “as a true family in Christ, with signs of deference, and bearing one another’s burdens”77; “helping, encouraging or spiritually enkindling one another”78. This also implies accepting with kindness the defects, real or apparent, of others, even when they bother us, and gladly accepting all the sacrifices imposed by living together with those whose mentality and temperament do not fully agree with our own way of seeing and judging79. From this follows the next note for practicing joy according to the spirituality of our Institute.

▪ To live in an affectionate and joyful family climate: whether in the minor or major seminary, in a contemplative monastery or in a small community dedicated to the mission, the religious, the seminarian, the priest must love his companions as true brothers and his Superiors as true fathers80.

In this sense our Proper Law, condescending to our weaknesses and with great pedagogy, exhorts us, telling us that “to foster the practice of charity in the joyful mutual help that is born of the contemplation and love of God, and which can be summed up in the oratorian spirit81, it is important to celebrate duly the solemnities of the liturgical year, promoting participation in the liturgy of the mysteries of Christ and of the Church, as well as the proper feasts of the Congregation and of the Seminary, prepared by means of novenas or triduums, especially the Octave of Easter, Sundays, and the feasts of the Apostles and of Our Lady82. All this is achieved by solemnizing and actively participating in the liturgy, and also by means of rest, joy and fitting celebrations”83. “Likewise it will help –to the extent possible– to set aside certain solemnities or feasts for gathering with the other communities of the Religious Family that are in the Province”84. It is important to know how to ‘invest’ in everything that contributes to that spirit of a festive family climate, as we are explicitly told: the religious “must have spiritual joy but also manifest it”85. “Everything must contribute to joy”86.

At times people tend to think that manifestations of joy are reserved for the youngest, who have not yet been ‘hardened’ by the sufferings of the mission; or else, others think that because religious are older they no longer have fun. Not infrequently those who think this way believe that joy is merely ‘horsing around.’ The truth is that for each age there is a way of having fun and of expressing that joy. Let us pay attention to what our Proper Law points out to us: “The presence of elderly persons in communities can be very positive. An elderly religious who does not let himself be overcome by the ailments and limits of age, but keeps joy, love and hope alive, is a support of incalculable value for the young”87. As time goes by and –by the grace of God– in various communities of our Institute we come to have religious of ‘the youngest’ and of ‘the oldest’ together, we must know how to tap the potential of wisdom and experience of the latter, but they too must know that they are called to practice, in keeping with their age, the virtue of eutrapelia, which is the next characteristic note of our joy.

▪ Eutrapelia: “Recreation and rest in common are of the highest importance. We call this by the name of the virtue to which they belong: eutrapelia”88. “The soul, like the body, grows tired and must rest. The rest of the soul is delight, so that this must be the remedy against the weariness of the soul, interrupting the tension of the spirit. Hence it is necessary to procure this rest for the soul by means of games and festivities, whose moderation belongs to the virtue of eutrapelia, practiced in times of recreation”89.

“St. Thomas wisely says that no one has the right to be burdensome to others”90. Therefore, the virtue of eutrapelia is not a synonym for the religious who makes everyone else the object of his jokes, or for the religious who thinks he is a comedian and makes himself the object of the jokes of others. Eutrapelia “has as its object to regulate games and amusements according to the right order of reason”91. Opposed to this virtue is foolish mirth, which gives itself over to illicit amusements, whether by reason of their very object or for lack of the due circumstances of time, place or person; and also opposed to this virtue is the excessive austerity of those who neither want to take recreation themselves nor let others do so.

“It is in eutrapelia that generosity is seen and good spirit is verified; not in amusement in itself, which may be the fruit of human enthusiasm, but insofar as it is supernatural joy, which can exist even in the company of trials or interior dryness”92. Our Proper Law also fittingly points out that “there is no fixed or obligatory measure for taking part in the eutrapelias, but it is certain that the more charity one puts into it, the more of God’s goods one will receive; and it can be verified that those who are most generous in times of joy are those who are most productive in their other activities”93.

Fulton Sheen said that there is no group in the world with more humor than priests at their recreations –we could say– the “Pro” gatherings of priests. And in a few lines –and with no little irony– he exemplifies the healthy joy that must reign in them: “the well-meant jokes that immediately deflate the puffed-up, the endless stories about bishops, mother superiors and parishioners make laughter reign at every gathering. [There] no talent is defended, no achievements are rewarded, but neither are the losers allowed to get depressed. Only about one thing does one never laugh, and that is the salvation of a soul. […] Since faith reigns at those gatherings, and therefore sound judgment, our opinions are ‘always correct.’ If we say that ‘such-and-such a priest is a madman,’ that priest is a madman. Individual opinion is often absurd, but when faith reigns: where two or three are gathered in My name, there am I in their midst94”95.

▪ To rejoice in Christ proclaimed: that is, in apostolic work. “The characteristic of every authentic missionary life is the inner joy that comes from faith”96. It is important to recall what our documents say about religious brothers, but which applies well to any of the members: “Through work the religious brother brings great help to the community, especially if he knows how to find in work a source of joy. In work he can see the progress of his abilities, the development of his personality and the perfecting of his physical and spiritual qualities. Joy, moreover, comes from service, according to the saying of our Lord quoted by St. Paul: There is greater happiness in giving than in receiving (Acts 20:35)”97. “The idle man will never taste the joys of a well-earned rest, for work is pleasant also because it is afterward rewarded with rest”98.

How edifying is the example of the religious of the Institute devoted to the sublime and at the same time demanding office of evangelization in countries at war, or amid great privations and trials, who, surpassing themselves, know how to transform suffering into self-giving and service, and keep the joy of evangelizing, even when they must sow in tears. Truly their example spurs us on, gladdens us exceedingly and stimulates us to greater self-giving.

▪ Lived even in poverty: We members of the Institute are commanded “to accept with joy, for the love of God, privations, even in necessary things, for the sake of holy poverty”99. Many of us who remember the first years of the Institute, or who were present at the beginnings of some mission, know –not from hearsay– the needs we went through, and yet the common denominator was the immense joy we enjoyed, to the point that shortages seemed to us a reason for greater joy and became anecdotes. That is why we think we are not mistaken when we say that joy is deeply related to poverty of spirit. “For the poor in spirit is more constant and joyful in want, because he has placed his all in nothing at all, and so finds in everything breadth of heart”100. In other words: “There are no worries about this world for one who wants to have nothing in it; for his wealth is God”101.

*****

In short, let us not seek another path to the deep and serene joy of religious life, for there is none other than this: active and responsible abandonment for the sake of the will of God. The difficulties, the crosses that will never be lacking “nor does God want them to be lacking”102, must not diminish our joy.

For in life “there is only one sadness, that of not being saints”103. The rejoice in the Lord always104 is something theological: it is the fruit of a continual friendship with the Incarnate Word and of letting oneself be guided by his Spirit, one of whose fruits is joy105.

Now that together with the whole Church we rejoice in the Resurrection of our Lord, let us remember that our spirituality is also that of the “Gloria” and of the “Magnificat”106, and let us take up again with vigor our struggle for holiness; let us advance with renewed enthusiasm in our mission so that our witness of hope in the resurrection may be credible107.

Let us never lose heart. In the midst of the insecurity of situations, of the helplessness that is a file for the soul, of the scarcity of resources of every kind, of the perplexities that try to halt our march, we must not look back, nor must we ‘shrink back.’ Jesus gives us an assurance: You will be sad, but your sadness will turn into joy. […] I will see you again, and your heart will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you108. I have spoken to you of this so that my joy may be in you, and your joy may be complete109.

Today the Church needs our witness of joy. Let us raise our prayers so that the recognition of our limits and weaknesses, and our Christian acceptance of challenges, may become an occasion to experience the power of God and the extraordinary riches of his grace. The Risen One is beside us and repeats to us once again: Do not be afraid!110Why are you troubled, and why do doubts arise in your heart… it is I myself111.

May the Blessed Virgin, Mother of the Risen One and Cause of our joy, make us witnesses of the joy of Christ who lives forever.

This Easter we greet you with the customary greeting of Don Bosco, the Saint of grace and joy:

Be joyful!

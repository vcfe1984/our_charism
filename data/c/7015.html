Theologians teach1 that there are three types of will or volitional attitudes. Namely:

  • The fickle will, which expresses itself in the “potential indicative”: I would want, I would like, I would have.. This form of will does not reach true willing; it remains on a preliminary plane. It is a will with bags under its eyes: whiny, sad, embittered. In the most serious cases, there is not even a would want but indifference. It is the attitude of the “first class of men” in the language of St. Ignatius: “I would like to remove the attachment”2.
  • The deceived will is more dangerous than the previous one, and the danger lies in the falsehood that envelops it. It is a will that lies to itself about the “means” it chooses to attain a given end. St. Ignatius describes these persons by saying that they want to give up what God requires them to give up, but in such a way that, in the end, they wind up keeping what they claimed to be giving up. They have the attitude of the “second class of men.” It is the person who lies about the means, that is, who wants to convince himself −and often also wants to convince others− that the means he has chosen are ordered to the end he has set himself, when that is not so.
  • The true will is the one that wills an end and the means that effectively lead to that end, even if they are hard and difficult means. And it wills them sincerely and puts them into practice promptly.

This type of will has certain fundamental characteristics, among which we can say that: it is persevering, tenacious, firm; it overcomes failures by starting over, if necessary, the works that did not turn out as expected; it accepts challenges, rises above falls and is capable of finishing the works it has undertaken. This is the type of will that St. Ignatius refers to in his Exercises as that of the “third class of men”:

“The third wants to remove the attachment, but wants to remove it in such a way that he also has no attachment to keeping the thing acquired or not keeping it, but wants only to will it or not will it according as God our Lord will place it in his will, and as shall seem better to that person for the service and praise of His divine majesty; and meanwhile he wants to consider that he gives it all up in affection, striving not to want that or any other thing, unless only the service of God our Lord moves him, so that the desire of being better able to serve God our Lord moves him to take the thing or leave it”3.

This last is precisely the type of will that the Incarnate Word demanded while His adorable feet trod this earth: If you want to enter into life, keep the commandments4; If you want to be perfect, go and sell what you possess…5; If anyone wants to be first, he must be last6; If anyone wants to serve me, let him follow me7.

And that is also “the priestly attitude proper to the ‘third class of men’”8 that the religious vocation in the Institute of the Incarnate Word demands of us.

In a world plagued by wavering attitudes in the face of questions or situations that are decisive, by souls disoriented and perplexed before the challenge of the present times, in a world of blurred identities, where commitment, responsibility and the heroism of fidelity seem remote realities, we, the members of the Incarnate Word, must strengthen ourselves, that is, consolidate ourselves in our own identity, “striving” −as St. Ignatius said− for that which we know makes us serve God better, that is, not only “to fulfill with the greatest possible perfection the evangelical counsels and the self-giving to Jesus through Mary, but also ‘to order one’s life according to the Proper Law of the Institute and thus strive to attain the perfection of our state’9”10.

The Spiritual Father of our Religious Family already said it: “This is not the moment for indecision, absence or lack of commitment. It is the hour of the bold, of those who have hope, of those who aspire to live the Gospel in its fullness and of those who want to carry it out in today’s world and in the history that is approaching”11. It is our hour!

That is why I would like to share with you a reflection on what we must apply our will to in order to act according to what God wants for us and how God wants it. For we must not deceive ourselves: if we are to attain the perfection of our state, it must be in no other way than by ordering our own life according to “the Constitutions bequeathed by the Father Founder; the Directories, which contain subsidiary and practical norms applying the Constitutions”12, as well as by practicing the traditions and sound customs of the Institute. That is why the great Doctor of the Church, St. John of the Cross, gave this counsel to a religious: “In no way should you want to know anything, except only how you will serve God more and better keep the things of your institute”13.

1. Two clarifications

But before beginning the subject in earnest, I would like to make two clarifications here:

According to “the priestly attitude proper to the ‘third class of men’”14 that our call to follow Christ in this Institute demands, there is no place here for the first two kinds of will we mentioned at the beginning −fickle and deceived−. The Directory of Spirituality teaches it in number 42 when it says that the religious in this Institute must be “firmly resolved,” that is, with a true will, to attain holiness. And it clarifies that if “a religious is not willing to pass through the second and third conversion, or does nothing concrete to achieve it, even though he is with us in body, he does not belong to our spiritual family”15.

Therefore there is no place among us for the indifferent religious who thinks that the norms of the Institute “do not apply to him,” that is, who believes that what the Proper Law commands is not for him but for others to fulfill, and meanwhile lives embittered, discontented, regretful in his state16 simply because he lives ‘outside the rule,’ hoping to fulfill it to perfection when he makes perpetual vows, when he is finally a priest or when he celebrates his golden priestly jubilee… but in reality he secretly hopes that the rule will change and adapt itself to him or to his own judgment; meanwhile, he wastes his time. The initiatives of the Institute slide right off this religious, if indeed he does not obstruct them himself, because he is one of those who “neither burns nor is burned by the fire of the Holy Spirit”17; it is all the same to him. All of these attitudes are proper, as we have said, to a fickle, whimsical, inconstant will, that of the first class. A typical attitude of religious who have misunderstood the freedom of the children of God and live enslaved under the letter that kills or under the spirit of the world18, and even under their own changing whims, forgetting that our task is “to imitate more closely and to represent perpetually in the Church that form of life which the Son of God chose when He became incarnate”19 and that this includes, of course, “the self-emptyings of Nazareth and Calvary”20.

Nor is there a place here for the excuse put forward by many religious of wanting to change the rule with ridiculous proposals (at times even outlandish ones) or by making far-fetched or even diametrically opposed interpretations of what is prescribed regarding the means the rule commands for our sanctification, which is proper to the “second class.” And so these think that one can be poor without being poor, penitent without doing penance, docile to the Holy Spirit without having one’s soul ready for all that God disposes, persevere in one’s vocation only if the optimal conditions for living in community are present … They consider themselves reformers, innovators… even “just interpreters of the law” and zealous pastors. Then −always under the cloak of zeal, to make their aim viable− they gather hundreds of pretexts and conveniences before their superiors or even before their subjects, but in reality they are only seeking themselves and their own comfort. They think they are going to make the IVE great with their ideas, and meanwhile they dilute what identifies us; with their fallacies they undermine precisely what we consider non-negotiable: serious spirituality, Thomistic formation, mission in the outposts… and they look with disdain on observant religious, and if such men are given authority, they even persecute them.

Let us say it explicitly: ‘the will of God is expressed… specifically for religious through their own Constitutions’21, and that is why superiors, in exercising their power, must do so ‘in accordance with the proper and the universal law’22”23. That is why St. John Bosco advised his religious: “let us flee from the itch for reform. Let us strive to observe our rules without thinking of improving or reforming them”24, and he pointed out to them the advice Pius IX had given them: “without seeking to improve your constitutions, try to observe them punctually [and] your society will flourish more and more”25.

For this reason it is fitting to fix in our minds and souls the Ignatian watchword of “striving not to want that or any other thing, unless only the service of God our Lord moves him, so that the desire of being better able to serve God our Lord moves him to take the thing or leave it”26. And we, as we said, know very clearly that what makes us serve God better is what is clearly prescribed in the Proper Law.

Pay attention to the words of the founder of the Little Work of Divine Providence, which our Proper Law makes its own and, therefore, normative for our life. Thus “Don Orione wrote: ‘…We will become saints just as the Lord wants of us: that is, by tenderly loving our Congregation and observing its Constitutions. The Congregation is truly loved, and it is loved as much as its Rules are sincerely loved and practiced with diligence and good spirit’27. ‘Love your Congregation in its holy purpose!… Love it because it is your Mother! Give it great consolations, honor it with your life as good and holy religious; as its true and holy sons’28”29.

Therefore, we cannot be saints except according to what God inspired in our Founder and what is set down in the Constitutions and in the Directories. That is the clearly marked path we must follow; there the means we must use are explicitly stated. There is no need to go shopping at other congregations to see what they do so that we can do it too. We must not have an ambivalent attitude when we already know plainly the position we must take and the path we must follow. We must not let ourselves be carried along by our own tastes, or our own judgment, or our passions in doing or not doing. Thus the Mystical Doctor said to the Carmelite nuns: “What good is it for you to give God one thing if He asks you for another? Consider what God will want and do it, for in that way you will satisfy your heart better than with that to which you are inclined”30.

Now, let it be well understood: this is not rigidity, this is not closed-mindedness, this is not self-referentiality (in its negative aspect), this is not “immovability of judgment,” because there will be no lack of those who accuse us of it. Since when is fidelity a bad thing?

Know that the Proper Law also has provisions made for possible adaptations with a view to a renewal of religious life in the Institute, but it establishes that those necessary adaptations consist “in a continual return […] to the spirit bequeathed by the Founder and to the fulfillment of the Constitutions, that is, to the patrimony of the Institute, in which are contained the riches that the Holy Spirit has granted it for the good of the Church”31. It does not say that the adaptations must follow the whim of someone labeled as more astute, much less that those adaptations allow one to depart from the spirit bequeathed to the Founder. On the contrary, it implies “a constant return to the original inspiration of the Institute”32 and therefore invites us to “recognize and maintain ‘faithfully the spirit and proper aims of the founders, as well as the sound traditions’33, in a ‘better observance of the rule and Constitutions rather than in the multiplication of laws’34”35. Consider yourselves warned.

“It follows, from what has been said, in an evident way,” in the very words of the Proper Law, “that he cannot be a true religious who does not accept, unjustly criticizes, or simply wants to live on the margins of the charism given by the Holy Spirit to the Founder. A true religious, on the contrary, must love and keep strict fidelity to his Institute, which has begotten him to religious life, to the point, if necessary, of giving his life”36.

This is important to bear in mind. For there have been difficulties, there are, and there always will be. Simply because the following form part of the program of our life: “labors, humiliations, affronts, torments, sorrows, persecutions, misunderstandings, setbacks, reproaches, contempt, insults, calumnies, [and even] death [itself]”37. So once again: we have followed Christ not with the intention of turning back in the face of the difficulties of religious life, or in the face of slander, or worse still, in the face of slight setbacks. We religious of the Incarnate Word must “have a will ready for anything: so the nature of the vows we have made demands”38.

Consequently, realizing that our being faithful to the will of God is summed up in strict fidelity to the Constitutions, and that any departure from that entails a certain fault toward God, is important to know when it comes to “transmitting to the new generations of our Religious Family the charism that the Holy Spirit granted to the Founder”39; otherwise the letter of the Constitutions will be a dead letter. And what we have just affirmed concerns not only the formators, not only the priests or the Provincial Superiors; let it be understood that each religious of the IVE must live as if the survival of this marvelous charism in the Catholic Church depended on him alone. To the point that he himself is an incarnation of the charism, of what a true religious of the Incarnate Word must be.

2. Our willing

Now, we said earlier that we must apply our will to act according to what God wants for us and how God wants it, all of which is expressed specifically for us, the religious of the Incarnate Word, in our Constitutions40. Therefore, we must identify our willing with the willing of God expressed in the Proper Law, and do this “striving,” as St. Ignatius said, without ambivalence, but rather with boldness, with constancy, with “a great and very determined determination”41.

Let us note at this point that the Constitutions and the Directory of Spirituality combined contain the expression “we want” 49 times, 24 of them in the Constitutions. And although many other classifications are possible, it has seemed to us that these statements can be classified according to whether they pertain to the identity of a religious of the Incarnate Word, to the spiritual imprint or interior attitude with which a religious of the IVE must live his self-giving, to the demands of formation required of a religious of the IVE, or to the works to which the Institute is dedicated.

It would be desirable for each religious to read these “we want”s, replacing them with “it is my true will,” “I have a great and very determined determination to…,” “I strive to want…,” and to incarnate them in his own life, for in this way he will not be slow to carry out “the service of God with perfection”42.

– Identity of a religious of the Incarnate Word:

  • “We want to be ‘like another humanity of His’43, we want to be chalices full of Christ that pour out His superabundance upon others, we want to show with our lives that Christ lives”44.
  • “We want to give the ‘witness that the world cannot be transformed and offered to God without the spirit of the beatitudes’45”46.
  • “We want to be founded on Jesus Christ”47.
  • “We want to be men docile to the great discipline of the Church, expressed in the Code of Canon Law, in all the other ecclesiastical norms and laws, and docile to the particular discipline of our Institute…”48.

– According to the spiritual imprint or interior attitude with which a religious of the IVE must live his self-giving:

  • “We want to manifest our love and gratitude to the Most Blessed Virgin while at the same time obtaining her indispensable help to prolong the Incarnation in all things, by making a fourth vow of Marian slavery according to St. Louis Mary Grignion de Montfort”49.
  • “We want to have a right intention: do everything for the glory of God (1 Cor 10:31)”50.
  • “We want to constitute a Religious Family in which the members are ready to live with complete radicality the demands of the Incarnation and of the Cross, of the Sermon on the Mount and of the Last Supper”51.
  • “We want to follow Christ more closely under the action of the Holy Spirit”52.
  • “We want to nourish our desire to abandon ourselves entirely to God’s will of good pleasure, and our love for the Trinity and for men created by God in His image and likeness”53.
  • “We want to offer God the holocaust of our body and of all our natural affections, living ‘the obligation of perfect continence in celibacy’54”55.
  • “We want justice, which gives to each his own: to God, worship; to the Superior, veneration and obedience; to one’s equal, respect; to one’s inferior, service; to all –according to measure– charity; that virtue so beautiful that neither the morning star nor the evening star can be compared to it in beauty; to shine resplendent in our communities”56.

– Demands in the formation of a religious of the IVE:

  • “We want to form the members of the Institute in great human and Christian maturity so that they may attain the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ (Eph 4:13)”57.
  • “We want to form authentically free men, masters of themselves, who, because they possess themselves, can give themselves totally”58.
  • “We want to form virtuous men (from vir and from vis: who have the strength of a man) according to the doctrine of the great masters of the spiritual life…”59.
  • “We want to form priestly souls and priests who are not ‘tributaries’60”61.
  • “We want to form a ‘school’ and not ‘loners’”62.

– According to the works of apostolate to which a religious of the IVE will have to dedicate himself:

  • “We want to love and serve, and to make Jesus Christ loved and served: His Body and His Spirit”63.
  • “We want to work for his [man’s] integral good, revealing to him his nature, his dignity, his vocation, his inalienable rights, his freedom, his eternal destiny, attaining the goal of faith, the salvation of souls”64.
  • “We want to prolong the Word”65 (through the spoken and written word).
  • “We want to dedicate ourselves to the works of the apostolate, imitating Christ who ‘proclaimed the Kingdom of God’66”67.
  • “We want, as our specific and singular end, to dedicate ourselves to the evangelization of culture”68.
  • “We want, in Christ, to seek the glory of God and the integral good of man”69.

Likewise, the remaining 25 times the expression “we want” appears in the Directory of Spirituality can be grouped under the heading of the firm interior attitude or spiritual temper of a religious of the Incarnate Word in facing life and carrying out his mission in the Church, even stating explicitly what we do not want.

– Interior attitude or spiritual temper of a religious of the IVE

  • “We want to be anchored in the most sacred mystery of the Incarnation…”70.
  • “We want to be another Incarnation of the Word in order to incarnate Him in all that is human”71.
  • “We want to draw ever new light and strength”72 (from the fact of the redemptive Incarnation).
  • “We want to base our spirituality on the absolute of God, before whom everything is as nothing73”74.
  • “We want to see ourselves and every man in Him [Christ]”75.
  • “We want to have: a) Attention to the inspirations of the Holy Spirit, like the Virgin… b) Discernment of spirits in order to accept and second the motions of the Holy Spirit and reject those of the evil spirit… c) Docility and promptness in carrying out what the Holy Spirit asks, like the Virgin…”76.
  • “We want to set aside every stance of pure humanism (humanism without transcendence), which ends up annihilating man, and every false kenoticism (self-emptying) which, under the pretext of going toward what is lower, empties itself of what is higher”77.
  • “We want to imitate more closely and represent perpetually in the Church that form of life which the Son of God chose when He became incarnate78”79.
  • “We want to imitate Jesus Christ as perfectly as possible”80.
  • “We want to imitate Him until we can truly say to others: Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ (1 Cor 11:1), it is no longer I who live, it is Christ who lives in me (Gal 2:20)”81.
  • “We want to live the exhortation of St. Ignatius of Antioch: “Stop your ears when you hear talk of anything that does not have Christ Jesus as its foundation…82”83.
  • “We want to work with all our strength to build our life in union with the legitimate Pastors, and most especially with a heartfelt adherence to the Bishop of Rome”84.
  • “We want always to seek the glory of God”85.
  • “We want to tend toward the sanctification and salvation of men, our brothers”86.
  • “We want ‘to enter into the womb of our Mother [the Virgin] and be born again’87”88.
  • “We want to learn to be firm and faithful to the call, to the vocation, above any other claim of this earth”89.
  • “We want to contribute to your joy through your firmness in the faith (2 Cor 1:24)”90.
  • “We want to count ourselves [among the religious institutes] which, although they do not belong to the hierarchical structure of the Church, belong ‘undeniably to her life and holiness’91”92.
  • “[We want93] to set aside everything that could impede or distort this unanimity of mind. In the first place, pride, by which we seek our own excellence inordinately and do not want to submit to others or recognize the excellence of others, and for that reason where there is pride only discord dwells”94.
  • “We want to preach [the content of the Word of God] certain that ‘only one who lives the Christian vocation in its fullness can be immunized against the contagion of the errors with which he comes into contact’95”96.
  • “We want always, as we have opportunity, to do good to all, especially to our brothers in the priesthood”97.
  • “We want our spirituality to be, in summary form: Not Jesus or Mary; not Mary or Jesus. Neither Jesus without Mary; nor Mary without Jesus…”98.

– We do not want

  • “We do not want ‘to leave anything untried so that the love of Christ may have supreme primacy in the Church and in society’99”100.
  • “We want nothing but to please Him [Christ]”101 (and to show Him that we love Him).
  • “We do not want to know anything outside of Her [the Church]”102.

Each of these willings is so intrinsic to our sanctification that the complete practice of one of them –according to the spirit in which they were conceived and with, of course, the mediation of the grace of God– necessarily leads to the fulfillment of others, to the sanctification of the religious, and contributes magnificently to the end of the Institute and the building up of the Kingdom of Christ.

That is why we do not think we are mistaken if we say that, if a religious takes as his motto some of these statements and makes it life through constant practice and full identification with what is stated there, he will not be slow to become holy.

*****

Let us have, then, the boldness to live heroic daily fidelity to what is ours. Let us live, not in theory but in practice, with a true will, the “manner of existing and acting of Jesus, the Word made flesh (cf. Jn 1:14)”103. Let us be firm in our convictions; let us feel fully identified with the charism and the spirit that has been bequeathed to us. Let us be promoters of everything that is in tune with our charism; let us live within our communities −small or large, far from or near to other communities− the sound traditions that remind us of the cradle where we were born to religious life and that are like flashes of light on the dark pilgrimage. Let us be self-motivated men when it comes to spreading the spirit of the Institute. Let us cultivate in ourselves availability and creativity in carrying out our proper apostolates “with generosity, discernment and seriousness”104. Let us take an interest in the missions of the Institute; let us flee with a true will from the temptation to live wrapped up105 in our little parish, in the little world of our province… Let us embrace as our own −which in truth it is− the cause of the Institute and feel as done to ourselves the injuries it may suffer.

And, at the same time, let us firmly reject whatever contradicts, dilutes, cuts back or endangers what identifies us. The Institute, although it transcends us, in some way lives in us. People will judge the Institute according to how each of us is.

That is why let us be those priests whom the Catholic Church needs today: “capable of bearing the weight of responsibilities, priests who love truth and loyalty, who respect persons, who have a sense of justice, who are faithful to their given word, who have true compassion, who are consistent, and, in particular, balanced in judgment and behavior”106. Each and every one of the religious of the Incarnate Word can and must contribute to preserving the very rich patrimony with which God has blessed the Institute, and can even increase it with a witness of life that is truly “a concrete trace that the Trinity leaves in history”107.

One day the Incarnate Word asked the sons of thunder, James and John, whether they were ready to drink the chalice. And they quickly answered with a resolute will, not a drowsy one: Possumus! So we too, when it seems to us that we are abandoned by our friends, by our superiors, by God… let us say: Possumus! When the enemies seem so strong that our defeat appears imminent… Possumus! When the struggle seems so unequal that victory is impossible… Possumus! In spite of our weakness and littleness: Possumus! Let us realize that our weakness is the strength of God. Our situation will always be fragile, individually speaking and as an Institute. But holding the hand of the Virgin, let us say forcefully, today and always: Possumus! We want what God wants!

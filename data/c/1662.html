“To persevere in this purpose until death”
Constitutions, 1

“Vocation, like faith itself, is a treasure that we carry in earthen vessels1; that is why we must guard it, as the most precious things are guarded, so that no one may rob us of this treasure, nor may it lose its beauty with the passing of time”2. With these words the Holy Father addressed the participants in the plenary assembly of the Congregation for Institutes of Consecrated Life and Societies of Apostolic Life (CICLSAL), gathered in 2017 to deal with the topic of the lack of fidelity and the consequent lack of perseverance in religious life.

The fruit of that meeting is the document recently published by the CICLSAL entitled: “The Gift of Fidelity. The Joy of Perseverance.” It was presented online this past July 73. On that occasion Emili Turú (Secretary General of the Union of Superiors General) stated “that between 2008 and 2012 the annual average of persons leaving religious life is more than 3,000 persons, some 10 a day; which represents 0.3% of all consecrated life”4 and, therefore, he went on to say, speaking of a “hemorrhage that weakens consecrated life and the very life of the Church”5—alluding to the words of Pope Francis in the same address mentioned above—would seem to be a bit of an exaggeration6. Without wishing to enter into debate about the magnitude or dimensions of the problem, we must affirm with both sides that the lack of perseverance is a dramatic reality recognized by the CICLSAL itself. And that, as the Holy Father said very well, we must guard that treasure7, because both vocation and perseverance itself are a gift of God. And whoever has received the grace of the vocation to consecrated life must, therefore, in order to be faithful to such a gift, use the means and implore from the Lord the grace to persevere in it. What St. Thomas says when speaking of perseverance in sanctifying grace applies here analogously, namely, that the help of God is necessary in order to persevere in it and to be able to keep oneself from evil until the end of life8.

In this sense, it seemed to us that the publication of this document gives all of us members of the Institute the occasion to point out once again what we have been taught from the beginning about fidelity to vocation and the means of perseverance, as well as the opportunity to dispel the vain arguments in which some take refuge to excuse their lack of perseverance and which others use to present as logical something that is not. Lastly, we must give thanks for the work of God in our souls day by day; this is imperative.

1. Fidelity to vocation and means of perseverance

Already in the first point of our Constitutions the topic of perseverance is mentioned. And so, after stating what our intention is as a religious Institute in the Church, it says explicitly and textually: “We ask the Blessed Virgin Mary to obtain for us the grace to persevere in this purpose until death.”

In fact, the very formula of profession that we make invokes the Most Holy Trinity, asking for this favor in a very particular way: “May the love and grace of the Most Holy Trinity help me to be faithful in the work that it has begun”9.

Blessed Giuseppe Allamano, for his part, told his own: “Do not forget that to persevere is a duty when we have freely accepted this state of life and have bound ourselves to it by solemn promises. It is a duty toward God, for whom we have made the vows, and it is a duty toward ourselves”10.

The fact is that not all religious persevere in their duty, and many leave. Unfortunately this is a reality in the Church that is not foreign to us.

According to the data we have, in the period from July 2019 to July 2020, 12 priests of the Institute obtained the indult of departure. That is, less than 1.4% of the current population of members of the Institute11. Although the figure in itself is not alarming, nor is it the purpose of this writing to present either a gloomy or a softened view of reality, we would like, on the one hand, for this to alert us to a latent danger and to keep us from falling into it, according to that wise warning of the Apostle: let him who thinks he stands take care lest he fall12. And, on the other hand, we want it to serve to give thanks to God and to prioritize those areas or means that are most conducive to perseverance. For it is also a reality that the Institute, even having lost those members, has increased its total number of priests with respect to the 2018-2019 period. It is an enormous grace that is impossible not to see in these times of great lack of perseverance, as the very Authorities of the Church cited above affirm! In fact, from July 2019 to September 2020 alone, 27 of our members received holy orders. And not only that, but between 90 and 100 new candidates have been received13.

That is why, before listing the possible causes of “abandonment,” we want to recall once again some basic principles to help us always remain in fidelity and dispose ourselves for the gift of perseverance.

a. The first of them is that “vocation is a gift, freely offered and freely accepted. It is a profound expression of God's love for us and, on our part, it requires in return a total love for Christ”14. That is, the religious vocation is born of divine initiative, and what matters is to correspond to it15. Therefore the whole life of a religious must be directed toward tightening that bond of love with God, a love that is unequivocally consummated on the cross. Hence, whatever draws me away, seeks to shift the focus of my attention, or, put another way, makes us place something before the love and the Cross of Christ—that simply is not the will of God.

b. The second principle follows from what our Constitutions call the foundation of our Religious Family and, therefore, the reason for being of our consecration: “We want to be founded on Jesus Christ, who has come in the flesh16, and on Christ alone, and Christ always, and Christ in everything, and Christ in everyone, and Christ All, because the rock is Christ17 and no one can lay another foundation18”19. That is: do I want to persevere? Do I want to remain firm amid the ups and downs of life, amid the crises that sooner or later come? Then the foundation, the bedrock of my life, must be Christ and not any other foundation. Perseverance is not given by superiors, nor by studies, nor by years of experience, nor by the offices I hold or have held… Fidelity to the religious vocation must be founded on Christ, if we want to remain firm when the rains fall and the torrents come and the winds blow20.And in a first and fundamental consideration, it is a grace of God. All this requires on our part a continual adherence to our Lord, the search for his Most Holy Will in everything, and the responsibility of a pure and blameless life. It is a vain illusion to think that one perseveres or will persevere in one's vocation by sheer muscle (by one's own merit, by one's natural and supernatural virtues), because one comes from a good family, because the superiors are favorable to him or his subjects follow him, because his good works vouch for him, etc.

c. And as a third principle we would like to make explicit here something that is implicit in the two previous ones, and that is faith. Only in faith have we been able to take the first step in response to God's call; only in faith have we set out on the path of following the Incarnate Word and placed ourselves at God's disposal according to the vows we profess. That is, in faith we have pronounced our fiat to everything God may dispose and forever. In faith, too, we must persevere. “Only God can keep alive in us the gift of vocation. Only He can, through his Spirit, overcome the weaknesses we experience again and again”21. Therefore, also from faith, we must remember that God is faithful and that if he has called us to religious life in this particular Institute, he will not fail to provide us with all the means we need to persevere in this holy vocation and to advance to the end according to its demands. Did God not say: Though a thousand fall at your side and ten thousand at your right hand, you will not be touched22?

2. Vain arguments – true causes

Having established these principles, let us now say what some of the causes are for which some abandon the religious vocation. Not without first clarifying that we are not referring here to those who recognize, after serious discernment made at the right time, that they simply do not have a vocation (whether because they lack suitability, or because they recognize that God is calling them to sanctify themselves in another vocation or in another Institute). Rather, we are referring to those who, after having seriously and conscientiously discerned their vocation to religious life, knowing that it was God's will for them, professed their religious vows forever in the Institute, and in a time of desolation made a change23—some only a few years after perpetual profession or almost immediately after ordination, others after many years of religious and priestly life.

Regarding this group of religious—but at the level of the whole Church—we read in a 2013 report prepared by the secretary of the CICLSAL: “that it is almost impossible to identify such causes with precision. The reason? It is very simple: we do not have totally reliable data. Sometimes, what is written is one thing and what is lived is another. Moreover, in many cases what the documents say, which are available at the end of a procedure, does not necessarily coincide with the real cause of the departures”24. However, according to the documentation the Dicastery does possess, the main causes mentioned for abandoning religious life are: crisis of faith, crisis in community life and crisis in affectivity25. The same Prelate affirmed this again recently when Rome Reports interviewed him this past August 7, 2020, on the occasion of the presentation of the document we mentioned earlier26.

For his part, the Holy Father, already in 2017 in his address to the CICLSAL, summarized in one paragraph some sociocultural factors that contribute to the problem: “We live immersed in the so-called culture of the fragmentary, of the provisional, which can lead to living ‘à la carte’ [according to one's own taste] and to being slaves to fashions. This culture induces the need always to have the ‘side doors’ open toward other possibilities; it feeds consumerism and forgets the beauty of the simple and austere life, often provoking a great existential emptiness. A strong practical relativism has also spread, according to which everything is judged in terms of a self-fulfillment often foreign to the values of the Gospel. We live in societies where economic rules replace moral ones, dictate laws and impose their own systems of reference at the expense of the values of life; a society where the dictatorship of money and profit advocates a view of existence by which whoever does not produce is discarded”27.

But we must add that all these factors—very true and influential—are not the only cause, and that we cannot refer to them to reassure ourselves in trying to understand this phenomenon, to the point of coming to see as “normal” or “logical” what is not. Emili Turú mentioned something of this in the presentation of the book when he said that “consecrated life is in crisis not only because society is in crisis but because the Church is in crisis”28. Unfortunately the document does not address this topic, but it is certain that the lack of clear ideas and of capacity for leadership affects the perseverance of religious.

John Paul II was a great promoter of consecrated and priestly life, and throughout his 26 years of pontificate, through his writings and on his numerous journeys, he addressed religious and priests with special affection and dedication as that portion “indispensable for the good of the Church and of men”29. Already in 1990 St. John Paul II, the 1st centenary of whose birth we celebrate this year, saw it clearly and affirmed: “nor are certain signs of deterioration in the discipline of ecclesial life and with regard to canonical legislation on priestly and religious life […] a help in overcoming such situations, nor are certain mistaken conceptions of liberation”30. “Thus we sometimes see how those who disturb and weaken the Church on this point [of vocations] are not so much her enemies from outside as some of her sons from within, who claim to be her free promoters”31. That is why “the vocational crisis,” the Pope went on to say, “should not be attributed principally to a lack of generosity on the part of young people, but rather to the fact that a prophetic sign of the presence of God, which is precisely the first dimension of religious life, is not sufficiently perceived in religious life”32.

In that same year, Fr. Benedict Groeschel, founder of the Franciscan Friars and the Franciscan Sisters of the Renewal, referred to it in his book “The Reform of Renewal”: “Beyond the apathy [of today's world toward the clergy] there is a much more serious problem, and that is growing cynicism. Cynicism has always been the underlying problem of the clergy, because cynics are disillusioned idealists. […] When the leaders and teachers of religious faith begin to show signs of confusion and indecision, when they sound an uncertain trumpet, who is going to follow them? When the positive response toward the clergy and their ministry diminishes, then cynicism increases through the growing belief that they have been deceived by God”33.

It is fitting, then, to be warned about the dangers and subtle deceptions in which not a few religious let themselves be trapped, with the consequent abandonment of their own vocation. It is not our intention to make an exhaustive list of all of them, but we would like to include those criteria that we consider most diametrically opposed to the principles we listed at the beginning, and others which, not infrequently, are the most common arguments one hears.

We said that our vocation is a divine invitation to imitate the way of life of Christ and his apostles, which also means embracing the cross as a means of union with God through the vows and through the inevitable trials that God in his Providence permits in order to bring us to heaven. Here come in all the temptations and spiritual trials through which our spiritual progress and good must pass: apostolic failures, declining health, moral sufferings (calumny, loss of one's good name, mistreatment, lack of recognition, humiliations, etc.), family losses, loneliness, the difficulties of the mission, etc. Each one could make his own list. This makes manifest—as if we needed it—how necessary prayer (in all its forms) is in order to persevere in the struggle, to persevere in the authentic search for the will of God, not to fall into the temptation of discouragement, to obtain divine help and overcome difficulties of whatever kind; in short, to be strong and to persevere to the end. St. John of the Cross told one of his spiritual directees: “as long as prayer is not lacking, God will take care of its affairs, since it belongs to no other master, nor ever shall”34. That is, prayer is of capital importance when it comes to disposing oneself to receive the gift of perseverance in one's vocation. Without prayer it is impossible to fulfill the Lord's commandment: remain in my love35. For what He tells us in that same discourse will always be true: whoever remains in me and I in him bears much fruit; for apart from me you can do nothing36.

Prayer, the care of the interior life (applying oneself to work on concrete resolutions of the spiritual life, receiving spiritual direction, having recourse to the sacrament of penance, striving to attend the Spiritual Exercises every year, etc.) is an inherent part of that care of vocation to which Pope Francis referred in the address already cited. That is why he went on to say: “Such care is the task first of all of each one of us, who are called to follow Christ more closely with faith, hope and charity, cultivated each day in prayer and strengthened by a good theological and spiritual formation, which defends against fashions and the culture of the ephemeral and allows us to walk firm in the faith. On this foundation it is possible to practice the evangelical counsels and to have the same sentiments as Christ37. Vocation is a gift we have received from the Lord, who has set his gaze upon us and loved us38, calling us to follow him in consecrated life, and it is at the same time a responsibility of the one who has received this gift”39.

Without doubt, the care of the spiritual life is like the axis and support of consecrated life. That is why Blessed Paolo Manna explained: “The Missionary who wishes to live and remain at the height of his vocation must nourish himself constantly on this spirit of faith, enlightening and inflaming himself with meditation on our Holy Religion. He must receive from God, whose instrument he is, through continual prayer, the grace he needs for his ministry, and without which he can do nothing with regard to the eternal salvation of his own soul and of those he went to evangelize. Therefore, meditation and prayer constitute the strength of the Missionary; the only true sources and causes of his zeal, of his perseverance and of his success”40.

Hence, abandoning religious life often implies a prior neglect of the life of prayer, which little by little leads to a rupture in one's relationship with our Lord. It should not astonish us, then, if one who does not have a solid life of piety does not persevere in our Institute.

Unfortunately, there is no lack of those who tend to downplay this and prefer instead to argue that the fault lies with the superiors who did not know how to give the necessary accompaniment, who did not know how to discern in time that such persons had no vocation, or because it was they who sent him to a very difficult mission, etc. There are even those who, without any serious foundation, readily blame the formation and the Seminary, and forget to look within themselves at their failures of fidelity to grace. All these ‘arguments,’ although they may be true in some cases and are of course possible—because superiors are certainly fallible, and sometimes very much so—do not mean that, if one really has a vocation, he will lack the means to persevere even in the most difficult situations and the most adverse circumstances. That “would be to do God an injury”41. For “the Lord will never abandon those who love him when they venture for Him alone”42. Let us think of our dear John Paul II, of the difficulties he had in his formation and in his “Seminary,” and how, through his fidelity to God, he managed to amaze us with the man we all knew. That is why it is no less true that “if God calls and if there is true love in giving oneself to him, he will also give the graces to carry through every difficulty that must be overcome on the way to heaven. That is why, in the midst of crosses, good religious are the happiest men on earth”43. In fact, Christian hagiography abounds in examples of saints who became such in the midst of great difficulties within their own communities: they persevered in their holy resolutions and were faithful until death to the word given to God on the day of their vows. The rest are excuses.

We must be convinced that the “cross of Christ, with all that [Jesus] did and suffered on it, is at the beginning, development and final perseverance of every consecrated vocation. That many consecrated persons are afraid of the cross of Christ is a more than eloquent sign of the decadence of consecrated life and of the reason for the lack of vocations in many communities”44.

That is why we want to emphasize once again that one must devote oneself in a special way to prayer, to the practice of charity and piety, to self-denial, to fulfilling better the obligations of one's state45. For the lack of perseverance not infrequently indicates a lack of love for the cross or a wish to escape precisely from labors, from humiliations… and a giving in to the spirit of the evil world; making the religious forget that union with God, for which we have been created, is obtained through the cross, is consummated on the cross, and is marked for all eternity with the seal of the cross46.

In the moment of trial each one must know how to accept purification and self-emptying as essential aspects of following Christ crucified and as an integral part of his vocation. The cross is part of the plan. And if we stop to think about it carefully, the trial itself is like a providential instrument of formation that God uses to draw us more to Himself.

That is why perseverance is ultimately a question of faith. “Do we not believe that Christ has sanctified us and sent us? Do we not believe that He remains with us even though we carry this treasure in fragile vessels and we ourselves are in need of his mercy, of which we are ministers for others? Do we not believe that He acts in us, at least if we carry out his work, and that He will give growth to what we have laboriously sown according to his Spirit? And do we not believe that He will also grant the gift of the priestly vocation to all those who are to work with us and take over from us, above all if we know how to rekindle the gift we have received through the laying on of hands? […] Christ will not abandon those who have entrusted themselves to Him, those who entrust themselves to Him each day”47.

The rock is Christ. How many religious, priests, monks, brothers there are who, through the crucible of spiritual and apostolic trials, through a certain immaturity and psychological fragility, through not “remembering that common life requires sacrifice, and can become a form of maxima poenitentia”48, begin to feel “a certain uneasiness with regard to the regularity of ‘common life,’ which may seem to them too rigid, distant, anachronistic in relation to certain demands of contemporary mentality and sensibility; and then it seems to them legitimate and valid to share concepts, lifestyles and modes of behavior that are opposed, of themselves, to the austere but wise regime that the vows and our consecration to God, made with an undivided heart for the kingdom of heaven, require of us in every circumstance”49. Such people forget that “if we are religious it is in order to imitate the Incarnate Word, chaste, poor, obedient and son of Mary”50, and that implied in our religious profession is the fact that we become “victims with the Victim”51. Then they begin to make rereadings of the Rule, which “carries with it the risk of substituting a particular interpretation for the text of the Rule itself or, at least, of obscuring the simplicity and purity with which it was written”52.

We must not be naïve: the enemy of our souls is very cunning in “exploiting” that communal or apostolic uneasiness, and he uses a variety of situations to cause divisions and, at times, irreparable separations. For example, the promise of something better—which in reality is a mere whim—can draw us away from, and in fact separate us from, that which truly deserves our most loyal adherence. We must be attentive and realize that the evil one always seeks to lay another foundation that is not Jesus Christ. His most common objective is to distract us from the focus of our life, namely, Jesus Christ; and with incredible tricks and very subtly he seeks to remove us from our mission, or at least to divert us from it little by little. That is why the Holy Father pointed out how very necessary it is “that we keep our gaze fixed on the Lord, always attentive to walk according to the logic of the Gospel and not to yield to the criteria of worldliness. Often great infidelities begin with small deviations or distractions”53.

The devil will use the artifices of the imagination, the recesses of our sensibility, the suggestion of the world, to seek by every means to discourage us in our vocation: that there is no spiritual progress, that we have not been able to achieve what we had planned (in terms of apostolate, spiritual life, studies, etc.); he will even use the very ideal of religious life or community life that drives us in order to divert us from our commitments (vows), and he will make of tiredness, of misunderstandings and of the possible injustices that can occur and do occur in Christian life a means to make us conclude that there is no future, that there is no potential, that everything is a disaster, that it is better to go somewhere else, etc.; and what began with discouragement ended with abandoning the path of total self-giving to God.

We must “be men of discernment”54, and not fall into the pessimistic attitude whereby “because of the tree of difficulties we lose sight of the forest of things that are going well”55. Many have fallen into this!

In the face of these possible temptations, and without intending to offer a simplistic solution, it is fitting to recall and plant in our soul the 5th rule for the discernment of spirits that St. Ignatius gives for the first week of the Spiritual Exercises: “in time of desolation never make a change, but remain firm and constant in the resolutions and determination in which one was in the preceding consolation.” And to read again what the saint goes on to say in the following rules: “it is very profitable to change ourselves intensely against the desolation itself… and to extend ourselves in some suitable way of doing penance”; “let him consider that… he can resist the various agitations and temptations of the enemy, with the divine help, which always remains with him, even though he does not clearly feel it”; “let him work at remaining in patience.”

These rules, which we consider every year, which we preach to others, which we have heard since we first set foot in the Institute, must be applied to the life and situation of each one, knowing that the enemy, with his great power of suggestion, goes on sowing falsehoods in one's understanding “and goes on hurrying him along and deceiving him most subtly with plausible things”56. We must not let ourselves be deceived. Things do not improve unless there is, on the part of each of the members of the Institute, of each Province and of each community in particular, an ever greater fidelity to Jesus Christ.

That is why disposing oneself to receive the gift of perseverance requires, in addition to a tested discernment rooted in prayer, a solid intellectual formation. And it also requires certain human virtues that lie at the base of the whole formation of the consecrated person and in which we must continue to grow, such as fidelity to one's given word, which is a supremely valued good in any human society. And moreover, if that word—that of persevering in our vows in the Institute of the Incarnate Word—was given to none other than God, and was given publicly, before the Church, and forever, and without restrictions or conditions, constituting what St. Ignatius calls an “immutable election”57, we must keep it always and at all costs, even if it costs us “our skin and all the rest,” as the holy doctor St. John of the Cross said58.

In addition, we want to add another temptation which, although common to many souls, perhaps tempts religious priests more because it presents itself to them as easier, and that is the idea that if one leaves the Institute he will be able to ‘manage on his own.’ Sometimes the promises of a future made by persons outside the Institute, and the possibility of having certain worldly ‘securities,’ are quite attractive. It is not the same to live on Providence as to receive a check in the bank every month. It feels much better to know how things are going to go than not to know, even when that feeling is based on something that is improbable and, more often than not, also false. It feels better to move forward with a certain autonomous control than to walk armed only with trust and hope in God, without having any control over what will happen… since we are under the guidance of a superior to whom we profess obedience.

In this regard, one need simply say what C. S. Lewis said: “almost all vices are rooted in the future. Gratitude is turned toward the past and love toward the present; fear, greed, lust and ambition are turned toward the future”59. We cannot be so unwary as to think that the devil will keep his promises… This means that we expose ourselves to being terribly deceived and incredibly disappointed if we try to anticipate what will be. These deceptions and consequent disappointments can come from a thousand things: ‘how much I am going to enjoy having money in my pockets’; ‘I will no longer have to ask permission to do whatever I please’; ‘how much I am going to enjoy the advantages that working in this or that diocese or in this or that position will give me’; ‘what bliss to be able to live in this or that country,’ etc. We must open our eyes and realize that we are not masters of future contingents!

So it is also fitting to recall what the Proper Law points out very clearly to guard us against that idealism and to teach us to “be realistic men. It is necessary to root out false beliefs that mistake the path and falsify community life:

– to believe and demand that everything must come from others. Against this, one must discover with gratitude all that one has received and is receiving from others;

– not to be “consumers” but builders of community;

– to know oneself capable of helping and being helped, of replacing and being replaced.

– to channel the natural first charm that fraternal and shared life exercises on the young, making them aware of the sacrifices that living in community demands; but in the certainty that when one loses oneself for one's brothers, one finds oneself.

– to point out that whoever claims to live an independent life, on the margins of the community, has certainly not set out on the sure path of the perfection of his state.

– to dispel the illusion: the “ideal,” perfect “community” does not exist yet; it will exist in Heaven. Here one builds upon human weakness. It is always possible to improve and to walk together toward the community that knows how to live forgiveness and love. Unity is established at the price of reconciliation. The imperfect situation of communities must not dishearten”60.

St. John of the Cross, for his part, in writing his Counsels to a religious on how to reach perfection, includes this wise piece of advice that we would do well to keep in mind. It reads: “he must understand that all those who are in the convent are nothing but craftsmen whom God has placed there only to work and polish him in mortification, and that some are to work him with words, telling him what he would not like to hear; others with deeds, doing against him what he would not like to suffer; others with their temperament, being bothersome and burdensome to him in themselves and in their way of acting; others with their thoughts, his feeling in them or thinking of them that they neither esteem nor love him.

And all these mortifications and annoyances he must suffer with interior patience, keeping silent for the love of God, understanding that he did not come to Religion for anything else but that they might work him in this way and he might be worthy of heaven. For if it were not for this, there would be no reason to come to Religion, but rather to stay in the world seeking his own comfort, honor, reputation and ease”61.

The Spiritual Father of the Institute told us clearly: “What must absolutely be avoided is the weakening of consecrated life, which consists not so much in numerical decline as in the loss of spiritual adherence to the Lord and to one's own vocation and mission”62.

For this reason, those who are superiors are strongly urged to be fervent in living and genuinely transmitting the spirit of the Congregation63, to instill the family spirit in the communities, being true spiritual fathers, knowing how to comfort their subjects in temptations and help them in their needs.

For it must also be added that, independently of the various stages of life or of age, a religious may go through critical situations, either because of various external factors —a change of place or of office, difficulty in work or apostolic failure, misunderstanding, marginalization, etc.—, or for more strictly personal reasons, such as physical or psychological illness, spiritual aridity, bereavements, problems in interpersonal relationships, strong temptations, crises of faith or of identity, a feeling of insignificance, or other similar things. When fidelity becomes more difficult, the person must be offered the help of greater trust and greater love, both at the personal and at the community level. It is above all in those moments that the affectionate closeness of the superior becomes necessary64.

There is no lack of those who argue that their lack of perseverance was due to the fact that ‘they left him alone.’ That is why, although the importance of the accompaniment of one's brothers in religion, and especially on the part of superiors, is neither denied nor diminished, it is also true that “there can be no loneliness when He (God) fills the heart and life”65. It is also fitting to call to mind what the Mystical Doctor of Fontiveros wrote to Doña Juana de Pedraza when she complained of “her sorrows and ills and the loneliness she felt”66: “It is all knocks and blows on the soul so that it may love more, which cause more prayer and spiritual sighs to God, so that He may fulfill what the soul asks of Him.[…] Those who truly love God, He takes care of their affairs, without their being anxious about them”67.

3. Take courage!

We are not unaware of the real difficulties that confront all the members of the Institute in their effort to be configured to Christ, to be coherent, to remain faithful over the years and in the midst of hardly encouraging circumstances—socially and ecclesially speaking. All of this is certainly a true trial and does not cease to be painful; but it must not lead us to discouragement. Have we perhaps forgotten the “providential view of life”68 that must characterize us? It is one of the non-negotiable elements attached to the charism.

We must know how to accept these trials, and if earlier we said that we had to be men of discernment and realistic men, now we add that we must be supernatural men. Accepting with faith and abandonment to Providence the designs of God regarding each one of us, the Institute, the Church and the world.

So it is neither a matter of an unreal optimism nor of a pessimism that is in contradiction with Providence; but of a healthy Christian realism that accepts the reality of one's own situation, of the Institute and of society, in order to strive to give of oneself as much as one can so as to raise it up in the name of Christ with industriousness and patience.

We must realize that “lack of perseverance is something that has always happened and will happen in the Church.” St. John Paul II already said it more than three decades ago: “It is not the first time that, in the course of history, religious life has encountered serious difficulties. In some periods it has gone through analogous crises, sometimes even more difficult ones, and it has always emerged from them more generous and more alive, after having deepened and enhanced its own character”69. And for that very reason, we must not let ourselves be discouraged by peremptory pronouncements that only obscure the beauty of consecrated life, discouraging not only those who have embraced consecrated life but also potential candidates. ‘Departures,’ the lack of vocations and the aging of members in many religious congregations and in many dioceses of the world are serious challenges for each Institute and for the whole Church. That is beyond doubt. However, these are not new phenomena in the long experience of the Church. History teaches us that, by generally unforeseeable paths, the radical ‘newness’ of the message of the cross is capable of inspiring many to renounce everything for the kingdom of God, in order to possess the pearl of great price70.

Without doubt, we admit that there is no magic recipe for persevering in one's vocation; because it is definitely a grace from Heaven that must be implored in season and out of season71. We all need the grace of God in order to will and do good perseveringly. But one must also be determined and use the means (so important are these that they seem to tell us that “he perseveres who wants to,” that is, he who uses them):

– To be assiduous in prayer and diligent in the care of the interior life. This includes “entering daily into the crucified and crucifying love of the Incarnate Word. Only He can keep alive in us the gift of vocation. Only He can, through his Spirit, overcome the weaknesses experienced again and again”72. One must persevere constantly in the practice and spirit of the vows as well as in fidelity in the celebration of the liturgy.

– To do penance: bodily and interior. As the Proper Law very clearly indicates to us: “we must hold exterior penance in the highest esteem: regarding eating and drinking, regarding the manner of sleeping, ‘giving sensible pain’73 to the flesh by means of hair shirts74, disciplines, etc.” 75. But “above all interior penance, metanoia76, that is, the intimate and total change and renewal of the whole man in all his feeling, judging and disposing”77.

– To deny oneself and give oneself in trusting abandonment to the service of God. It would be a grave error to let our arms drop in the mission, to diminish in our self-giving, to distrust God's help. That is why at this point we would like to recall those heartfelt words that John Paul II addressed to some religious: “Persevere in love! Persevere in sacramental grace and in the mission, at once demanding and wonderful, of the salvation of souls. Let your first means of perseverance be apostolic zeal. The priest must have an ‘eschatological’ view of existence and of history, and must live with this perspective. Souls must be evangelized, saved and sanctified: this is the will of God”78.

– To be faithful to the patrimony of the Institute. “To love one's vocation is to love the Church, it is to love one's own Institute and to feel the community as one's true family”79. In this sense all the holy founders and the Proper Law itself strongly counsel—in addition to prayer and recollection or the rejection of the spirit of the world—clinging firmly and faithfully to the Constitutions, to the spirit of the Congregation, and growing fond of its interests. “What will make you saints?” St. Peter Julian Eymard asked some religious while preaching the Spiritual Exercises to them: “The rule, your religious rule, the rule of the congregation to which you belong. For you holiness comes down to practicing it, because the rule is the will of God. […] What [God] wants to see in you is perfect religious of such an Institute, and that you be its living incarnation. In heaven you will be crowned, not as holy men, but as holy religious”80. Therefore, an element underlying the care of our vocation is full, conscientious and fervent adherence to the discipline of the Institute, which includes the constant observance of common life. To be taken with the prospect of a religious life or a priesthood less austere in its demands of sacrifice and renunciation is already, in a way, to capitulate. And joined to this means is that of purity of thought through orderly study and good reading, in order to discern and guard oneself against the ideologies that try to turn us away from our purpose. “How important it is to remain in ‘divine intimacy’ through the meditation of serious and profound books that set the soul on fire with the love of God and keep it serene and enthusiastic in whatever situation or circumstance it may find itself”81, said the Pope.

– The practice of tender devotion to Most Holy Mary. The yes of Most Holy Mary, pronounced on the day of the Incarnation and maintained throughout her whole life, must be for us a stimulus and a help in our total self-giving to God. In the daily recitation of the Holy Rosary we must ask Most Holy Mary for the courage to stand with her beside the cross and to accept the dialectic of the cross, which is nothing other than asking for the grace to persevere in this purpose until death. Our Lady is our hope.

At present we are almost 900 members in the Institute of the Incarnate Word. Let us know how “to celebrate and give thanks together for the common gift of vocation and mission”82. Let us rejoice in every vocation with which God blesses our Institute and renew our efforts “to persevere and excel in the vocation to which we were called by God, for a more abundant holiness of the Church”83.

Let our prayer, then—for one another—go up to implore “perseverance in good, which, although it may meet misunderstandings and obstacles, in the end always reaches a goal of light, of fruitfulness and of peace. It is what St. Paul reminded the Galatians: Whoever sows in the spirit will reap eternal life from the spirit. Let us not grow tired of doing good; for in due time we shall reap the harvest if we do not lose heart”84.

Let us listen to the words of John Paul II to some religious, in the hope that each one may feel them addressed especially to himself: “Never fall into discouragement”85. “In these present circumstances do not doubt your vocation. The God who has invited you to leave everything for love of him is a faithful God: he will never fail you! …remain firm in your vocation, in the certainty that it is the surest means of fulfilling the will of God”86.

May Most Holy Mary obtain for us the graces we need for our sanctification and for the religious prosperity of the Institute and of our missions. May she help us to be faithful to the divine call and make us understand all the beauty, the joy and the strength of a priesthood lived without reserve in dedication and self-immolation for the service of God and of souls. May she help us, finally, to say after her example: Yes to the will of God, even when it is demanding, even when it is incomprehensible, even when it is painful for us.

We leave you a prayer composed by Blessed John Henry Newman:

“God has created me to render Him a particular service. He has entrusted to me a task that He has entrusted to no one else. I have a mission. Perhaps I will never know it in this life, but I will be told it in the next. In some way I am necessary for His purposes, as necessary in my place as an archangel in his, and it is true that, if I fail, He can raise up another, just as He can make sons of Abraham out of stones. Yet I am part of this great undertaking. I am a link in a chain, a bond of connection between persons. He has not created me for nothing. I will do good, I will do His work. I will be an angel of peace, a preacher of truth in my own place, without aiming at anything else when I do so than to keep His commandments and serve Him in my vocation. Therefore I will trust in Him; wherever I may be, I can never be cast aside. If I am in sickness, my sickness can serve Him; if in perplexity, my perplexity can serve Him. If I am in sorrow, my sorrow can serve Him. My sickness, or perplexity, or sorrow may be the necessary causes of some great end that lies far beyond us. God does nothing in vain. He may lengthen my life or He may shorten it; He knows what He is doing. He may take my friends from me and He may cast me among strangers. He may make me feel desolate, sink my spirit, hide the future from me; even so, He knows what He is doing. […]. O Emmanuel, O Wisdom, I give myself to You. I entrust myself entirely to You. Deign to fulfill Your high purposes in me, whatever they may be. I was born to serve You, to be Yours, to be Your instrument. Let me be a blind instrument. I do not ask to see, I do not ask to know; I ask simply to be used”87.

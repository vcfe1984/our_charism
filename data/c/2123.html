The thought of St. Thomas Aquinas in St. John of the Cross

Today we are invaded by false mystics who, although they apparently study a great deal, are greatly mistaken1. They are those who “do not know the way (which is the Word) to descend from themselves toward Him, in order to be able to ascend to Him. Ignorant, then, of this way, they think themselves lofty and luminous like the stars, when in reality they have fallen to the ground and their heart has been darkened. They do not seek the Maker of the universe with a spirit of piety, and that is why they do not find Him”2.

This was not the case with the Angelic Doctor, who, besides being an extraordinary philosopher, was also a great theologian who defended and explained the divine truths of the faith with lofty mastery, to the point of earning him the title of “Light of the Church and of the whole world”3. His, “rather than the technical methodology of a master,” St. John Paul II clarified, “was the methodology of the Saint, who lives the Gospel in its fullness, in which charity is everything. Love of God, the supreme source of all truth; love of neighbor, God’s masterpiece; love of created things, which are also precious coffers full of treasures that God has poured into them. That was the inspiring force of all his zeal as a scholar and the secret impulse of his total self-giving as a consecrated person. Indeed, the gigantic intellectual effort of this master of thought was stimulated, sustained and guided by a heart filled with love of God and of neighbor. ‘Per ardorem caritatis datur cognitio veritatis’4”5.

Nevertheless, there has been no lack of those who pigeonhole St. Thomas Aquinas in the sphere of philosophy, making of him a sort of “Christian Aristotle,” and who are surprised that there are those who want to find in his works the principles of mystical theology. There are even many who make a very unsupernatural and anti-mystical reading of and commentary on his writings, steering the spirit in a direction very different from that intended by the Aquinate6.

Those who think this way are very probably not at all familiar with St. Thomas; they have not read his treatises on the Trinity, the Incarnation, the Eucharist, grace, the theological virtues, the gifts of the Holy Spirit; they have not opened his commentaries on St. Paul, St. John, the Psalms, the book of Job, Isaiah; they are unaware of his devotional opuscula, his prayers, his Office of the Blessed Sacrament; perhaps they do not know his life, they know nothing of his nights spent before the Tabernacle, nor of his raptures and his eminent contemplation, which compelled him to say of his Summae that they were nothing but straw in comparison with what he glimpsed. He himself “gladly acknowledged that he had learned more in prayer than in study7, and he kept the sense of divine transcendence so alive that he set as a primary condition, prior to any theological investigation, this principle: ‘in this life we know God all the more perfectly the better we understand that He surpasses every intellectual capacity’8”9.

We wish to emphasize, then, that the doctrine St. Thomas imparted from his chair was corroborated by the testimony of his life. “In this way, all his theology was prayer, was conversation with God, was contemplation of God”10. That is why his works –St. John Paul II affirmed– reveal to us a “thinker capable of the boldest speculative flights, the mystic accustomed to drinking directly from the very source of all truth the answer to the deepest questions of the human spirit.

[…] Whoever approaches St. Thomas cannot disregard this testimony that emerges from his life; what is more, he must set out courageously in his footsteps with the commitment to imitate his examples, if he wishes to come to taste the most hidden and savory fruits of his doctrine”11.

What we have just affirmed is particularly challenging for those who, like us, are exhorted not only to assimilate “the doctrine of the great masters of the spiritual life”12 but also to follow their examples in order to “seek the city that is to come… perfect union with Christ, that is, holiness”13.

1. Theology and Mysticism

It is fitting now −by way of a preliminary note− to state that “with the term theology we indicate all the elements of a reflection on God and His mysteries in the life of the Church, whereas to mysticism we reserve the elaboration of the data of that experience of faith, undoubtedly higher, lived intimately by the Christian”14. In this sense, theology and Mysticism are not two different things, they are not two sciences that run parallel; for Mysticism as a science is the vital and practical exposition of what Theology teaches us speculatively about the supreme work of the divinization of the soul, or the ascent of the soul to God15. We can say, then, that mysticism is a proper and specialized sector of theology itself; “it is its summit, its crown”16. Understood in this way, the experience of the mystics comes to confirm and vivify the theological teachings on the spiritual growth of the soul, up to the transforming union17.

The Angelic Doctor was a mystic. So much so that Menéndez-Reigada goes as far as to affirm that the very “theological work of St. Thomas is essentially mystical, as mystical is the participation in the divine life and that ascent of the soul to God”18. That is why not a few highlight the powerful influence of the Angelic Doctor on the great mystics, among whom St. John of the Cross is the greatest of all.

Therefore, although St. Thomas is an outstanding reference point for philosophy and theology, and St. John of the Cross is one for mysticism, from the outset it seems right to us to apply the title of theologian and of mystic to St. Thomas and to St. John of the Cross respectively, but without exclusivism.

“St. John of the Cross, and the mystics in general, look rather to the practical and experiential side of the soul in its becoming ‘deiform’19, almost leaving aside the speculative side, which is the properly scientific one; so we should not be surprised that, in studying St. John of the Cross, we notice that the gifts of the Holy Spirit are hardly mentioned, when almost everything he teaches us is the proper effect of those very gifts. We could say as much of grace. The fact is that he is not interested in investigating its causes or in declaring its proper nature, but in describing, as far as possible, the living and transcendent reality that the soul gradually discovers within itself, and the practical means leading to perfect union with God in this life, which is the end he sets himself.

St. Thomas, on the contrary, poses and resolves the human problem in all its extent and complexity, by a rigorously scientific method; solidly establishing the principles, he analyzes the causes, records the effects, and does not stop to describe the phenomena that accompany such effects. That is why, to anyone who studied him superficially, it might seem that St. Thomas is not a mystic, when in reality he is the incomparable Master of Mysticism, as he is of Theology”20.

Our purpose in this article is to demonstrate the fact that the doctrine of the Angelic Doctor and the doctrine of the Mystical Doctor are not only in complete agreement, but that both systems −each with its singular richness− make a unique contribution that in some way defines authentic Christian spirituality and gives it a singular stamp.

To this end, we must consider the presence of Thomistic thought or, rather, of some of the fundamental doctrines of the Thomistic synthesis most closely related to the spiritual life, in the writings of St. John of the Cross.

2. St. John of the Cross, a Thomist

Let us begin, then, by saying that “the scientific theological atmosphere that was breathed at the University of Salamanca in its best times, when St. John of the Cross attended its lecture halls, was certainly Thomistic”21. That is why the works of the Aquinate were the text that the doctors commented on and the students studied, and which both the former and the latter recognized as the indisputable authority in scholastic questions. But not only did he take the first year of theology at the University of Salamanca where, as we have just said, Thomism was the dominant current, but at the same time John of the Cross complemented his formation at the college of St. Andrew with the teachings of various Carmelite authors, and there, even allowing for clear divergences that concern philosophical topics rather, Thomism was the system followed by most of the Carmelite masters22. The Thomistic atmosphere in which St. John of the Cross was educated is therefore undeniable. What is more, it was during those years when the Mystic of Fontiveros attended the university of Salamanca that St. Thomas Aquinas was proclaimed Doctor of the Church (1567).

But not only did he have a Thomistic formation; he was “eminently Thomistic in all his doctrine”23 and followed him “always most faithfully”24.

Now, “the presence of Thomistic thought in the writings of St. John of the Cross cannot be measured by explicit quotations; they are few, practically two, and moreover from rather unrepresentative writings. The whole synthesis of St. John of the Cross rests, however, on the philosophical and theological conception of Scholasticism, which has in St. Thomas its greatest exponent. Almost every time the Mystical Doctor refers to ‘the theologians,’ without giving names, he has the Aquinate in mind”25, affirms the great scholar of the figure and themes of St. John of the Cross, Eulogio Pacho. Fr. Juan González Arintero also agrees with this statement, saying that John of the Cross had so assimilated the St. Thomas of the Salamanca schools that, although he quotes him explicitly only a few times, in everything he seems inspired by or supported by him, and we see him expound the purest Thomistic doctrine26. By way of illustration we present here one example of the many that could be cited27. The Mystical Doctor says in his book the Ascent of the Mount, when speaking of how faith is a dark night for the soul: “Faith, the theologians say, is a certain and obscure habit of the soul. And the reason it is an obscure habit is that it makes us believe truths revealed by God Himself, which are above all natural light and exceed all human understanding beyond any proportion”28. This statement, fundamental for understanding the whole doctrine of St. John of the Cross, particularly for the Ascent and the Night, is an echo of the classical scholastic thought on the nature of faith expressed by St. Thomas in the Summa Theologiae II-II, 1-4; or in the Summa Contra Gentiles 3, 40; or in De Veritate 14, 1 (“because the truths of faith exceed human reason. That is why they do not fall within man’s contemplation unless God reveals them”), etc.

The fact is that in reality “St. John of the Cross was a good disciple of St. Thomas: faithful and original; because, basing himself on traditionally valid principles, he knew how to make his way into the field of mysticism with a sense of balance, of renewed wonder and of theological orientation. He even created an original system, describing and elaborating the effects of grace in those souls who live only in God and for God”29.

The whole doctrine of St. John of the Cross is firmly rooted in the purest current of Catholic thought. Let it serve as an indication that among the literary-doctrinal sources the saint used for his writings30 are found the “holy doctors”31 (an expression by which the saint denotes the Holy Fathers); the ascetical writers the saint knew; “some points of scholastic theology”32 −as he himself points out in the Prologue of the Spiritual Canticle− referring especially to Thomistic doctrine; Sacred Scripture (with 1653 biblical citations, of which 1538 are explicit and 115 implicit); and finally, as the supreme norm and unfailing touchstone of his orthodoxy: “the judgment of Holy Mother Church”33.

Eulogio Pacho affirms that “John of the Cross’s predilection for St. Thomas among the scholastics is well established by the fact that he is the only one explicitly mentioned. This assessment is not contradicted by the fact that the two most representative and substantial quotations correspond to writings considered apocryphal today. They were not so in the Saint’s time, and he values them precisely because he thought they were genuine works of the Angelic Doctor”34.

One of these explicit mentions is the case of the famous quotation from St. Thomas that St. John of the Cross uses to describe the ten degrees of love in the Dark Night (Book 2, chs. 19-10). The writing that the Master of faith follows very closely is, as we said, an apocryphal work of St. Thomas35, entitled De decem gradibus amoris secundum Bernardum. Although more diluted, the presence of another Thomistic apocryphal work called De beatitudine is much more extensive; it is quoted explicitly in the Spiritual Canticle B (stanza 38, 4), which reads thus: “Until it reaches this point the soul is not content, nor would it be so in the other life, if, as St. Thomas says in the opusculum De Beatitudine, it did not feel that it loves God as much as it is loved by Him. And, as has been said, in this state of spiritual marriage of which we are now speaking, although there is not that perfection of glorious love, there is nevertheless a living glimpse and image of that perfection, which is wholly ineffable.”

The quoted text is found in the second chapter of that opusculum, which, although it was not an authentic Thomistic writing, had no small impact on the writings of St. John of the Cross. Likewise, this serves to confirm the fact that the pages of those opuscula were very much frequented by the spiritual authors of the Spanish Golden Age, above all because of the aura of wisdom of the master to whom that work was attributed36.

The explicit quotations from authentic writings of St. Thomas concern precisely secondary themes in the synthesis of St. John of the Cross, and they also refer to Thomistic writings of second rank. The first Thomistic text explicitly quoted comes in to explain, with a simple phrase, the vision of the whole world that St. Benedict had in a spiritual vision. “Which vision, says St. Thomas in the first of his Quodlibets, was in the light derived from above, of which we have spoken”37. Note that the reference of St. John of the Cross is very precise and very exact, and it is indeed found in the Quodlibets, question 1, article 1, in the reply to the first objection.

Less concrete is the other reference to St. Thomas by name, in which the reference is so generic that its identification is not certain. It is introduced thus: “this is mystical theology, which theologians call secret wisdom, which St. Thomas says is communicated and infused into the soul through love, which takes place secretly, in darkness to the work of the intellect and of the other faculties”38. It is true that this doctrine could be attributed to various theologians; however, some argue that in saying “mystical theology,” St. John of the Cross would be referring to the Thomistic commentary on De divinis nominibus, ch. 7, lect. 1-2. In any case, this is something marginal, with little impact on the thought of St. John of the Cross.

Other quite perceptible Thomistic resonances do have greater impact. For example, the immediate source for defining the number of the passions and their distinction, “which are: joy, sorrow, hope and fear”39, is very probably based on the Thomistic summary in Summa Theologiae I-II, 25, 4; Summa Theologiae I-II, 80; II Sent., dist. 5-8. Or when John of the Cross writes in the Ascent that “God loves everything good, even in the barbarian and the gentile… as He did in the Romans”40 because of their just laws, he seems to allude to the text De regimine principium of the Doctor of Aquino (bk. 3, chs. 5-6). Likewise, the Thomistic doctrine of Contra pestiferam doctrinam retrahentium homines a religionis ingressu seems to resonate when the Mystical Doctor denounces the spiritual directors who prevent many from embracing the evangelical counsels. The consonance extends even to vocabulary, with such typical and strong expressions as “pestiferous manner,” “to be compelling”41, etc.

There are, moreover, many concrete points which, without such clear textual resonance, announce the Thomistic kinship.

One may recall, by way of example, the theme of revelations and their inclusion within the framework of the spirit of prophecy: “it now follows to treat of the second kind of spiritual apprehensions, which above we called revelations, which properly belong to the spirit of prophecy. Concerning which, it is first to be known that revelation is nothing other than the uncovering of some hidden truth or the manifestation of some secret or mystery […] Accordingly, we can say that there are two kinds of revelations: some, which are the uncovering of truths to the intellect, which are properly called intellectual knowledge or understandings; others, which are the manifestation of secrets, and these are properly called revelations, and more so than the others […] Accordingly, we may well now distinguish revelations into two kinds of apprehensions. The one we shall call intellectual knowledge, and the other, the manifestation of secrets and hidden mysteries of God”42. All of which is magnificently treated in the Summa Theologiae II-II, 171-174.

The doctrine on charisms: “Our Lord infuses habits concerning many things into many souls […] among which he places wisdom, knowledge, faith, prophecy, discernment or knowledge of spirits, understanding of tongues, interpretation of words, etc. All of which kinds of knowledge are infused habits, which (God) gives freely to whom He wills, now naturally, now supernaturally. […] But it is to be known that those who have a purified spirit can very easily know naturally […] The spiritual man judges all things. […] And although in knowledge by signs they can often be deceived, most of the time they are right. But one must not trust either the one or the other, because the devil meddles here greatly and with much subtlety”43.

St. Thomas, for his part, teaches: “as graces gratis datae, they entail a particularly high degree of knowledge and wisdom, thanks to which the one who holds them is enabled not only to judge rightly for himself about divine things, but also to instruct others and refute those who contradict”44.

The Saint of Fontiveros takes up the Thomistic doctrine on essential and accidental glory when he writes in the Canticle: “essential glory consists in seeing the being of God. […] [And this] Is for two reasons: The first, because just as the end of everything is love, which resides in the will, whose property is to give and not to receive, while the property of the intellect, which is the subject of essential glory, is to receive and not to give, the soul, being here inebriated with love, does not set before itself the glory that God is to give it, but rather to give itself to Him in the surrender of true love, without any regard for its own advantage”45; and also the teaching of the Aquinate on the endowments of the glorified body, when he writes, for example, in the Ascent: “the goods of glory that follow in the other life from the denial of this joy, there is no need to mention; for, besides the fact that the bodily endowments of glory, such as agility and clarity, will be much more excellent than those of the ones who did not deny themselves, so also the increase in the essential glory of the soul, which corresponds to the love of God for whose sake he denied the said sensible things: for each momentary and perishable joy that he denied, as St. Paul says, it will work in him eternally an immense weight of glory46”47. This doctrine is set forth by the Angelic Doctor in his Summa I-II, 2-5; 67, 3; Suppl. 92-96.

The great Thomistic influence on the writings of St. John of the Cross being beyond doubt, and having pointed out some passages where the influence becomes most evident, it seems fitting to us to note, together with Eulogio Pacho, that “John of the Cross departs at times from ‘Thomism’ because of the demands of his thought, through the influence of other philosophical currents and, mainly, for practical reasons of communication. To this last reason is probably due his departure from St. Thomas in proposing three distinct faculties of the soul and in pairing memory with hope. Something similar occurs with the enumeration of the interior bodily senses and the functions attributed to them. However, these are details that do not substantially alter the constant closeness of John of the Cross to the Angelic Doctor”48. In the words of Fr. Arintero: “St. John of the Cross, while being also to a certain extent highly original, resembles him in everything, in substance and even, very frequently, in the very form or expressions of the most lofty teachings that most characterize and accredit him”49.

3. Thomistic mysticism

We now want, starting from some of the principal fundamental doctrines of the Thomistic synthesis most closely related to the spiritual life, to point out how these are reflected in the mysticism of the Master of faith.

Concerning our intellectual knowledge of the natural order, above all of the first rational principles, such as the principle of contradiction, the Aquinate teaches that no being, created or uncreated, can be and not be at the same time and in the same subject; in accordance with which the Mystical Doctor does not hesitate to affirm that “two contraries, as philosophy teaches us, cannot fit in one subject. And because darkness, which is the affections for creatures, and light, which is God, are contraries and have no likeness or agreement with each other”50, it is necessary for the soul to pass through the dark night in order to reach the divine union, since the affections are darkness and God is pure light, and light cannot agree with darkness.

Concerning the principle of causality, according to which everything that can not-be, body or spirit, has a cause, St. John of the Cross consequently writes: “All the being of creatures, compared with the infinite (being) of God, is nothing. […] All the beauty of creatures, compared with the infinite beauty of God, is supreme ugliness […] And all the grace and charm of creatures, compared with the grace of God, is supreme gracelessness and supreme insipidity […] And all the goodness of the creatures of the world, compared with the infinite goodness of God, can be called wickedness […] And all the wisdom of the world and human skill, compared with the infinite wisdom of God, is pure and supreme ignorance”51.

Likewise, based on the principle of finality, by which every agent, material or spiritual, acts for an end, St. John of the Cross affirms: “God places the soul in this dark night in order to dry up and purge its sensitive appetite, [and] in nothing does He let it find delight or relish”52. “Because the end of everything is love”53 and “although it is true that glory consists in the intellect, the end of the soul is to love”54.

Finally, according to the fundamental principle of morality, which says that one must do good and avoid evil, the Mystic of Fontiveros writes that “the way of seeking God is to go on doing good in God and mortifying evil in oneself”55.

St. Thomas affirms that the intellectual knowledge of these primordial truths comes in a certain way from the senses, because our intellect abstracts its ideas from sensible things. However, such a statement has nothing to do with saying that the intellectual certainty of first principles is resolved formally in sensation (that would amount to subordinating the higher to the lower, the intellect to the senses). St. Thomas says that “the intellectual certainty of first principles is resolved only materially in the prerequisite sensation (I, 84, 6); formally it is resolved in the purely intellectual evidence of the absolute truth of these principles, which appear to it as fundamental laws not only of phenomena, but of being, or of all intelligible reality, corporeal or spiritual. This evidence presupposes in us an intellectual light of an order infinitely superior to sensation or to the most subtle imagination, which is continually enriched; an intellectual light which is a distant image of the divine light and which can illumine nothing without the constant concurrence of God (I, 84, 5)”56. In this sense, and well situated in the natural order, St. John of the Cross expresses himself by saying: “For although it is true that all of them [sensible things] have, as the theologians say, a certain relation to God and a trace of God −some more and others less, according to their more principal or less principal being−, from God to them there is no relation or essential likeness; rather, the distance between His divine being and theirs is infinite, and therefore it is impossible for the intellect to attain to God by means of creatures, whether heavenly or earthly, since there is no proportion of likeness”57. That is why “[the soul] must empty itself of everything that can fall within its capacity, so that, however many supernatural things it may come to have, it must always remain as if stripped of them and in darkness, like the blind man, leaning on dark faith, taking it as guide and light, and not leaning on any of the things it understands, tastes, feels and imagines. For all that is darkness, which will make it go astray; and faith is above all that understanding and tasting and feeling and imagining. And if it does not blind itself in this, remaining totally in darkness, it does not come to what is greater, which is what faith teaches”58. He adds still more in the Canticle: “God is not to the soul wholly dark, like a dark night, but repose and quiet in divine light, in a new knowledge of God, in which the spirit is most gently quiet, raised up to divine light. And he very properly calls this divine light here the rising of the dawn, which means the morning. For just as the rising of the morning dispels the darkness of the night and reveals the light of day, so this spirit, calm and quiet in God, is raised from the darkness of natural knowledge to the morning light of the supernatural knowledge of God, not clear but, as has been said, dark, like the night at the coming of the rising of the dawn”59.

Well known is the principle of St. Thomas regarding the supernatural life, which we have so many times read, studied and taught: “Grace perfects nature; it does not destroy it”60. “Sanctifying grace is what makes us sharers in His divine nature (2 Pet 1:4); it is, as the theologians say, a physical and formal, though analogous, participation in the divine nature itself”61, which denotes the irreplaceable importance of grace in the economy of salvation.

Now, it is clear that, although God gives Himself to the soul in infusing His grace into it, the soul does not respond to Him with the perfect gift of itself, nor does it live for God alone. Grace modifies the soul in its very substance, communicating to it that divine life, but there remain in the soul the disorder of its passions and an imponderable quantity of appetites and affections that are incompatible with grace and prevent the soul from going out of itself toward that total self-giving which correspondence to God’s gift asks for and demands. St. Thomas admirably maintains the infinite elevation of grace above nature, and at the same time their harmony; but this harmony will not be manifested until after a profound purification of nature through mortification and the cross. That is why the teaching of St. John of the Cross is typical, which explains, so that “the good spiritual person may understand that the more he is annihilated for God, according to these two parts, sensitive and spiritual, the more he is united to God and the greater the work he does. And when he comes to be reduced to nothing, which will be the height of humility, the spiritual union between the soul and God will be accomplished”62. Hence the necessity of the active and passive purifications as the normal means of arriving at union with God. “And if there is a lack in this exercise,” warns the Mystical Doctor, “which is the whole and the root of the virtues, all those other ways are beating about the bush and making no progress, even though one may have considerations and communications as lofty as those of the angels”63. For, as he himself clarifies, it is so that we may be “deiform,” that is, so that the soul may be transformed into the three Persons in power and wisdom and love, that God created us64.

With respect to the theological virtues and the gifts of the Holy Spirit, St. Thomas analyzes each of them in detail, in general and in particular, concerning himself little with the effect they produce in the soul as far as the descriptive side is concerned. St. John of the Cross, on the contrary, hardly concerns himself with investigating the causes, showing us only the way of the soul, in its experiential and positive side, until it reaches union with God.

According to what has been said above, “faith is a participation in the knowledge that God has of His own essence, which no created intellect can reach by itself. That is why it is an infused and wholly supernatural virtue, by which the life of grace is actualized in the intellective order”65.

Faith gives us a dark knowledge, since it concerns a transcendent truth: “because God is of a different being from His creatures, in which He is infinitely distant from all of them”66. Therefore, even the highest ideas we can form of God by abstracting them from things are infinitely distant from God.

This supernatural knowledge of God leads us to love, not to a natural love as of our first principle, but to a love of true friendship, which is what unites us to God. Faith itself, without charity, St. Thomas explains in his treatise De charitate, is dead and cannot lead us to the goal it points out to us.

“The relationship between knowledge and love becomes ever more intimate. True knowledge always leads to love, while love ‘animates’ or gives new value to all operations. The Angelic Doctor reaffirms this in clear terms: ‘Although the contemplative life consists essentially in the intellect, it has its principle, however, in the will, inasmuch as the love of God impels to contemplation. The delight produced by the sight of a beloved object further arouses one’s love for it. And that is the ultimate perfection of the contemplative life: not only the vision of divine truth, but also the love of it’67. And the Mystical Doctor makes this same doctrine his own: ‘Contemplation is the science of love, which… is a loving infused knowledge of God, which at the same time illumines and enkindles the soul with love, until it raises it step by step to God, its Creator, because it is love alone that unites and joins the soul with God” 68. That is why our Proper Law teaches: “by faith man entrusts himself entirely and freely to God”69

Love is unitive; “because the thing loved becomes one with the lover; and so God does with whoever loves Him”70. And just as God’s love for man makes him united with Him through His grace, charity, which is man’s love for God, is, as it is perfected, what must lead him to the heights of mystical union.

It is interesting to note at this point that both Doctors use the classical categories to describe the faithful (beginners, proficients or advanced, and the perfect) or the principal stages of the spiritual way (purgative, illuminative and unitive), and their conclusions are certainly similar. Every human act has saving power if it is moved by charity; every step is a means toward Christian perfection when it is assisted by the Holy Spirit. And the whole of temporal existence acquires an extraordinary value when what we are and do is permeated by divine love itself.

The Mystic of Fontiveros delights in his works in explaining the consequences of this. For example, he speaks of charity, which enters the soul71 and empties it of everything that is not authentic love of God and of neighbor, leaving in writing that beautiful phrase which our Proper Law takes up in the Constitutions and which says: “to love is to strip oneself for God of all that is not God”72. For charity is “the bond and tie of perfection”73, and consequently we want to make “our whole existence a continual worship of God in charity”74.

What stands out in the treatise of the Angelic Doctor mentioned above is the totality of love which, he says, is required toward God. To love Him above all things is to love Him very little, because that means that we love other things along with Him. Therefore, the love of God must absorb all our capacity, inform and vivify all our faculties and move all our acts. It is through the continual purification of the senses and of the higher faculties that the soul is made ready to receive the degree of love that God has prepared for it; or, as St. John of the Cross says, “until God brings it into His divine splendors through the transformation of love”75. For until that happens, perfect union with the Beloved will not come about, which will be when “all the strength of its faculties is turned to spiritual converse with the Beloved”76. Then the soul, inebriated in the wine cellar of the Bridegroom, will be able to sing:

My soul has been employed,

and all my wealth, in His service:

now I guard no flock,

nor have I any other office,

for now in loving alone is my exercise77.

Now, above these two theological virtues of faith and charity, another theological virtue is required, which is hope. For, as St. Thomas teaches, love is not content with a superficial knowledge of the Beloved, but wants to penetrate to His innermost depths; nor is it satisfied with an imperfect possession of His goodness and His beauty, but tends toward full possession, without hindrances or veils. And that is why the soul longs for the veil of faith to be torn, the veil through which it contemplates its Beloved, so as to see Him as He is in Himself, and to be fused into Him and enjoy Him without measure78. And “until it reaches this point,” says St. John of the Cross, repeating the teaching of the Angelic Doctor, “the soul is not content, nor would it be so in the other life, if it did not feel that it loves God as much as it is loved by Him”79.

At this point we wish to note that if “a single thought of man is worth more than the whole world”80, or a human act cannot be equated with all the treasures of the world81, the elevation of the human person to the divine condition is the best sign of his inalienable dignity. That is why St. John Paul II said with sapiential insight: “I think that, in order to understand the dignity of the human person, the capacity of the human person, it is necessary to pass through the theology of St. John of the Cross; one must pass −let us put it this way− once through that dimension of man which opens up with the doctrine of St. John of the Cross, and then one sees what ‘man’ means. Afterward one cannot forget his dignity”82.

* * *

We see, then, how St. Thomas Aquinas, the Sun of scholastic Theology, and St. John of the Cross, the Eagle of Mysticism, each with his specific contribution, integrate, complement and illumine one another, in such a way that they point out and foster the development of the life of the soul until it reaches the highest degree of union with God, the summit of authentic Christian spirituality.

Common to both doctors is the passion for “the Truth that saves”83; a knowledge that increases love for God; a love that animates all the acts of the virtues. That is why we can say that the clear-sightedness of the holy theologian of Aquino was the mystical experience of the Saint of Fontiveros.

The testimony of both is particularly stimulating for those who, like us, have the task of the evangelization of culture, a task that “demands a faith enlightened by continual reflection that confronts itself with the sources of the Church’s message, and a constant spiritual discernment sought in prayer”84, together with an intellectual formation that is ordered to the truth and does not stop at knowing the mere opinions of theologians85.

Both St. John of the Cross and St. Thomas Aquinas centered their investigations on the Person of Christ and found in contemplation that “Christian common sense which is nothing other than holy familiarity with the Word made flesh”86. Both gave themselves to God, understanding that this self-giving could only be absolute and unconditional. Both embodied a mystical life, unitive from early on, beyond mystical phenomena. Both lived Christ interiorly with such intensity that they themselves became living images of the Lord87.

From all that has been said it follows that both the one and the other, through their works, not only laid the foundations for a profound Christian life, but explored its treasures and riches and set them forth for the glory of God and the extension of His Kingdom. That is why nothing is more in keeping with our “serious spirituality” than the study of St. Thomas in everything pertaining to theology or sacred doctrine, and the following of St. John of the Cross in his teaching on the mystical life.

For it will always be true that genuine “theological reflection ‘is centered on adherence to Jesus Christ, the Wisdom of God…, [which] helps to develop, in addition to scientific rigor, a great and living love for Jesus Christ and for His Church’88, a love which, at the same time […] nourishes the spiritual life, [and] serves as a guide for the generous exercise of the ministry. That is why Theology must be nourished in prayer and in love for Jesus Christ. [In this sense] St. Bonaventure warned: “Let no one think that reading is enough for him without unction, speculation without devotion, searching without wonder, observation without jubilation, activity without piety, knowledge without charity, intelligence without humility, study without divine grace, investigation without the wisdom of supernatural inspiration’89”90.

In short, may our longing always be “Christ known, sought, loved ever more through studies, through personal sacrifices, through victories over oneself, in the slow conquest of the virtues of justice, fortitude, temperance and prudence; Christ contemplated with patient and fervent perseverance so that… the very face of Christ may be imprinted (cf. 2 Cor 3:18)”91. Because “there is no authentic Catholic pastoral work without a profound spiritual life, without a solid doctrinal formation and without a manly discipline”92.

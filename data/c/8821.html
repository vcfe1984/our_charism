[Exordium] Insecurity and uncertainty are real facts of life. The very life of Christ begins with the Child in danger, and, truth be told, insecurity accompanied every step He took. If we read the Gospel from the first page to the last, we will not find a single time when Christ encourages us to nurture feelings of security. The message of Christ, as you well know, is that if one is trying to reach the end for which he was created, it is important that he realize that worldly security is an illusion. That is why, when God gives us a shake, it is important to have our priorities in order.

Having decided that in these sermons I wanted to preach using examples of American saints, I came across a book that in several chapters describes various trials that our dear Fulton Sheen had to go through, and which certainly made him stagger and caused him great sorrow, but did not bring him down, precisely because he had his priorities well defined. Many of you may already know them, so I will mention, if you will, the most notable ones.

1. Trials Fulton Sheen went through as a bishop

The greatest trials came upon Fulton Sheen −as you probably know− when he was already auxiliary bishop of New York. There he had several disputes with Cardinal Spellman. Spellman was the head of the richest diocese in the world and was used to getting his way. That is why some say that the clash between the most powerful and the most popular was something inevitable.

On one of those occasions Cardinal Spellman insisted that Sheen, as director of the office of the Propagation of the Faith, reimburse him an enormous sum of money for powdered milk that Spellman had donated during the Korean conflict and that Propaganda Fide had distributed. Sheen refused. Because he knew that Spellman had received the milk as a donation, and so he reasoned that if the archdiocese had not paid anything, he did not have to reimburse anything. Spellman became furious and appealed to the Pope. They were fighting over powdered milk! The cardinal even tried to persuade the Pope to replace Fulton Sheen as head of the office of the Propagation of the Faith, and what is more, he offered Fulton Sheen a wealthy parish, which the latter flatly refused. Furthermore, Spellman launched three different investigations hoping to find something with which he could incriminate the auxiliary bishop, but he found nothing.

Eventually the two appeared before Pope Pius XII, each presenting his case. But Fulton Sheen made a documented presentation of his, and, obviously, Spellman had not paid a cent for the milk. That meant that the cardinal had lied to the Pope and to Fulton Sheen. The Pope obviously sided with Fulton Sheen, and he remained head of Propaganda Fide. But the cardinal assured Sheen that he would make him pay for it: “it will take six months or ten years, but everyone is going to know who you are,” Spellman told Fulton Sheen. Years later Sheen would refer to this incident of the milk money as one of his crucifixions.

They say that when the cardinal visited the Seminary in New York he told the seminarians that Sheen was “the most disobedient priest in the country” and that he did not want any of them to be like him. And over the years he kept coming down hard on him: traditionally Fulton Sheen preached on Good Friday from the most coveted pulpit, that of St. Patrick’s Cathedral in New York. Spellman decided that he would no longer do so, and so Sheen went to preach in New Jersey (another diocese). Later Sheen withdrew from television, and although he publicly said that he did so for “spiritual considerations,” many knew that it was the cardinal who forced him off television, even though his was the number one show in the ratings. Speaking of his struggles with the cardinal, he once said: “envy is the tribute the mediocre pay to genius.” It was during that time that Sheen wrote the Life of Christ −which I believe we have all read− because, as he himself explained, “during those days when my life was pressed against the cross, that is when I learned to know it and to love it more.” Many distanced themselves from Fulton Sheen because they did not want to risk becoming victims of the cardinal’s fury for being friends of the auxiliary bishop.

A short time later Spellman exiled him from New York −some say so that he would stop overshadowing him− and made him bishop of Rochester. In his farewell sermon in St. Patrick’s Cathedral, in front of Spellman himself and some 3,000 faithful, Fulton Sheen began his sermon by thanking the cardinal, with his usual irony, “for those priestly intimacies that have united us forever.”

2. A petition against him

Barely 15 months after becoming bishop he was already thinking of leaving, since in fact things were going rather badly for him. 125 priests of his diocese signed a petition against him. And that is how he later submitted his resignation and ceased to be bishop of Rochester after only 34 months.

As he was leaving to take the plane to leave his diocese, he said to his vicar: “It used to be a glory to be a bishop. But nowadays I would not wish it even on my best friend.”

Two weeks later he appeared on a television program and, without referring to Cardinal Spellman, said: “I would have gone far in the Church… but I did not want to pay the price.” The journalist asked him what that price was, and Fulton Sheen simply evaded the question, saying: “Well, I felt it would have been a lack of loyalty to my own principles and, I think, to Christian practice.” An upright man with his priorities very clear.

When he moved to New York he told his niece: “I never thought I would end up washing dishes and making my own bed. It is not an unhappy adventure.” He distributed his vast library among various people and institutions and moved into an apartment that his family found for him.

That is how this man went from being followed by multitudes, respected, admired… rubbing shoulders with the celebrities of the world and the great figures of the Church, with all the worldly securities that this entails, to being a poor priest living alone in a three-room apartment. But he lived and died with his head held high because he was not beholden to anyone.

Everything was crumbling around him; those who before followed him and wanted his company now avoided him. His own cardinal went around everywhere running him down. Imagine how piercing the pain must have been that more than a hundred priests of his diocese asked that he leave… The sense of failure and frustration must have been immense.

3. We have a purpose in life

And yet, this is the same man who wrote: “Very often the temptation will be very strong and the temporal advantages will seem grand to us” −for example, if one concentrates more on what is most immediate to him: the parish, study, preaching spiritual exercises, giving a little talk here or there… even one’s own province, simply because it is more gratifying, one sees immediate, tangible results, if you will−. “We have a purpose in life, but if we never stop to think why we are here, or where we are going, or what the purpose of our life is, we will end up changing our direction but we will not make progress. And what is worse, we will get stuck in those tiny, successive and incomplete destinations, only to end up completely lost. If a man who is lost in the forest had a distant point outside the forest to serve him as a reference, he could freely leave the forest or remain there. The same happens in life: if we have a fixed goal, we can make real progress toward that goal, but it would be complete stupidity to say that we are progressing (or doing things well) if we are constantly changing that goal. Our Lord on the Cross gave us an example of this: He left everything, even His own life, in order to run toward the goal. […] That is why we must NEVER step down from the supreme end and purpose of our life.” So far Fulton Sheen.

This great man went through everything he went through, but they could not defeat him because he had his priorities in order, and thus he made history.

[Peroratio] Mutatis mutandi, we, as religious of the Institute, and by the office that Providence has assigned us, have a goal, which in our case is to defend and guard the patrimony of the Institute, not to allow them to come and steal our candidates before our very eyes, to ensure unity among our own, to see to it that our ranks increase and do not decrease… That is the noble responsibility and the priority at this moment. It depends on us.

If Fulton Sheen had shrunk back and had not stood firm against the leader of the richest diocese in the world, he would have ended up paying Spellman what he did not owe. If he had not had his priorities very clear, he would have left Propaganda Fide for a comfortable and wealthy parish, or he would have negotiated keeping quiet in order to continue with his television show. If he had let it go to his head, he would have clung to his office as bishop whether the priests liked it or not… but that was not the case. He was a man of a single purpose: to serve God, come what may.

In one of his books he wrote −and with this I conclude−: “We must do everything that is within our power to fulfill the will of God… but everything that is outside our power we must set aside and submit to His Holy Will. Note the difference, Sheen goes on to say, between within our power and outside our power. We must not be fatalists. Some things are under our control. What is wrong with fatalism is that it fails to recognize that, within certain limits, our will can affect the events of life. It would therefore be a grave error not to do the very best we can for the good of our brothers and the glory of God. It is the will of God that we men use our free will, subordinated to His Will, in order to be happy.”

We ask the Virgin Mother for light to have our priorities very clear at this moment.

After increasing the number of seminarians from 42 to 147 in eight years, and that of minor seminarians from 200 to 500 in four seminaries; after providing ongoing formation to the priests of six dioceses of the metropolitan Church of Hue; after developing and intensifying the formation of young people and laity in the diocese of Nhatrang (Vietnam), the then bishop of that diocese, Francis Xavier van Thuan, was named by St. Paul VI coadjutor archbishop of Saigon. However, nine months after arriving there he was arrested by the communists and sent to a “re-education camp”—a euphemism they used for the word prison—where he remained from 1975 to 1988, suffering the “harshest captivity”1.

Of those 13 years, he spent 9 in isolation, alone with two guards, something he himself describes as “a mental torture, in absolute emptiness, without work, walking around the cell from morning until half past nine at night so as not to be destroyed by arthrosis, at the edge of madness”2. He was then 48 years old.

Certainly his was an extreme case; nevertheless, it is common for many good men and women to suffer moral evils through no fault of their own. That is why, as could happen to any one of us and in fact does happen to us when the things for which we fought so arduously are destroyed from one day to the next, Bishop van Thuan was tempted, and more than tempted, he was tormented by the fact that, being 48 years old, having worked eight years as a bishop, having acquired much pastoral experience, etc., he now found himself isolated, inactive, separated from his people.

How many times throughout our history—at the level of the Institute, but also personally—have we had to leave unfinished a program or a project that was well planned, well organized; how many times have activities that we set in motion with great enthusiasm been obstructed; apostolates or projects of great scope have become minor activities; how many times, seeking to do good, have we been calumniated, persecuted, threatened… Nevertheless, we too, like Bishop van Thuan, must learn to “choose God and not the works of God”3, who disposes all things for our greater benefit.

God grants us today to begin a new year. And so it might seem strange to speak of such an experience, when the whole world is planning the new year with optimism, but frequently without including God in its projects.

For our part, it is not that we do not have projects, it is not that we do not have goals to reach, nor old challenges to keep facing, but it is no less true that divine providence has merciful designs for us that do not always coincide with our plans. This does not mean that this providential intervention of God must always be adverse; on the contrary, it is always positive and seeks to smooth the way for us, even when those situations do not please us and seem to us bad and very unfavorable. For this reason, it has seemed to us that it may be very healthy for our souls to take into account in ‘our planning’ that master coordinate, which in reality is the central axis on which hang the various variables that make up the interweaving of the many events of our daily life. So that, far from beginning the year with a pessimistic view of reality, we wish to offer a brief reflection on the special and merciful divine providence that is manifested even when bad things happen to the good and good things to the wicked, since, as St. John of the Cross says, “His Majesty having so ordained it, it is what is most fitting for us all”4.

1. The “hidden treasures” of divine providence

It happens to not a few Christians that, even while they profess their faith in divine providence and strive with all their effort to give the best of themselves to do something good, for themselves or for others, they see their efforts despised and the work of their hands vanish before their eyes. Thus not a few are faced with the reality, for example, that their businesses went to ruin; that even having married and given everything, their family ends up divided; that the mission which cost them so many sacrifices has been closed, or an illness has taken them away from the apostolate; or simply that those from whom they expected help have betrayed them. Hence the reality of evil and suffering, present in so many forms in human life, constitutes for many—even religious—the principal difficulty in accepting the truth of divine providence (and we say accept in the sense of assimilating it interiorly and not simply of giving intellectual assent to a truth).

Echoing this reality, St. John Paul II stated in one of his audiences: “In some cases, this difficulty takes on a radical form, when God is even accused of the evil and suffering present in the world, to the point of rejecting the very truth of God and of His existence (that is, to the point of atheism). In a less radical and yet disquieting way, this difficulty is expressed in so many critical questions that man poses to God. Doubt, questioning and even protest arise from the difficulty of reconciling the truth of divine providence, of God’s fatherly solicitude toward the created world, with the reality of evil and suffering experienced in various forms by men”5.

On the other hand, every year we make the Spiritual Exercises and every year, therefore, we meditate on the Principle and Foundation: “Man is created to praise, reverence and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul; and the other things on the face of the earth are created for man, and that they may help him in pursuing the end for which he is created. From this it follows that man is to use them as much as they help him toward his end, and ought to rid himself of them as much as they hinder him from it. For this reason it is necessary to make ourselves indifferent to all created things, in all that is granted to the liberty of our free will and is not forbidden to it; in such a way that we, for our part, do not want health more than sickness, riches more than poverty, honor more than dishonor, a long life more than a short one, and so in all the rest; desiring and choosing only what most leads us to the end for which we are created”6. St. Ignatius calls it the Principle and Foundation of his Spiritual Exercises, but it is also the most fundamental truth about the existence of man and divine providence. St. John Paul II expressed this when he said: “Faith in divine providence is intimately linked with the basic conception of human existence, that is, with the meaning of man’s life. Man can face his existence in an essentially different way when he has the certainty that he is not under the dominion of a blind destiny (fatum), but depends on Someone who is his Creator and Father. For this reason, faith in divine providence, inscribed in the first words of the Apostles’ Creed, ‘I believe in God the Father almighty,’ frees human existence from the various forms of fatalistic thinking”7. Nevertheless, it sometimes happens that under pressure or in difficult circumstances we forget this, or we accept these words as mere abstract principles of the spiritual life without their becoming an integral part of our daily life. That is, we do not take them on in a vital way. That is why in this first point we would like to bring up what the Magisterium of the Church and her illustrious Doctor, St. Thomas Aquinas, teach on this so fundamental topic.

On the one hand, none of us is unaware that the truth of divine providence is present throughout all of Revelation. Even more, we can say that it “permeates all of Revelation”8. Likewise, it is found from the beginning, as a fundamental truth of the faith, in the ordinary Magisterium of the Church, although only the First Vatican Council pronounced on it within the framework of the solemn dogmatic Constitution De fide catholica, in connection with the truth about creation. Here are the words of Vatican I: “God preserves all that He has created and directs it with His providence, ‘reaching from end to end mightily and governing all things with goodness’9. ‘All things are naked before His eyes’10, even what takes place through the free initiative of creatures.” Following in the footsteps of the constant tradition of the Church’s teaching, it is also deduced from the texts of the Second Vatican Council that God is the one who “cares for all with fatherly solicitude”11, and in particular “for the human race”12. A manifestation of this solicitude is also the “divine law, eternal, objective and universal, by which God orders, directs and governs the whole world and the ways of the human community according to the design of His wisdom and His love”13. “Man… does not in fact exist except through the love of God, who created him, and through the love of God, who preserves him. And he can only be said to live in the fullness of truth when he freely acknowledges that love and entrusts himself entirely to his Creator”14.

In the Summa Theologica, I, q. 22, the Angelic Doctor treats of the providence of God. We shall not transcribe everything explained there—although we strongly recommend reading it15—but shall simply confine ourselves to offering the basic concepts that will serve us as guidelines for approaching this topic.

  • What in God is called providence is the plan of the order of things toward their ends16.
  • All beings are subject to divine providence, and not only as a whole, but also in particular17.
  • Providence comprises two things: the plan of the order of the beings provided for toward their end, and the execution of this order, called “government.” As to the first, God provides immediately for all things, because in His intellect He has the plan of them all, even of the least, and because to whatever causes He entrusted some effect He gave sufficient activity to produce it, for which it is indispensable that He should know beforehand, in its proper plan, the order of such effects. As to the second, divine providence makes use of intermediaries, for it governs lower beings by means of higher ones, not because its power is insufficient, but because its goodness is so great that it communicates to creatures themselves the prerogative of causality18.
  • It is an effect of divine providence not only that anything whatever should happen, but that it should happen in a necessary or a contingent way; and therefore what divine providence disposes to happen infallibly and necessarily happens infallibly and necessarily, and what is in the plan of divine providence to happen contingently happens contingently19.

Now, the suffering that arises from the many experiences of evil, especially when it is without fault, leads man to pose those difficult and dramatic questions which are at times an accusation, at other times a challenge, or a cry of rejection of God and His providence. In some way these questions could be summed up thus: “How can evil and suffering be reconciled with the fatherly solicitude, full of love, that Jesus Christ attributes to God in the Gospel? How can they be reconciled with the transcendent wisdom of the Creator? And in an even more dialectical way: in the face of all the experience of evil there is in the world, especially in the face of the suffering of the innocent, can we say that God does not will evil? And if He wills it, how can we believe that God is love, and all the more so that this love cannot but be omnipotent?”20. The affliction or suffering of the good—if it is without fault—is what seems to stand in opposition to divine providence.

Faced with these questions, we too, like Job, experience how difficult it is to give an answer. That is why, with humility before the mystery21, we seek the answer in the Word of God, which the Holy Spirit made fruitful with the truth22. Thus in the Old Testament we find the affirmation: …but wickedness does not prevail over wisdom. Wisdom reaches mightily from one end to the other and governs all things gently23. We see, then, that in the face of the manifold experiences of evil and suffering in the world, the Old Testament already bore witness to the primacy of the Wisdom and goodness of God, of His divine providence. This attitude is outlined and developed in the Book of Job, which is entirely devoted to the theme of evil and pain seen as a trial, at times a tremendous one, for the just man, but overcome with the certainty, laboriously attained, that God is good.

St. Thomas Aquinas in his Expositio super Job ad litteram states the following: “After most men had affirmed the opinion that natural things did not happen by chance but by providence, because of the order that clearly appears in them, a doubt arose among most men about the acts of man, that is, whether human affairs developed by chance or by causality, whether they were governed by some kind of providence or higher order. This doubt was nourished especially because no sure order is apparent in human events. For good things do not always happen to the good, nor bad things to the bad. On the other hand, bad things do not always befall the good, nor good things the wicked, but good and evil fall indiscriminately upon the good and the wicked. This fact especially moved the hearts of men to hold the opinion that human affairs are not governed by divine providence. Some said that human affairs proceed by chance except insofar as they are governed by human providence and counsel; others attribute their outcome to a fatalism governed by the heavens.

This idea causes great harm to humanity. For if divine providence is denied, no reverence or true fear of God will remain among men. Every man can well weigh how great the propensity to vice and the lack of desire for virtue that follow from this idea will be. For nothing so draws men back from evil things and induces them to good as much as the fear and love of God. For this reason, the first and principal aim of those who had sought wisdom inspired by the spirit of God for the instruction of others was to remove this opinion from the hearts of men. So… the Book of Job… is directed to this: to show that human affairs are ruled by divine providence…

[…] The affliction of the just is what seems especially to impugn divine providence in human affairs. For although at first sight it may seem irrational and contrary to providence that good things sometimes happen to bad men, this can be excused in one way or another by divine compassion. But that the just should be afflicted without cause seems to undermine the foundation of providence entirely. Thus the varied and grievous afflictions of a certain just man named Job, perfect in all his virtues, are proposed as a kind of theme for the question that is to be discussed”24.

In the Christian spiritual tradition Job symbolizes resigned suffering, the trusting acceptance of pain. That is why ‘Holy Job’ is the archetype and paradigm of Christian patience. Moreover, he is, according to scholars, “the best typical and tropological figure of passive purification”25, for he passes through all the stages of the dark purification: from the physical trial—nakedness, abandonment by friends, loss of goods—to the heartrending cry of the afflicted and humiliated soul that “roars and bellows”26. And this purifying night has the meaning of a means with respect to the end, which is to arrive at union with God. That is why we must know how to recognize that whatever ‘our night’ may be, it is “a night that conceals the hopes of the light of day”27.

This being so, the affirmation of Sacred Scripture, wickedness does not prevail over wisdom28, strengthens our conviction that, in the providential plan of the Creator regarding the world, evil is ultimately subordinated to good. And it is very important always to bear this in mind. Our Proper Law reminds us of this very thing when it exhorts us to give glory to God by trusting without limits in His providence, telling us: “that all things work together for the good of those who love God29. In saying all things, it excepts nothing. Therefore, here are included all events, prosperous or adverse, what concerns the good of the soul, the goods of fortune, reputation, all the conditions of human life (family, studies, talents, etc.), all the interior states through which we pass (delights, joys, privations, dryness, displeasures, tedium, temptations, etc.), even faults and sins themselves. Everything, absolutely everything. In saying work together for the good, it is understood that they cooperate, contribute, happen for our spiritual good. One must have this vision and not that of the carnal or worldly man. One must see everything in the light of the loving designs of the providence of God, which only the spiritual man discovers: the spiritual man judges all things30. We must believe with unshakable firmness that even the most adverse events, those most opposed to our natural outlook, are ordained by God for our good, even though we do not understand His designs and do not know the end to which He wishes to lead us. But for our part, we must fulfill a condition for this to come about. That is why it adds of those who love God, that is, those whose will is united and submissive to that of God, who seek above all the interests and the glory of God, who are ready to sacrifice everything without reserve, persuaded that nothing is so advantageous as to abandon oneself into the hands of God in everything it may please Him to ordain, as Jesus gave us to understand: if anyone serves me, the Father will honor him31. He alone knows everything, even our soul, feelings, character, the secret springs that must be moved to bring us to heaven, the effects that this or that thing will produce in us, and He has all means at His disposal”32.

Moreover, in the context of the integral truth about divine providence, and for a better understanding, it is necessary to bear in mind the following statements: “God does not will evil as such” and “God permits evil.” Concerning which St. John Paul II explains: “Regarding the first, it is fitting to recall the words of the Book of Wisdom: …God did not make death, nor does He rejoice in the destruction of the living. For He created all things that they might exist33.

  • As for the permission of evil in the physical order, for example, in the face of the fact that material beings (among them also the human body) are corruptible and undergo death, it must be said that this belongs to the structure of these creatures. On the other hand, the unlimited continuance of every individual bodily being would be hardly conceivable in the present state of the material world. We can, then, understand that, if God did not create death, as the Book of Wisdom affirms, He nevertheless permits it with a view to the overall good of the material cosmos.
  • But if it is a question of moral evil, that is, of sin and guilt in their various forms and consequences, even in the physical order, this evil God decidedly and absolutely does not will. Moral evil is radically contrary to the will of God. If this evil is present in the history of man and of the world, and at times in a totally oppressive form, if in a certain sense it has its own history, it is only permitted by divine providence, because God wills that there be freedom in the created world. The existence of created freedom (and consequently of man, and even the existence of pure spirits such as the angels, of whom we shall speak on another occasion) is indispensable for that fullness of good which God wills to bring about in creation. The existence of free beings is for Him a more important and fundamental valuethan the fact that those beings abuse their own freedom against the Creator and that, for this reason, freedom can lead to moral evil”34.

That is why our Proper Law, with a fatherly accent, calls us to offer with our lives—with all their vicissitudes and particular circumstances—“an unceasing worship to divine providence,” having “the certainty that ‘bodily danger does not threaten those who, with the intention of following Christ, abandon all their things, entrusting themselves to divine providence’35. That Father full of goodness who takes care of the birds and the flowers of the field36 will not abandon those who give themselves to Him with such confidence”37.

Undoubtedly reason and revelation greatly enlighten us concerning the mystery of divine providence which, while not willing evil, tolerates it in view of a greater good. However, the definitive light can only come from the victorious Cross of Christ.

The Spiritual Father of our Religious Family develops this masterfully in a series of catecheses, saying: For “in the eternal plan of God and in His providential action in the history of man, all evil, and especially moral evil—sin—is subjected to the good of redemption and salvation precisely through the Cross and Resurrection of Christ. It can be affirmed that, in Him, God draws good out of evil. He draws it, in a certain sense, from the very evil that sin implies, which was the cause of the suffering of the immaculate Lamb and of His terrible death on the Cross as an innocent victim for the sins of the world. That is why in the liturgy of the Easter Vigil we exclaim: ‘felix culpa’38”39.

“Thus, to the question of how to reconcile evil and suffering in the world with the truth of divine providence, no definitive answer can be offered without reference to Christ. Indeed, on the one hand Christ—the Incarnate Word—confirms with His own life—in poverty, humiliation and toil—and especially with His passion and death, that God is at man’s side in his suffering; even more, that He Himself takes upon Himself the manifold suffering of man’s earthly existence. Jesus reveals at the same time that this suffering possesses a redemptive and salvific value and power, that in it is prepared that imperishable inheritance of which St. Peter speaks in his first Letter: the inheritance that is reserved for us in heaven40. The truth of providence thus acquires, through “the power and the wisdom” of the Cross of Christ, its definitive eschatological meaning. The definitive answer to the question about the presence of evil and suffering in man’s earthly existence is offered by divine Revelation in the perspective of ‘predestination in Christ,’ that is, in the perspective of man’s vocation to eternal life, to participation in the life of God Himself. This is precisely the answer that Christ has offered, confirming it with His Cross and with His Resurrection. In this way, everything, even the evil and suffering present in the created world, and especially in the history of man, is subjected to that inscrutable wisdom, about which St. Paul exclaims, as if transfigured: O the depth of the riches, of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God! How inscrutable are His judgments and unfathomable His ways…!41. In the whole salvific context, it is in fact the wisdom against which wickedness cannot prevail42”43.

That is why what we have been saying—although, as we have said, we shall treat it on another occasion—applies also to the action of the demons, who are mere creatures and cannot escape the causality of God, the First Universal Cause of all being and acting. They, much to their regret, end up fulfilling the plan of God, who disposes all things for the good of those who love Him44. And the more they rage against the works of God and against Christians, even to causing them martyrdom, the more they contribute to the sanctification of the elect and to the spread of the Kingdom of God. That is why Tertullian said that “the blood of martyrs is the seed of new Christians.”

2. Some practical counsels of the saints

It is well known that the saints also suffered and were purified in the crucible of the sorrows that befell them so often ‘unjustly,’ while they were totally innocent. Now, since the saints were not mistaken in the practice of the virtues45, it is fitting to learn from them to complete what is lacking in the Passion of Christ46 with an affective… effective… and afflictive reparation… for our own benefit and that of the whole Mystical Body, as is asked of each one of us47. So that, if at some time everything should crumble and everything for which we fought and which we so defended should seem ruined; if the things we plan to do should come to face threats and we should find ourselves wrapped in moral evils and suffer the crushing weight of humiliation and calumny; if the question “what to do when nothing seems to make sense?” knocks at the door of our souls; let our answer be: it is time to believe. It is time to exercise our faith. It is time to cling still more to God. It is time to live with confidence the folly of the Cross, which consists in living the beatitudes48. It is the time to live “the folly of love without limits or measure, by which we are to bless those who curse us, without returning evil for evil49”50. It is the time to strive to acquire the wisdom of the Cross, by which the world will not be able to deceive us51.

The apostolic writings are full of precisely this wisdom, to help us, when we are afflicted, to recognize the passing of the grace of God. Thus St. Peter writes to the Christians of the first generation: Rejoice in this, even though now you must be saddened a little, in various temptations52. And he adds: so that your faith, tested, more precious than gold, which perishes even though refined by fire, may be found worthy of praise, glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ53. These last words refer to the Old Testament, and especially to the book of Sirach, in which we read: For gold is tested in the fire, and men pleasing to God in the crucible of humiliation54. That is why the Apostle, taking up the same theme of trial, continues in his Letter: Rather, rejoice insofar as you share in the sufferings of Christ, so that at the revelation of His glory you may exult in His joy55.

In an analogous way the Apostle James expresses himself when he exhorts Christians to face trials with joy and patience: Consider it the greatest joy, my brothers, to find yourselves surrounded by various temptations, knowing that the testing of your faith produces patience. But let patience be accompanied by perfect works, so that you may be perfect and complete56. Lastly, St. Paul in the Letter to the Romans, after comparing human sufferings to “labor pains,” adds: Now, we know that God makes all things work together for the good of those who love Him…57, and further on, Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Tribulation, anguish, persecution, hunger, nakedness, danger, the sword?58, finally concluding: For I am persuaded that neither death nor life… nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God (manifested) in Christ Jesus, our Lord59.

That is why we must learn to see, together with the fatherhood of God, which is manifested through divine providence, the pedagogy of God: You suffer for the sake of your correction. God is treating you as sons; for what son is there whom his father does not correct (educate)…? God, looking to our benefit, corrects us to make us sharers in His holiness60.

Thus, then, seen with the eyes of faith, suffering, although it may present itself as the darkest aspect of our earthly destiny, allows the mystery of divine providence, contained in the revelation of Christ, and in a special way in His Cross and in His Resurrection, to shine through for us.

The saints of all times had this truth of divine providence firmly settled in their souls. And so when things were not going well, when they saw their plans seriously altered and the fruits of their efforts lying on the ground, when they were insulted and driven out of towns (even, in some cases, by the bishops of the dioceses), when they were not allowed to preach, when they were falsely accused, even when they were threatened with death and led to the scaffold, they were not disturbed, they did not lose their calm, they forgave their enemies and even rejoiced.

We, like every Christian, are not exempt from those trials, and therefore, if any of this should happen to us, we must do ourselves the favor of praying. Not a prayer that tells God the things He has to do about the injustices we suffer and the sufferings that lacerate us. God already knows what He has to do. Rather, ours must be a prayer that nails us even more to our center, namely, God Himself—who for us was nailed to the Cross61—and reaffirms us in the knowledge that we are in His hands, striving, as St. John of the Cross says, “to keep the heart in peace,” letting “no event of this world disquiet” us; for “consider that everything must come to an end”62.

The Mystical Doctor had the truth about the fatherly solicitude of our Lord so deeply imbued in his soul that in the most arduous combats he did not see his confidence diminished; quite the contrary, it grew in proportion to the trials he faced. And he counseled this very thing to souls, as is seen, for example, in the letter to Doña Juana de Pedraza: “All of it is knockings63 and blows upon the soul so as to love more, which bring about more prayer and spiritual sighs to God, so that He may fulfill what the soul asks of Him. I already told you that there was no reason to be disquieted over those little things, but to do what you have been commanded, and, when they prevent you, obedience, and let me know, for God will provide what is best. Those who love God, He Himself takes care of their affairs, without their being anxious about them”64.

And in another letter he counsels a Carmelite nun something that all of us can practice: “When some unpleasantness and displeasure comes to you, remember Christ crucified, and be silent. Live in faith and hope, even if it be in darkness, for in that darkness God protects the soul. Cast your care upon God, for He cares for you; nor will He forget you. Do not think that He leaves you alone, for that would be to wrong Him”65.

Moreover, let us bear in mind that the truth about divine providence is also closely united to the truth of the kingdom of God, and for this reason the words spoken by the Incarnate Word in His teaching on providence have a fundamental importance: Seek first the kingdom of God and His justice… and all these things will be given you besides66. The truth concerning divine providence, that is, the transcendent government of God over the created world, becomes comprehensible in the light of the truth about the kingdom of God, about that kingdom which God planned from all eternity to bring about in the created world thanks to predestination in Christ, who was begotten before every creature67. That is why it is common doctrine among the saints to exhort the faithful to live poverty of spirit as the fundamental disposition for preparing oneself to receive the divine gift. Indeed, this is how our life “becomes an unceasing worship of divine providence”68.

The Mystic of Fontiveros recommends this very thing, for example: “preserve the spirit of poverty and contempt of everything… wishing to be content with God alone. And know that you will not have or feel more needs than those to which you wish to subject your heart; for the poor in spirit is more constant and joyful in want, because he has placed his all in nothing at all, and so finds in everything breadth of heart”69.

For his part, St. John of Ávila, Doctor of the Church, in the same line as St. John of the Cross, also invites us to see the adverse events that God permits to happen to us as moments of special purification for the good of our soul. Thus the Doctor of Ávila writes with a wise pen to a married woman whose son had suffered a misfortune: “Our Lord… takes care to send us some tribulations so that we may see our weakness, and be undeceived if we thought ourselves strong, and see the fortitude God gives us to suffer them joyfully, and know how powerful His hand is, which places virtue in such weak vessels, and how good He is, since He makes us gain from evils”70. And then he shows her how God, through these sufferings, “cleanses [us of] our faults and fashions crowns for us in heaven; and the thanks given to God in these tribulations are a Christian and sweet music in His ears. I say Christian, because to give Him thanks in consolations belongs to everyone, but in tribulations only to good Christians”71.

Therefore, the Saint of Ávila goes on to say, those events that are adverse to us, painful, and not seldom very sorrowful, must not disturb us, nor must we consider them punishment, “because Christian truth confesses that nothing comes by chance, but all things under the providence of God; and as something from His hand let your grace take what has happened. And although you take it from His hand, look at His heart, and you will find that He sent this with great love, although in the hand it may seem severity. God truly loves us, although at times He conceals His love and pretends to go far away, not because He forgets us, for He has sworn, saying: If I forget you, may my right hand wither, and may my tongue cleave to my palate if I do not remember you72. Certainly, He who has us written on His hands73, and at great cost to Himself, fulfills it just as He says it; but He withdraws so that we may sigh for Him and hunger may disquiet us, so that afterwards the bread that sustains heaven and earth may taste better to us”74.

So, even though for the moment it may seem that everything is coming to an end and everything is very toilsome for us, St. John of Ávila says: “in truth, whoever sees a little will find that there is no other joy or rest than that the will of God be fulfilled in us; and true consolation is to rejoice in the will of God, even if it leaves us disconsolate. And if it seems to us that these desolations come from our lukewarmness (which is what usually distresses many), I say, after having looked at it, that it is better to bear one’s fault with calm evenness of heart and good confidence in the divine mercy than, in order to kill the fly (as they say) that is stinging my forehead, to give myself a blow that kills me. Not all who are to go to heaven have to be equal; nor must we despair because we are not among the best or even the middling; but give thanks to our Lord because He gave us hope of salvation through His clemency. And it is fitting to gladden the heart in this and to thank God for it, so that He may not take from us, as from ingrates, what He has given us, and so we fall into hell because God did not make us among the best in heaven. Believe me that this matter of the peace of heart that the perfect have is not given because of discontent or blows of the fist, but God gives it to whom, and how, and when it pleases Him”75.

St. John Paul II taught something similar, inviting souls not to be disquieted if for the moment it seems to them that they cannot bear with a resigned spirit, or find consolation in, the events that are adverse to them, or if they even experience interior and at times also exterior complaints about the ‘injustices,’ mistreatment, etc. that they suffer. For “gradually,” the Holy Father stated, “and with the help of faith nourished by prayer, one discovers the true meaning of the suffering that each one experiences in his own life. It is a discovery that depends on the word of divine revelation and on the ‘word of the Cross’76 of Christ, which is the power and the wisdom of God77”78.

The Master of Ávila continues, giving us the following counsel: “Let us not fail to do what we can and to have good confidence in God, in whom we must place ourselves so wholeheartedly that we do not dare to pass sentence even on ourselves as to how we are faring79; but, trusting in Him, let us run with joy the course of His commandments and of His footsteps, and hope that He will reward our good deeds and pardon our evil ones, so that for the one and the other we may praise and bless Him forever and ever”80. And St. John of the Cross could well add at this point what he wrote in a letter to one of his directees: “But all is brief, for all lasts only until the knife is raised, and then Isaac remains alive, with the promise of a multiplied offspring81”82.

For this reason the need becomes evident to “ask for the grace of the science of the Cross and of the joy of the Cross, which are attained only in the school of Jesus Christ”83, as our Proper Law expressly points out to us. For “if in the present economy of salvation the Passion of Christ was necessary, our suffering will also be necessary. If there were another way to go to heaven, Jesus Christ would have followed it and, what is more, would have taught it. But it is not so; Christ went by the royal road of the Holy Cross and taught us to go by it”84.

This very thing, although in other words, is what the Master of Ávila tried to make a bishop of Córdoba (Spain) understand when he went to preside over a provincial council held in Toledo (1565), and which it is well for the health of our soul to keep in mind as we begin this year: “Let not your lordship think to persuade anyone to reform if he himself does not go reformed. Nor let him think that his embassy is to be fruitful by other means than those which Jesus Christ, by the ordinance of His Father, took to fulfill His own. For if there were other more fitting ones, neither would divine wisdom have been ignorant of them nor would His providence have failed to ordain them instead; for since, with such deliberation, and though they were so costly to His own Son, He ordained the ones we know, it is great temerity for the servant and attendant to wish to flee the means the Son took and to esteem his own carnal wisdom more than that of God. Let your lordship raise his eyes to the Son of God placed on a Cross, naked and crucified, and strive to strip himself of the world and of the flesh, and of blood, greed and honor, and of himself, so that he may thus be wholly like Jesus Christ and his embassy may be effective and fruitful. Let him die to everything and he will live to God, and will be the cause of others living; because, if he does not do this, he will lose himself and the others, for the word of Christ our Lord cannot fail: Nisi granum frumenti85, etc.”86.

Lastly, St. John of Ávila recommends the reading of Sacred Scripture, since from it “one will draw great joy, founded on the mercy of God; one will draw contentment and patience in the labors and sorrows that may come, considering that, if not a leaf on the tree stirs without the will of God, neither will those labors have come without His providence; and so, considering that the one who sends them is his God, who died for him, he will understand that it must be something that is fitting for his salvation. He will also have long-suffering in the petitions he makes to God when He does not grant them in the way he asks, understanding that He who gave him His own life will give him what is less, if it is fitting for him. And so he will see that He, as supremely wise, seeing things to come, understands that this is not fitting for him or that, if it is fitting, it is not for that moment, for which reason His mercy delays it”87.

*****

That is why the time of trial, whatever it may be, is the time to prepare oneself to experience true joy, as St. Francis of Assisi calls the supernatural acceptance of the merciful designs of divine providence over us. You will remember that chapter of The Little Flowers when the saint answers Brother Leo: “When we arrive at St. Mary of the Angels, soaked by the rain and frozen by the cold and covered with mud and afflicted with hunger, and we knock at the door of the place, and the porter comes angrily and says to us: ‘Who are you?’ And when we say: ‘We are two of your brothers.’ And he answers: ‘You lie; you are two rascals who go about the world deceiving people and stealing the alms of the poor; get out of here’; and he does not open to us and makes us stay outside, in the midst of the snow, the rain and the cold, and hungry, until nightfall; then, if we bear so much injury, so much cruelty and so many insults patiently, without being disturbed and without murmuring against him, thinking humbly and charitably that that porter truly knows us and that God makes him speak against us, oh, Brother Leo!, in this will be true joy. And if we persevere in knocking at the door and he comes out disturbed and, as if we were troublesome scoundrels, drives us away with abuse and with blows, saying: ‘Off with you, most vile little thieves; go to the hospital, for here you will neither eat nor lodge,’ and we bear it patiently and with joy and with love, Brother Leo, write that in this there will be perfect joy. And if, driven by hunger, by the cold and by the night, we knock again and call and beg for the love of God, with great weeping, that he open to us and let us in, and he, scandalized, says: ‘These are troublesome rascals; I will give them the pay they deserve,’ and he comes out with a knotted stick and, seizing us by the hood, throws us to the ground on the snow and beats us hard; if then we suffer all these things with joy, thinking of the sufferings of the blessed Christ, which we must suffer for His love, oh, Brother Leo!, write, here perfect joy will be found; but attend to the conclusion, Brother Leo: above all the graces and gifts of the Holy Spirit that Christ grants to His friends is that of conquering oneself and willingly bearing sufferings, injuries, reproaches and contempt for His love; for we cannot glory in the other gifts, because they are not ours but God’s; whence the Apostle says: What do you have that you have not received from God? And if you have received it, why do you glory as if it were your own? But in the Cross of tribulation and affliction we can glory, because this is ours, and for this the Apostle says: I do not wish to glory except in the Cross of Our Lord Jesus Christ”88.

Now at the dawn of this new year we entrust ourselves to Most Holy Mary, Mother of Divine Providence, so that she who, like no one else, abandoned herself to God, may grant us to face this 2022, with all its graces and with all its trials, entirely abandoned to the will of good pleasure of our good God89.

With gratitude, let us advance confidently along the level path of the Divine Will, striving to fulfill completely what He asks of us, according to the circumstances He disposes, and at the time He requires it, which will always be what is best.

[Exordium] Dear brothers and sisters, we have heard in the Gospel we have just read three of the parables known as the parables of the kingdom, which Jesus spoke in His preaching through the Decapolis, that is, through the cities near the Sea of Galilee. In these parables is narrated, in a very simple but very beautiful way, the truth of our Christian life: how it is born, how it must grow and, still more, how it must culminate. And if we go deep, these parables are ultimately teaching the truth of human life, its beginning, its end, where we come from, where we are going.

1. In the first place we have heard the parable of the weeds. The kingdom of heaven, then, is like

a sower who sowed good seed in the field. This parable in the Gospel of Matthew is related immediately after the parable of the Sower, which we read last Sunday. Jesus will better clarify the meaning of this parable, saying: it is the Son of man who sows the good seed; the field is the world; the good seed are the children of the kingdom (those who are faithful, those who will make the good seed bear fruit). The weeds are the children of the evil one, and it is the devil who sows them. Both must grow together, and the good seed must persevere so as not to let itself be choked by the weeds, because one day—and we all know that it will be so (it is dogma of faith)—the time of harvest will come; the harvest is the end of the world, says the Gospel. The reapers will be the angels, and just as the weeds are gathered and thrown into the fire, so will it be at the end of the world. As graphic as it is dramatic: just as the chaff, the bad, dry weed that has borne no fruit, is thrown into the fire, so will it happen to the wicked on the day of the last judgment. The Christian life is no joke, nor is human life on earth; there is an end and an eternal destiny: either eternal happiness for those who were faithful, or a sad, horrifying and eternal end for those who are damned; His angels will gather out of His kingdom all causes of scandal and all evildoers, and throw them into the furnace of fire, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth. For the faithful, on the other hand, quite the opposite: the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father. An eternal end: eternal happiness or eternal damnation. That is the drama the Gospel sets forth; that is the drama of human life, of which we are invited today to become aware, and therefore to act accordingly. Not for nothing did St. Jerome say in his life, considering these realities: “territus terreo,” “terrified, I terrify.”

2. In the second place, more briefly but no less eloquently, we have the parable of the mustard seed, that tiny seed that is cultivated in Jerusalem, especially in the area of Bethany, which can barely be seen because it is a tiny black speck; yet it is destined to grow, and if it grows and overcomes the difficulties that all growth has, its dignity is very great: and though it is the smallest of all seeds, when it has grown it is the largest of all garden plants and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and nest in its branches. The kingdom of heaven—to which we all aspire—is a seed that is destined to grow.

3. In the third place, very similar to the previous one, we have the parable of the dough and the leaven. Once again, it is a little leaven that ferments everything, that grows, that bears fruit. Very simply, but in a very clear and forceful way, the drama of man is set forth. God has given to man, He has given grace (the Gospel says this in many ways: He has given some talents, He has given us the vineyard to care for, He has leased us His field, etc.), and that treasure which is grace has been given to us so that it may grow, so that it may bear fruit, and it is we who must make it grow. In the end, if we are aware, that is the only work in our life; that is what God created us for: that you may go and bear fruit, and that your fruit may be abundant and lasting. We could summarize the teachings of the Gospel in three great truths, of which the Church invites us to become aware this Sunday:

a. The seed of grace

The Christian life is like a seed. It is a seed that God our good father placed in our souls on the day of our baptism. A very great treasure. We do not have time here to speak of all that grace means in a soul, of the value a soul in grace has (whose value is greater than that of the whole world). The gift of baptism is a very great one (that is why the Church insists so much that children be baptized when they are little, and the sooner the better, because something very great happens in each baptism; if parents truly want the best for their children, it is incomprehensible that they should deprive them of becoming Christians as soon as possible). Grace is a very great gift of God that makes us children of God and heirs of heaven (if things are going well, speak a little more about grace). Well, this so great treasure is given to us on the day of baptism as a seed, which has to grow, which has to struggle. When we baptize children (and also adults) we always perform that rite which is the clothing with the white garment: “may this garment be a sign of your dignity, and with the help of the example of your family may you keep it pure until the day of your burial,” and when the paschal candle is handed over, the children being very little, the parents and godparents are given the mission of helping that light to grow: “to you, parents and godparents, is entrusted the mission of increasing this light.” The Christian life is a seed that has to grow, that must always grow. Spiritual authors rightly say that “in the Christian life, whoever does not advance goes backward.” The first truth of which we must become aware, then, is that we have a mission in our life, and it is to make the life of grace in the soul grow and bear fruit. The ancients used to say it: “I have a God to serve, I have a soul to save, I have a mission to fulfill in this life.”

b. The struggle

The second truth of which we must become aware—and it is another great truth revealed in the Gospel—is that we must struggle. In the Lord’s vineyard, where our seed must bear fruit, there will also be struggle. The good God sows the seed, but He also permits another truth: the enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat and went away. And then the weeds grew and there had to be a struggle. The Gospel is very clear: where do the weeds come from? And He answered him: “an enemy has done this. There are, then, enemies; we must know it; we must not deceive ourselves. And all the more care, since the weeds are the children of the evil one and the one who sows them is the devil. The Christian life is no joke. Whoever wants to be a good Christian, whoever wants to fulfill God’s design and make the seed bear fruit, will necessarily have to struggle. My son—says the book of Proverbs—if you decide to devote yourself to the service of God, prepare your soul for temptation. St. Paul often speaks of a struggle, and he will say forcefully: our struggle is not against flesh and blood but against the spirits of the air. Certainly whoever wants to be victorious in life will have to conquer those three great and powerful enemies of human life, which are: the devil, with all his pomps and deceits, Father of lies, and his fallacies that envelop us; the world, with its illusions and its passing pleasures of the moment, like—says Scripture—a feather carried off by the wind; and the flesh, with its passions that want to impose themselves over the rule of reason. All the saints and good Christians have had to fight, as St. Paul said, the good fight. Militia est vita hominis super terra, says the book of Job, the life of man on earth is a warfare. But before such strong and powerful enemies we have divine help and the divine promises that if we are faithful we will conquer, and if not we will perish. Christ said it to St. Paul when he complained of the din of the Christian struggle; Our Lord answered him: my grace is sufficient for you.

Let us ask God for this grace, so that we may say as St. Francis Xavier said when he was dying on the shores of India: “I have confessed You to the end, with firmness and without shame; never, Lord, did I put the light under the bushel; anguish and sufferings hemmed me in with rigor, but in my discouragement I conquered, Lord, with determination; You gave me five talents and I return to You five more.”

c. The glory

The third and last great truth of the parables of the kingdom that we highlight today and that we must never forget. It is that truth which increases our hope and whose promise makes us always vigorous and constant in the fight, and it is heaven. The unfaithful, those who despised the mercy of God and are damned, will go to hell forever (if it goes well, speak about the eternity of hell); the righteous, on the other hand, will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father; they will go to heaven, to that great heaven that Jesus has promised us and that will crown the victory of His faithful ones. Where—in the words of St. Paul—eye has not seen, nor ear heard, what God has prepared for those who love Him, because—as St. Peter said—there awaits us an eternal weight of incalculable glory. St. Alphonsus rightly said: “all suffering is little, all pain is nothing, so long as we gain paradise.” Life without end, life forever, eternal joy.

I count as nothing the sufferings of the present life, so long as I gain paradise. Always—every day of our life—we must ask God for this grace, what the fathers of the Church called the gratia gratiarum, the grace of graces, which is the grace of dying in the grace of God. Of being faithful until the last moment of our life. Always very beautiful is the example of St. Mary of Egypt and of the bishop St. Zosimus when he came to give her her last communion… how had she been able to bear so many sufferings?… With the hope of heaven.

[Peroratio] Dear brothers and sisters, we could say much more; may these considerations serve to consider in depth the value of human life, the value of the Christian life. On the day of our

baptism, God placed an immense treasure in the soul which, like a seed, like the mustard seed or the leaven in the dough, is destined to grow and to produce fruit, to become a leafy and fruitful tree

where the birds of the air may make their nests. May the Most Blessed Virgin, Mother of Victories, grant us the grace of fidelity, of knowing how to struggle and to conquer. So be it.

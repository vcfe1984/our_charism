Feast of the Presentation of the Lord

[Exordium] St. John Paul II used to say that although it is true that God has called each one of us individually, He has not called us to be alone, but to be part of a family. Today, on the feast of the Presentation of the Lord, we once again have the joy of celebrating the enormous and unmerited grace of being religious in a family which is our religious family of the Incarnate Word. This is where God has called us to work at stripping ourselves of all that is not God, in order to love Him and belong exclusively to Him1, according to a beautiful expression of St. John of the Cross in the Ascent of Mount Carmel quoted in our Constitutions.

On this day and on this feast, I would like to propose for consideration some points of the great Mystical Doctor of Fontiveros on religious life, remembering that our Proper Law proposes to us the doctrine of St. John of the Cross as outstanding when it comes to forming “virtuous men”2.

1. St. John of the Cross, religious

Of the total of 49 years that Friar John of the Cross lived (1542-1591), he was a religious for 28 years (at the age of many of us he had already completed his work and mission on this earth). Of those 28 years, he was a Discalced Carmelite for 233, holding positions of authority in his Order for most of those years. While in Segovia, as Definitor General, he said something very interesting which perhaps many of us can repeat without the saint’s exaggeration; he said: “when I remember the foolish things I have done as a prelate [superior], my face turns red”4.

Since he was there at the beginnings of the Carmelite Reform, it fell to him to build convents, to suffer the poverty of the beginnings, to revise the Constitutions, to carry out several foundations, to be superior and at the same time exercise many apostolates and study, to be present at the first Chapters –ordinary and extraordinary– of the Discalced, and to suffer in his own flesh the difficulties of his Order. With all the store of graces that this means.

The Incarnate Word chose him as one of the “first stones,” and a very special first stone, since on “this stone” Our Lord planned to build the edifice of the Carmelite reform. That is why we must say that John of the Cross was not only a privileged recipient of the Teresian charism, but a great transmitter of it. What is more, he was an unequaled and enriching exponent of that same spirit which he received, going far beyond the letter of the rule, to the point that he was regarded by his subjects as an “example of all the virtues professed in the religious state”5. (What praise!)

To look no further, another of the religious, speaking of his “rare and great” example, said that “the holy father lived with great perfection of life, and with the same great perfection life was lived in the convents that he had and had had in his charge”6. His secret lay in this: “the prelate ought to teach by deeds rather than by words”7. Because, as he himself wrote in the Flame: “As the master is, so will the disciple be, and as the father, so the son”8. So it was, then, that Friar John of the Cross resolved as very few have to put into practice that same counsel he gave to a religious: have “constancy in carrying out the things of your Religious Order and of obedience, without any human respect, but solely for God”9.

2. Some points on the understanding of religious life in St. John of the Cross

How did St. John of the Cross understand religious consecration? I would like to point out some points from a study by Father Vicente Rodríguez, perhaps the greatest expert on the life and work of the Saint today, entitled “San Juan de la Cruz y su estilo de hacer comunidad” (St. John of the Cross and his style of building community). There are six, and they are the following.

  1. The essential thing that the saint always kept in mind can be found in a letter to one of the nuns of Beas, to whom he insistently says: “you are not your own, but God’s”10. That is, in the first place and succinctly, his idea: to be a religious is to belong completely to God. That is why further on in that same letter he makes it explicit: “God wants the religious to be a religious in such a way that he has finished with everything and that everything has finished for him; because He himself is the one who wants to be his riches, consolation and delightful glory”11.
  1. A characteristic needed to achieve this essential aspect is constant determination. In his writings one often sees an insistence on this, as also happens in St. Teresa, and it is good to keep it in mind. We have come to religious life urged on –says the saint– “by holy desires and motives to leave the world and change our life or way, and serve God”12, “to walk imitating the model who is Christ”13. And for this there is required a “perfect boldness and determination in works [which] few spiritual persons attain; because, although some engage in and practice this way of life, and some even consider themselves among those far along [that is, very advanced], they never quite lose themselves in certain points, either of the world or of nature, so as to do perfect and naked works for Christ, not looking at what people will say or how it will appear. […] They are still ashamed to confess Christ by their deeds before men; [and] having regard for things, they do not truly live in Christ”14.
  1. A particular characteristic of living his belonging to God is given by what some scholars of St. John of the Cross consider “the four means” for union with God. In the Counsels he gives to one who “would be a true religious and fulfill the state he has promised to God”15, St. John of the Cross recommends: resignation, mortification, the exercise of virtues, bodily and spiritual solitude; as means “so that the heart may not be detained by any human thing”16. It is through the practice of these “four means” or “ways,” St. John of the Cross teaches, that the perfection of the religious state is attained, which is identified with true freedom, “the happy freedom desired by all”17, as he writes in his Dark Night.
  1. Love for his Institute and its rule. The same man who wrote to a religious: “In no way seek to know anything except only how you will better serve God and better keep the things of your institute”18, was the same who every Friday read the rule or had it read, fulfilling what his Constitutions commanded but going amply beyond it with the abundance and loftiness of the instructions he gave his religious. And so, what John of the Cross teaches in his books about the way of living and perfecting oneself in religious life, he himself embodied and encouraged from his post and activity as superior.
  1. Two great virtues stand out in the religious life of St. John of the Cross as the foundation on which he built charity, and these are: obedience and humility.

– Devotion to obedience, which he recommends mainly in relation to the office entrusted, by way of examen. He taught that it is in the office given that the will of God is best manifested. With great vigor he instilled in his religious: “Do not fail to do the office you have, and any other that obedience may command you, with all the solicitude possible and that may be necessary, but do it in such a way that no fault clings to you in it, because neither God nor obedience wants this”19.

– Humility, he says, must be the present virtue, and on the other hand he also says it is the manly virtue. When his star began to decline at that Chapter of Valladolid in which he found himself caught up in cruel calumnies and was left without office and went off to the desert of La Peñuela, he wrote: “To go about losing and letting everyone get the better of us belongs to valiant spirits, to generous hearts”20, because “temporal lordship and freedom, before God, are neither kingdom nor freedom”21.

  1. Finally, one cannot live religious life well without possessing an authentic freedom of spirit. And so, although he demanded great fidelity to the rule, at the same time he never tied himself to the letter. There are many examples of this.

His biographers recount that St. John of the Cross was more or less the soul of everything in his community, and it ran to the tune and rhythm of its superior. Nevertheless, even though he was very observant, the freedom of spirit of this man before what was prescribed is striking. Fr. Vicente gives an example concerning prayer in common. Gracián’s Constitutions said “prayer shall be in the choir with all together.” Some later Constitutions, approved even by John of the Cross himself, prescribe even the prayers that were to be said. Coming from St. John of the Cross, one would think that he would have observed them to the letter with exemplary scrupulousness. However, witnesses say that “he was seen solicitously seeking out secret places suitable for contemplation”22, and so for the prayer at the first hour of the night and for the morning prayer he would go outside the chapel, and sometimes he had his religious do the same in order to pray. In this way Friar John safeguards the prayer in common, but interprets broadly the matter of the place where it was to be made. Why? Because he warned that one must not be like those many who, “blinding themselves with the lowliness of the letter and not understanding its spirit and truth, took the life of their God and Lord”23.

There are many examples like this in the life of the saint: for instance, he changed the prayer schedules so that he and his friars could hear more confessions; he got a reprimand from the provincial because, it was said, he did not visit lay people and it would be good for him to do so in order to obtain alms; he did it once and never went back, because he said he was wasting time and it was better for him to pray that God would provide what was necessary (and in fact it always was so); another time he was criticized because his friars ate meat; he did not care, because his subjects said they needed it and therefore it was just that he should believe them and give it to them; and so many other examples…

[Peroratio] For our part, we must be persuaded of what our Directory of Spirituality says: that our consecration as religious “drives us to irresistible demands of sacrifice for love of Christ, which is at the same time an act of integral faith and total self-giving, with the formal decision not to make pacts, not to compromise, not to capitulate, not to negotiate, not to concede, nor to make deals with the spirit of the world”24.

That is why, in spite of our miseries and sins and the irrefutable fact that the years go by and it seems that we fall ever shorter, we can ask Our Lord today to grant us the precious grace of being good religious, without being discouraged by our faults, miseries and inconstancies, convinced that God will grant it to us. Perhaps we can do so with that beautiful prayer composed by John of the Cross himself, which goes like this:

Lord God, my beloved! If you still remember my sins so as not to do what I keep asking of you,
do your will in them, my God, for that is what I most desire,
and exercise your goodness and mercy and you will be known in them.

And if you are waiting for my works in order to grant my request by that means,
give them to me and work them in me, and the sufferings that you would accept, and let it be done.

And if you are not waiting for my works, what are you waiting for, my most clement Lord? Why do you delay?
For if, in the end, it is to be grace and mercy that I ask of you in your Son,
take my little coin, since you want it, and give me this good, since you also want it.

[Who can free himself from lowly ways and limits
if you do not lift him up to yourself in purity of love, my God?

How will man, begotten and raised in lowliness, rise up to you,
if you do not lift him up, Lord, with the hand that made him?]

You will not take from me, my God, what you once gave me in your only Son Jesus Christ,
for in Him you gave me all that I desire.
Therefore I will rejoice that you will not delay if I wait.

Pope John XXII used to say: “give me a religious who has been faithful all his life to his rule and constitutions and I will canonize him without further examination.” Hence we must constantly examine ourselves on this, since it is essential to our religious life. There is no clearer sign that one is progressing in the spiritual life than affective and effective regular observance, of both the letter and the spirit of what is written. We may have the greatest successes (apostolic successes, great constellations, enjoying a certain peace, etc.), but if there is no faithful fulfillment of the rule, it is of no use. That is why we must always have great confidence in our religious life and also great fidelity in fulfilling it.

Confidence in the rule

Certainly it is God who gives graces to religious, but it is from God through the rule that the religious must hope for everything: holiness and perseverance until death. This is the precious way we have of attaining holiness.

– Certainty that we are on the right path: And this is a great grace: to sanctify ourselves, it is enough for us to keep our rule carefully and lovingly. It is a path so straight and sure that going astray is impossible. For holiness consists in nothing other than the full conformity of our will with the divine will; and the religious who is faithful to his rule does nothing other than fulfill at every moment the will of God expressed and contained in it. His whole life is transformed into a continual exercise of obedience and love for the will of God.

– Certainty of perseverance: it is the best guarantee that we will persevere and remain in religious life. If we are faithful to the teachings of the founder, we can be sure that we will attain the threefold perseverance:

  • perseverance in grace: because incorruptible fidelity to one’s rule and constitutions presupposes in the faithful religious an exquisite delicacy of conscience and a profound spirit of prayer. One could say that it is impossible to sin if one does not depart from the fulfillment of one’s rule.
  • perseverance in the vocation: because this is the best way to become rooted in the vocation. One who tries to be faithful even in the smallest things of the rules will hardly feel tempted to betray the vows he has professed. There is no firmer guarantee of perseverance in the vocation than the exact and meticulous practice of one’s own rule and constitutions.
  • final perseverance, since the rule is for the religious “the key to paradise,” and given that one dies as one lives, the practice of most faithful fulfillment of the rule is a sure guarantee that the religious will die faithful to the vows he has made. “Everyone who dies kissing the habit of his order dies with the clearest and most unmistakable sign of the predestined.”

The risks of non-observance

While it is true that we must hope for everything from the faithful fulfillment of the rule, at the same time we must fear everything from its non-observance, which, if habitual and prolonged, can become a stumbling block for others and an occasion of ruin for ourselves. These are the main risks that are usually pointed out:

– Mediocrity of life: generally, God treats us as we treat Him. “God gives Himself wholly only to the one who gives himself wholly to Him,” said St. Teresa. And objectively, stepping outside the rule, acting by one’s own spirit (even in small things), holding a private judgment about its application, is objectively to place oneself outside the providential order and to withdraw from the influence of the Holy Spirit. God called us to holiness in a religious family and under its rule. If one lets oneself go, the consequences will be inevitable: languor of soul, the weakening of the virtues and an exhausted spiritual life. less grace, less sap, and therefore fewer flowers and less fruit.

“Without water and without sun, the soul becomes dry and parched earth, with little and stunted vegetation. This is the explanation of many religious lives, neither good nor bad, but simply insubstantial. It is the triumph of moral insipidity. No bursts of enthusiasm, not a single impulse to rise toward the ideal. A continuous and endless march along the track of mediocrity, a perennial spiritual drowsiness and somnolence, as if sleeping sickness had taken hold of the soul. What the moth is to clothing and the slug to flowers, that is what repeated faults of observance are to fervor.”

– Lukewarmness: the frequent, unjustified violation of the rule or constitutions, without a purpose of amendment, brings with it habitual venial sin, that is, lukewarmness. The one who practices regular observance only when it suits his taste, who with full deliberation and frequently disregards silence, dispenses himself from some exercises or community acts, judges and criticizes his superiors, sows discord with malicious or imprudent gossip, neglects the prescribed delicacies regarding poverty and modesty, is without any doubt a religious who has fallen into lukewarmness. A terrible sin, abominable in the eyes of God: “I know your works, that you are neither cold nor hot. Would that you were cold or hot! But because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I am about to vomit you out of my mouth” (Rev 3:15-16). From here to falling into mortal sin there is only one step, and some take it.

– Mortal sin: if it is an enormous misfortune for a Christian, mortal sin is a true catastrophe for a religious. Normally religious do not fall into it; however, just as a good religious who suffers such a misfortune rises quickly, the same does not happen to those who little by little let themselves fall irremediably into lukewarmness. “In the moral order, as in the physical, sudden deaths are an exception. The normal thing is to end one’s life through illness or worn out by old age. The soul almost always dies a victim of lukewarmness.”

  • Anemia. The habitual violation of the rule engenders lukewarmness, which is to the soul what tuberculosis is to the body: it weakens the organism, drains its strength and turns life into a slow spiritual agony.
  • Contempt of the law. St. Thomas teaches it: “the frequency of sin disposes to contempt of the law,” and formal contempt of the law constitutes grave sin.
  • Illusion of conscience. Lukewarmness darkens judgment and falsifies the conscience. From slight infractions one passes little by little to more important faults, and even grave ones, ending up without realizing it in enormous infidelities. “With the dividing line between mortal and venial sin hesitant and blurred, one rides along this slack rope cheerfully and dangerously.” A doubtful rule is formed in the intellect that works at the whim of the transgressor. Delicacies of conscience appear as “scruples” and “narrow-mindedness.” And so little by little, almost without realizing it, one begins to come crashing down.
  • Scandal of others. Few things spread so easily in a religious community as the bad example of one of its members. And so the fact of one being falsely lax leads the others and the whole community to break basic points of observance.
  • Divine punishment. It will be enough to neglect just one of those precautions to find oneself in a compromising situation or for a most grave temptation to arise which, by God’s just punishment, one does not manage to resist l How many deep and scandalous falls could have been avoided with a little more care and respect for the most holy prescriptions of the rule! Deus non deserit nisi prius deseratur says the Council of Trent: “God abandons no one unless we first abandon Him.”

– Loss of vocation: An exceptional grace—the greatest of all after redemption and baptism,

according to St. Alphonsus—the religious vocation can be lost through one’s own fault. It is true that not all religious who leave religious life are guilty of infidelity to their vocation. There may be entirely blameless causes that authorize and advise a return to secular life. But the usual thing is the reverse: generally the return to the world implies a true disloyalty toward God. The phrase of St. Paul in the letter to the Romans will always be true: “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.”

– Eternal damnation: the religious who have fallen into hell have been damned, without any exception, through

the fault of their non-observance. For if fidelity to the rule down to its smallest details is one of the clearest signs of predestination and an infallible pledge of salvation, the spirit of irregularity constitutes, on the contrary, one of the most fearsome signs of eternal damnation. It must be repeated many times: consecrated souls do not hurl themselves headlong into the abyss in one leap; they slip and fall into it gently and imperceptibly …

Conclusion

Let us always strive to have great fidelity to our constitutions, to their letter and to their spirit, which we, by a particular grace, have had the grace of drinking in from the founder himself, whom we have known, with whom we have lived. I think greater ease is impossible. And even in the smallest things, which is no mere detail. Don Orione spoke to his religious of the utmost care they had to take in preserving the spirit, even in the smallest details. St. Augustine said: “What is small is small, but to be faithful in small things is a very great thing.” Like St. Thérèse of the Child Jesus, of whom it was said that she had “a stubborn and tremendous obstinacy in fulfilling her duty in the smallest things out of love for the Beloved.” To this exquisite fidelity in small things it is owed that she is, in the words of St. Pius X, “the greatest saint of modern times.”

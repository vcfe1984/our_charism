[Exordium] Chesterton used to say: “Real soldiers fight not because they hate what they have in front of them but because they love what lies behind them.”

This was the case of many missionaries who one day left everything to embark on the marvelous enterprise of sowing the faith in distant lands. Many of them −as good soldiers of Christ− even gave their lives for what they rightly believed to be the greatest cause, the “most divine work among the divine”1, which is the salvation of souls. They took with them only the faith they had learned and, branded as with fire, the identity of religious of the congregation that had begotten them as such

This was the case of the North American Martyrs: Jean de Brébeuf, Isaac Jogues, Gabriel Lalemant, Antoine Daniel, Charles Garnier, Noël Chabanel (six priests) and René Goupil and Jean de la Lande (brothers). All Jesuits, all with very strong personalities, very different from one another, with different preparation, some with more and others with fewer years of religious life, but all united by the same ideal: the greater glory of God, as good Jesuits.

Incredible trials

These men were not content to live side by side, like someone who shares a mission as if sharing an office −in fact they changed companions quite often−; rather, they strengthened one another as they made their way in the mission, and they fought together as good soldiers for the noble cause for which they had been chosen according to the providentially merciful designs of our Lord.

To read their writings, their chronicles, their letters is to read a hymn of hope in the midst of incredible trials. If one thinks about it carefully, all the martyrs −and I believe these especially− proclaim to us the message of “not giving up,” of “not throwing in the towel,” even in the worst situations. This suits us very well, because the habitual setting in which the life of a member of the Institute must unfold includes precisely “the most difficult situations and the most adverse conditions”2.

Sometimes we think that the martyrs were only witnesses of our faith, and indeed they were! But they also remind us that God draws good out of evil. The martyrs did not think that tortures or death itself were the most disastrous thing that could happen to them. On the contrary! They realized the unparalleled opportunity they had in each of those trials to give glory to God, to serve their Institute. That is why it is important to ponder their lives so as to put ours in true perspective.

To make St. Jean de Brébeuf stop preaching, his torturers thrust a red-hot iron rod through his mouth down to his throat, cut off his nose, tore off his lips… With that same burning iron they gouged out his eyes and those of his companion (Gabriel Lalemant). In mockery, pretending a baptism, they poured boiling water over their heads, cut off their burnt skin in large pieces, and the Indians ate it in front of them. While they were still alive, they opened their chests, tore out their hearts and drank their blood. Brébeuf’s martyrdom lasted 3 hours. Lalemant’s, 15. Brébeuf was 52 years old, Lalemant 47. But greater was the love they had for what lay behind all that: God and the Society.

An eminent favor

St. Jean de Brébeuf has a magnificent letter that he wrote to his superior in France several years before his martyrdom, in which he tells him that perhaps they are close to martyrdom, but that he is not very sure that God is going to give him that grace; but that “be that as it may,” says this iron-willed Jesuit, “all our fathers await the resolution of this matter with great calm and a contented spirit. As for me, I can tell you in all sincerity that I have not yet had the slightest apprehension or fear of death for this cause (that of the salvation of souls). One thing we regret, and it is that these poor barbarians, through their own malice, close themselves to the Gospel and to the grace of God.” This is being said by a missionary who, after six years of apostolic work, had baptized only one adult. And he continues: “whatever conclusion they come to, and regardless of the treatment they give us, we will try, with the help of God, to bear it patiently for His service. It is truly an eminent favor that God does us in allowing us to bear something for His love. It is only now that we consider ourselves true members of the Society”3. Because “real soldiers fight not because they hate what they have in front of them but because they love what lies behind them.”

Again: the martyrs do not remind us that God can draw good out of evil. And although these Missionaries did not live to see it, their letters generated a quite singular enthusiasm in the Jesuit college in France, from which the missionaries for the foreign missions eventually came.

These missionaries were ordinary men, so to speak; they had their failings, they had their miseries, they had their temperaments; some never adapted to mission life, as is the case of the martyr Noël Chabanel, who felt great repugnance for the food and the customs of the Indians; everything bothered him; in fact, he never learned the language, and this giant made a vow to remain among those Indians until death. And he died from an ax blow to the head by a ‘Christian’ Indian who betrayed him. Some of these missionaries were already worn out from so much daily struggle, so much self-denial, so many spiritual trials, so much perplexity before an uncertain future4, so much misery of their own and of others, and yet what makes it possible for us today to call them martyrs was the heroism with which they took upon their shoulders the cause for which they had been sent. They endured everything they endured, not for their own glory, not to say: ‘ah… I am invincible,’ but for the glory of God and of the Society. Because “real soldiers fight not because they hate what they have in front of them but because they love what lies behind them.”

Another of the martyrs, St. Isaac Jogues, wrote to another priest asking for prayers before leaving for New France. And he tells him: “I will have to live among those barbarians almost without freedom to pray, without Mass, without sacraments. I will be held responsible for each and every incident between the Iroquois and the French… What does it matter? My hope is in God, who has no need of my achievements for the accomplishment of His designs. All we need to do is try to be faithful and not spoil His work with our weaknesses.”

That is the message.

The greatest cause

Throughout the whole pilgrimage through this valley of tears that the mission in New France meant, they encouraged one another, they gave each other courage, they forgot themselves in order to help the other. None of them expected to be spared from death. None expected heaven to open and an angel to come and rescue them from the midst of the drama they were living. But all of them hoped that God would be served by their lives and by their deaths.

Men: what is happening to us is what had to happen to us for being faithful to our ideal, to our identity, to our mission. And perhaps only now can we consider ourselves true members of the Institute. We will probably die of what any ordinary person dies of: a heart attack, cancer, a car accident… what does it matter? Most of us will know that we are dying when we are dying. But we too have the same opportunity as the martyrs to give our life to God day by day, with all its demands, with all its trials, with all the attacks they may wish to perpetrate against us… The important thing is that “if one must keep watch, one keeps watch; if one must suffer, one suffers; if one must be humbled, one is humbled; if one must beg for alms, one begs; if one must fall ill, one falls ill; if one must die, one dies; but one dies in battle, with honor and with glory, with Christ and in the name of Christ and for the glory of Christ”5 and the good of the Institute.

Our capacity to accept suffering out of love for the Institute, out of love for the good itself, for truth, for justice, is what makes each one of us a man like these martyrs we have spoken about. Because when my well-being, my reputation, my health, my little job, “my little kiosk” −as Fr. Buela would say− is more important than the greatest cause that unites us, than the priority for which we must all fight together, then my life is a lie… because we came here to give our life for our brothers, as the Incarnate Word taught us.

These men achieved what they achieved and persevered to the end because they remained united, because they fought it out together, because there was synergy between the gift of God and the commitment of each one6 to move things forward.

[Peroratio] We must succeed in taking on, with generosity and intelligence, the challenges that present themselves to us. Let us not be overcome by discouragement or weariness. Let us not, “because of the tree of difficulties, lose sight of the forest of things that are going well”7.

Many of us sometimes feel that we are not going to be able to do it, and we feel like David before Goliath, but we must surpass our expectations. Like these martyrs, we must choose to rise above the average.

If you allow me, I would now like to take advantage of this Eucharistic celebration to give thanks to God for each one of you, for the days past and for those to come!

May God and the Blessed Virgin comfort you and give you all the encouragement you need to continue fulfilling that commitment, always demanding and so frequently accompanied by the sign of the cross, by a painful loneliness, and which demands on our part a profound sense of responsibility, a generosity without weaknesses or straying, a constant forgetfulness of self.

May Our Lady of Luján, Mother of the Hope that does not disappoint, renew our vigor and assist us in our endeavor to do more and better for the cause of the Institute, which is none other than the cause of the Incarnate Word.
